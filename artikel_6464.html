Decades of research show just how much wild animals depend on insects for food, shelter and medicine

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

Moths feed algae in sloth fur, parrots live in termite mounds, and ants arm poison frogs. So, insect decline is costing wildlife more than just meals.

Brown-throated sloths carry moths in their fur, and the moths help feed them. The insects leave nitrogen behind, which feeds algae growing in the fur, and the sloths eat the algae.

Once a month, a sloth climbs down to the forest floor to leave its droppings, and the moths lay their eggs in them.

A new review of hundreds of studies finds partnerships like this all over the animal world. Animals with backbones, from bats to frogs, depend on insects for shelter, protection and cleanup, not only for meals.

When insects decline, the authors conclude, many of those animals are likely to suffer.

Eliza Grames, an assistant professor of biological sciences at Binghamton University, State University of New York, led the work.

She shared the lead with Rob Cooke of the U.K. Centre for Ecology & Hydrology (UKCEH) and Chris Elphick of the University of Connecticut. Members of the team spanned eight countries.

It’s a review, so they gathered decades of other scientists’ findings rather than collecting new field data.

“We often hear about their importance as pollinators, or decomposers, or food for insectivores like birds and bats, but we forget about all the other things they do for us and for other species,” Grames said.

Food is the best-documented link. The team pulled together diet records for 19,750 species of land and freshwater animals with backbones, about two in five of all such species.

Of those, 73 percent eat insects or other invertebrates, meaning animals without a backbone, such as spiders and worms, and 44 percent eat nothing else.

Those records are lopsided. Diets are on file for 95 percent of bird species, but for only 4 percent of ray-finned fish species.

Birds need insects most when they breed. Of North America’s land birds, 96 percent raise their chicks partly or entirely on insects. Purple martins alone eat an estimated 412 billion insects a year.

Other animals binge. A Couch’s spadefoot toad can eat more than half its body weight in termites at a single feeding, enough calories to last it a year. Diets built on ants and termites have evolved again and again.

Some animals use insects to get other food. Certain herons and egrets set insects on the water’s surface to lure fish. Kangaroo rats raid lines of harvester ants and steal the seeds the ants are carrying.

Termite mounds and ant nests tend to hold steady temperatures and are well ventilated, which makes them good shelters. Small mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians all use termite mounds when they breed.

Hooded parrots live inside them. White-throated round-eared bats dig out and maintain cavities in termite mounds, using a kind of scent camouflage to fend off attacks.

Frogs, salamanders and reptiles use ant nests to escape predators. Some also move in during dry seasons, because the humidity inside stays steady.

Some frogs, lizards and snakes lay their eggs in ant nests, and in some cases fungus-growing ants groom the eggs and cover them with material from their fungus gardens.

Other animals reuse what insects leave behind. Some bats move into abandoned holes made by longhorn beetle larvae.

Many birds use spider and insect silk in their nests, which strengthens them, keeps them warm and helps hide them. Bowerbirds decorate the structures they build to attract mates with butterfly wings and beetle parts.

Living near stinging insects can pay off. Many small birds build their nests close to wasps, ants or other stinging insects, and experiments suggest that choice helps more nests succeed.

Other animals take the insects’ chemical weapons for themselves. Pitohui birds carry toxins that come from the melyrid beetles they eat, and those toxins likely deter ticks and predators. Some snakes eat firefly larvae and store the toxins.

Amphibians probably do it more than any other group. Scientists have detected more than 800 defensive chemicals in amphibian skin, and most of them come from insects in the diet.

In some toxic poison frogs, ants make up 50 to 73 percent of what they eat, while ants are a much smaller part of the diet for their harmless relatives.

That means a frog’s chemical defenses depend partly on which insects are around to eat. When the available prey changes, the mix of toxins in its skin can change too.

Chimpanzees apply insects to open wounds, though scientists don’t know which chemicals are involved or what the treatment does.

More than 200 bird species use ants to groom themselves, a habit called anting. Proposed reasons include removing parasites, easing skin irritation and keeping feathers in good shape.

Insects also clean up. In hooded parrot nests, a moth lives alongside the chicks and eats their droppings. Moths in owl and kingfisher nests break down fur, crushed feathers and leftover pellets.

The arrangement is old: a beetle was feeding on shed feathers in dinosaur nests during the Cretaceous period.

Dung beetles help on a bigger scale. By drying out dung and letting air into it, they leave fewer parasites alive inside.

Where antiparasitic drugs, such as ivermectin, are given to livestock dung beetle communities have been devastated, and much less dung gets removed. What that means for disease in wild animals isn’t well studied.

The relationship isn’t all help. Mosquitoes spread avian malaria, which affects bird species worldwide, and mantids occasionally catch hummingbirds.

Researchers studying insect losses have mostly asked what happens to the food supply, and some of the numbers are stark.

Across Europe, insect-eating birds declined by 13 percent from 1990 to 2015, while birds with mixed diets held steady. Of the 3 billion birds North America has lost since 1970, 2.9 billion came from species that rely on insects for at least part of their lives.

That doesn’t prove the insects caused it. Bird, bat and insect declines don’t always line up on a map, and they may be separate responses to the same environmental pressures.

A pooled analysis of 125 studies did link more insect prey to birds raising more young, with chicks in better condition.

The risk is sharper for picky eaters like the Critically Endangered mountain pygmy possum, which times its breeding around the bogong moth. After the moth’s steep decline over the past decade, the possum’s shrinking food supply is now a primary threat.

The extinction of the Eskimo curlew has been blamed in part on the loss of the Rocky Mountain locust, a large part of its diet. “If you want to have vertebrates, then you’ve got to be thinking about insect conservation, and we don’t do that nearly enough,” Elphick said.

The team checked its diet records against the IUCN Red List, the global record of species at risk. Of the 622 Critically Endangered species in those records, 202 eat only invertebrates, so their survival depends on having enough of them.

Yet the Red List assessments for those species say little about insect decline as a main cause. Most threatened species lack detailed diet records, and losing food is tangled up with losing habitat.

Most insect species haven’t been formally described. Many diet studies lump insects together with spiders, worms and other invertebrates. And experiments that start with the insects and follow the effects up to the animals that eat them are lacking.

The review came out of the Status of Insects research network. The authors call for shared standards for recording which insects animals eat and use.

They also favor closer work between researchers who study animals with backbones and those who are insect specialists. Citizen science photos and DNA traces from the environment could also help.

Until that data exists, researchers can’t reliably rank which of these thousands of partnerships animals can least afford to lose.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Reviews Biodiversity.
