Korean Hair Vinegar Is the Secret to Glossy, Glass-Like Hair

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

If we’re being honest, shiny glass hair takes quite a bit of work. There’s the salon gloss every few weeks, the shine serum before you leave the house, and the professional blowout that never looks quite as glamorous the next morning. So when a two-minute shower step started promising the same mirror-like finish, it was bound to take off.

Enter Korean hair vinegar, which has been all over TikTok lately, with videos of dull, frizzy hair turning smooth and shiny after a single wash. It might sound like something you’d put on a salad, but the idea has deep roots. “In Korea, vinegar has always been both food and medicine,” says Ellie Byun, OMD, a doctor of Korean medicine and licensed acupuncturist.

Still, vinegar isn’t exactly the first thing you’d reach for to fix your hair, and plenty of viral trends don’t exactly live up to the hype. So does this one? Below, experts break down what hair vinegar actually does, who should try it and who should skip it, plus a few of our favorite formulas.

At its simplest, Korean hair vinegar is an acidic rinse that finishes the job your shampoo starts. “Shampoo lifts and opens the hair and scalp, and hair vinegar is the finishing step that settles everything back down,” says Dr. Byun. “Hair vinegar is to shampoo what toner is to cleanser.”

The idea isn’t new, but the format is. “The old kitchen version meant guessing at the ratio and hoping for the best, but the downside is that it could leave hair feeling stiff,” says Michelle Lee, chief marketing officer of K-Beauty World and former editor-in-chief of Allure. Dr. Byun points out that homemade rinses are also hard to get right. “The difference is that the strength changes every time you mix it, and if it’s too strong, it can irritate the scalp,” she says. “Korean formulas adjust the pH and add botanicals like green tea or mint, so you get the benefits without the guesswork.”

So why is it suddenly everywhere? “I think it’s kind of a classic case of an old idea getting a modern update,” says Lee. Most of the credit goes to glass hair. “Glass hair is about a smooth, light-reflecting cuticle, and vinegar is one of the simplest ways to get there,” she says. For many Korean women, it’s been part of the routine for years.

It mostly comes down to pH. “A healthy scalp has a pH of about 5.5, and the hair fiber is about 3.67, so both are naturally acidic,” says Byun. “Alkaline products increase the negative charge on the hair surface, which creates static, friction, tangling, and frizz. An acidic rinse reverses that.” Once the cuticle lies flat, hair reflects more light, which is where the shine comes from. “On the hair, a well-formulated acidic rinse can help reduce static and friction, leaving strands smoother, less frizzy, and easier to detangle,” adds Kristin Lim, founder of Base-K.

It can also help with dullness caused by buildup. “Vinegar also helps break down product and mineral buildup, which is often why hair looks dull in the first place,” says Lee. “It’s a gentle reset more than a miracle.” It’s not a substitute for a good shampoo, though. “Acidity alone does not guarantee effective buildup removal,” says Joe Cho, Team Lead of Media Relations at Medicube.

Lee says Korean hair vinegar works especially well for “people with hard water, since mineral buildup makes hair look flat and feel rough,” along with anyone who uses a lot of dry shampoo or styling products. Color-treated hair can benefit, too, since a smoother cuticle makes color look shinier. Byun also recommends it for finer textures. “It works well for fine hair that falls flat easily and scalps that get oily quickly,” she says.

The most obvious benefit is shine, and you’ll usually see it after one wash. “A mildly acidic wash or treatment helps the cuticle lie flatter, so hair looks shinier and feels smoother,” says Lee. If you color your hair, there’s another bonus. “It’s also wonderful for color-treated hair, because a smoother cuticle makes color look more vibrant,” says Byun. For oily scalps, it can even leave things feeling fresher between washes.

Acid isn’t gentle on every scalp. “If your scalp is very dry or sensitive, skip it or use a very mild formula,” says Byun. “Skip it if you have eczema, psoriasis, or any broken skin. If you have dandruff or seborrheic dermatitis, start with a medicated shampoo.” Ingredients matter, too. “Even a formulated hair vinegar can irritate some people, depending on its acidity, fragrance and other ingredients,” says Lim. And if your hair is badly damaged, keep your expectations in check. “While acidic products may temporarily improve smoothness, they cannot reverse structural damage,” says Cho.

Order matters here. Use it after shampoo and before conditioner, and there’s a reason for that timing. “Right after shampooing, your hair and scalp are at their highest pH, so that’s when an acidic rinse works best,” says Byun. Work it through damp hair and onto the scalp if the product says it’s safe there, then follow her method: “Leave it on for one to three minutes, rinse with lukewarm or cool water, then apply a light conditioner, mainly on the ends.” Some formulas are leave-in, so always check the label first. If you’d rather make your own, Byun’s ratio is a good place to start. “Dilute one to two tablespoons of apple cider vinegar in about two cups of water and start mild,” she says.

Less is more when it comes to how often you should use this type of product. “Start with once or twice a week. If your scalp is oily or your water is hard, you can use it a bit more. If your hair is dry or fine, use it less,” says Dr. Byun. Your hair will let you know when you’ve overdone it. “If your hair starts to feel squeaky or straw-like, that’s the signal to scale back,” says Lee. “I’ve found that less often usually gives better results than more.”

“For color-treated hair, choose a rinse specifically labeled color-safe. ‘Hair vinegar’ alone doesn’t guarantee compatibility,” says Lim. After a fresh color appointment or a keratin treatment, give it some time. “Just wait a few days after coloring or a keratin treatment, and check with your stylist,” says Dr. Byun. If you wear extensions, Lee recommends caution: “With extensions, keep the products away from tapes, bonds, and glued areas, since some can loosen with certain ingredients.”

So will your hair end up smelling like vinegar after use? Yes, but not for long. “And acetic acid, the compound behind the vinegar smell, is volatile, so most of it evaporates as you rinse and your hair dries,” says Dr. Byun. Most formulas also add fruit or floral scents to cover it. “It’s a tangy note in the shower and then it’s gone,” says Lee.

Look for a formula that lists its pH and tells you how to use it. “A brand that shares its pH and provides clear usage instructions is a good sign,” says Lim. Lee is even firmer about that: “If a brand won’t say anything about pH, I’d skip it.” Soothing ingredients like green tea, centella, panthenol and glycerin are good signs, while heavy fragrance and drying alcohols are worth avoiding if your scalp is sensitive. “‘Natural’ doesn’t automatically mean gentler,” says Lim.
