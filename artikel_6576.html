How to Make Whipped Cream Perfectly, Every Time

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

If you love a low-effort, high-reward kitchen assignment, learning how to make whipped cream is a walk in the clouds. The only ingredients are time and heavy cream. You can use an electric mixer, a whisk, or a mason jar and strong-armed devotion.

In whipped cream, and in life, the key challenge is knowing when to stop. Desserts like Strawberry Eton Mess or Triple Chocolate Mousse Cake fare best with silky, unsweetened, soft-peaked whip. Others, like this show-stopping Berries and Cream Mille-Feuille, gain structure from sturdier peaks.

To make whipped cream at home, all you need is cold heavy cream, a large bowl, and a whisk. You can also use a handheld mixer or a mason jar and some elbow grease (more on that later).

If you’re opting for a bowl-and-whisk combo, choose your tools wisely. Cream roughly doubles in volume as you whip it, so use a bowl with three to four times the capacity of your liquid. A large balloon whisk, rather than a narrow sauce whisk, helps incorporate plenty of air into the cream.

Be strategic in the dairy aisle too. Heavy cream and heavy whipping cream contain at least 36% milk fat and can be used interchangeably. Skip anything labeled simply “whipping cream”—or, heaven forbid, “light cream”—which contains less fat and won’t hold its loft as well.

For a basic batch, start with 1 cup cold heavy cream, which makes about 2 cups whipped cream. For lightly sweetened whipped cream, add 1 Tbsp. powdered sugar; for vanilla whipped cream, add ½ tsp. vanilla extract or paste.

With your equipment and ingredients in hand, there are only five steps and infinite possibilities.

Because whipped cream is usually eaten with or as dessert, you might not need to sweeten it at all. To keep flavors balanced, we tend to serve whipped cream au naturel with Bourbon Caramel Apple Pie or Diner-Style Rice Pudding.

But there are no hard-and-fast rules. Maybe you love sweetened whipped cream, or you’re serving it with something less sugary—like, say, a bowl of tart blackberries and torn mint leaves.

For lightly sweetened whipped cream, start with about 1 Tbsp. sugar per cup of cream. Powdered sugar dissolves easily and contains a little stabilizer (usually cornstarch), which can lend the whipped cream some extra holding power. Some cooks prefer the cleaner flavor of granulated sugar, which takes a little longer to dissolve. Either way, add the sugar before you start whipping.

If you’re gazing at a bowl of fluffy, ethereal whip and thinking, “Could I make this even more enchanting?” the answer is yes. There are two primary ways to make flavored whipped cream: infuse or stir.

Kitchen professionals and advance planners tend to cold-infuse. Combine your cream with flavorings like rice (for a creamy Horchata) or bourbon (to take a classic pumpkin pie over the top), then store it in the fridge anywhere from three hours to overnight. Strain out any solids before whipping.

You can also add simple flavorings, like vanilla extract, a small splash of liqueur, cocoa, ground spices, citrus zest, or coffee syrup, before you start whipping. For heavier or chunky additions, such as toasted nuts or chopped chocolate, use a rubber spatula to gently fold them in once the cream is whipped.

Peaks are the shape your whipped cream takes when you pull the whisk out of the bowl and hold it upright like the Statue of Liberty’s torch.

Soft peaks are cloudlike and slump over when you lift the whisk. Stiff peaks stand upright and hold their shape. Somewhere in between are medium peaks, which have enough structure to hold a shape while remaining soft and supple.

Most Bon Appétit recipes, such as these Mix-and-Match Fools, involve whipping cream to soft peaks. That velvety texture is our go-to when we’re dolloping whipped cream atop pecan pie.

But we aren’t always such softies. Medium peaks help this Ginger-Raspberry Icebox Cake keep its shape. And old-fashioned chocolate mousse involves whipping batches of cream into stiff and soft peaks: The former is folded into the chocolate mixture for structure, while the softer stuff tops the dessert.

By hand: Our preferred method, whipping cream by hand requires the most time and effort, but it also gives you optimal control over its final texture. Even if you use a mixer to get your whipped cream started, switching to a whisk for the final few moments makes it easier to stop exactly where you want.

In a Mason jar: No whisk? No mixer? No problem. To make whipped cream with your bare hands, put a container with a tight-fitting lid, large mason jar, or leak-proof cocktail shaker into the freezer for five minutes. Get the heavy cream very cold and make sure you have ample headspace in your vessel of choice. About one cup of liquid in a four-cup container is a good amount.

Secure the lid and shake it like you mean it. For the creamiest results, a consistently assertive pace is better than intermittent spurts of brute force. When the liquid stops sloshing and starts feeling and sounding heavier, open the lid and take a peek. If it looks underwhipped, close it back up and keep shaking.

Good news for meal preppers: You can make whipped cream hours in advance. Err on the side of underwhipping it during your prep. Store the lightly whipped cream, covered, in the fridge for up to four hours before serving, then give it a few energetic whisks to bring back its bounce right before you dollop it onto dessert.

You can also freeze whipped cream (no, seriously). It takes a bit of effort, though. Line a baking sheet with parchment, then pipe or scoop spoonfuls of whipped cream onto it. Freeze until the whipped cream nuggets are solid, then peel them off the parchment and store them in an airtight bag in the freezer for one to two months. When you’re ready to serve, let them thaw at room temperature for about 15 minutes.

Instant vanilla pudding is the key to luscious, long-lasting whipped cream.

Need it to hold up longer? Instant vanilla pudding mix is one of BA’s favorite shortcuts for stabilized whipped cream. For every cup of heavy cream, add 1 Tbsp. instant vanilla pudding mix (call it pastry cream powder if anyone gives you sass) and 2 tsp. powdered sugar, then whip as usual. The modified starch in the pudding mix absorbs excess moisture and helps prevent the cream from separating, keeping it fluffy for up to 24 hours.

For something tangier, sour cream adds both flavor and body. Our Pecan Cream Pie whisks ¼ cup sour cream into 1 cup heavy cream; the sour cream cuts the richness while helping the topping hold its shape.

In any case, your dinner party guests don’t have to sit quietly as you furiously whisk their dessert topping to order. Prep work never tasted so sweet.

If your whip isn’t coming together quite right, don’t fret. Simple solutions abound.

This is probably a temperature issue. Cold cream whips more readily and holds onto the air you’re incorporating more effectively, so it comes together faster—and stays fluffy longer. Pop the bowl, whisk, and cream back into the freezer for five minutes, or into the fridge for up to 30. Once everything is cold, resume whisking.

Another possible culprit is the fat content of your cream. Opt for heavy whipping cream or heavy cream over half-and-half or other heavy cream substitutes.

If the cream is just a little too stiff or starting to look grainy, add a splash of chilled heavy cream and slowly whisk it in. Add more, a little at a time, until the texture smooths out.

Keep a close eye on it: If you whisk for too long, you’ll end up with a bowl of butter.
