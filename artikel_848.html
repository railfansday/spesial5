Gonzalo García: ‘I love Craven Cottage – the fans can reach out and touch you’

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Spain Under-21 striker admits the pace of the Premier League has surprised him but says he must stay calm over goal drought at Fulham

The first thing Rita Hayworth’s great nephew says when he walks in is: “Sorry I’m late.” Gonzalo García is not late, but it’s very like him: he is impeccably polite, accommodating, and there’s an almost gentle quality to him, a calm that’s not common but can help, especially in moments when the pressure builds. There’s a reason one of the coaches standing in the rain by the pitch where Spain’s under‑21s have just finished training says everyone here loves García. The Fulham striker shakes hands, introduces himself and ducks into the dressing room to change; then a few minutes later he’s back, apologising for a wait that wasn’t one.

Time to talk, so let’s start with Tinseltown, the family tale. García laughs. His great-aunt, born Margarita Carmen Cansino, was among the biggest stars in Hollywood, although he’s not sure he’s seen any of her films. His grandfather was a surgeon and others in the family followed. His uncles played rugby for Spain, but that wasn’t his thing. As for him, he enrolled in a degree in business management and analytics, and reckons he might have liked law, yet even that wasn’t his chosen field.

Hayworth’s family was from Seville province. “I know she was a great actress but I’m afraid I have to admit I’m not one for old films,” García concedes in an even tone. “My uncles played rugby for Ciencias and practically all of them were in the national team together. They tried to get me to play with my cousins, but I was always more into football.” He was good at it too: another Spain international, the Club World Cup’s top scorer, playing for Real Madrid.

Then this summer, with opportunities limited, he joined Fulham, his introductory interview conducted in impeccable English. Having joined Madrid aged 10, he was leaving home for the first time, as Fulham’s record signing, at 22. He had the language and intelligence to embrace it. As for being uprooted, he was used to that, growing up in Argentina and Chile as well as Spain.

“From very little my parents impressed upon me the importance of study, learning languages,” García says. “I’m at Villanueva university [in Madrid]. I started with dual honours business and analytics, although that became difficult with my profession. Now it’s just business, and I’m trying to arrange with the university how best to handle it [from London]. My parents considered it fundamental to speak English and I was always at international schools.

“Every two or three years we would change city. Obviously, there would be phases when I felt: ‘Pfff, again?!’ Having to move home because of my parents’ work. But I look back and feel grateful because, seen with perspective, it gives you opportunities to meet new people, see new places, learn. I’ve always had foreign friends with whom I spoke English.”

García has been joined at Fulham by César Palacios and Manuel Ángel from Madrid’s academy. Three 22-year-olds, like your very own Erasmus course. “Nah,” García says, laughing. “I wouldn’t call it an Erasmus because we’re talking about a big, ambitious career step. We’re grateful for the trust placed in us. I’ve been pleasantly surprised by London, a huge city with people from all nationalities and cultures and Fulham fit the situation I was in, the desire to grow.

“Álvaro Arbeloa explained the project, convinced me. I’d worked with him in Madrid. He knows me perfectly, how far he can push me. Equally, I know what he wants of me. That helps … [Arbeloa] told me it was a project that it will take time but that he needed me. I’m very, very happy. It’s one of the world’s best leagues, if not the best.”

And the toughest? Is English football as wild as it looks? “Yes!” García replies. “They had told me it was demanding, hard, but …” He cracks up. “I have to admit it still surprised me. That first game against Chelsea, I finished really, really tired. It was box to box, intense, a lot of running. Bit by bit I’m adapting. The intensity and pace is very different.

“Spain is more tactical, a bit more technical. English football is pace. You can’t lose concentration for a second, not a second. Anything can happen any moment. How many Premier League games are decided late? Suddenly there’s a goal in the 90th minute and again on 93. It’s your corner and their chance. The level of concentration, that constant transition, is what most sets the Premier League apart.”

Despite defeat on that opening day against Chelsea, García scored. A superb goal followed against AFC Wimbledon in the Carabao Cup. “It was super special to do it at Craven Cottage. I love how close the fans are – they can reach out and touch you. But I don’t consider myself someone who’s easily satisfied. I’m not like that. I always want more, more, more.”

García hasn’t scored since and Fulham went five league games without a win, pressure building. Which is where the calmness comes in. By the time they faced Hull, Arbeloa appeared on the edge of the sack, questions asked about his signings, García especially. Were three Madrid academy kids really what Fulham needed? Was the coach’s relationship with them not such a good thing after all?

No player is impermeable to what is said, García admits, but he insists: “We treat that as outside noise. And we all knew that the beginning wouldn’t be easy. There’s a change of cycle: the coach goes, a new coach comes, there are signings, a different system, idea. Everyone has to adapt: those already at the club and those of us who are new. We’re young too. Results weren’t going our way but we had good intentions: to play good football, build, apply the manager’s ideas bit by bit. We had to remain calm.”

Fulham go into Saturday’s game at Manchester City unbeaten in four in the league, the sense of them shifting towards something more pragmatic not shared by García. “I don’t think we changed anything against Hull,” he says of a game Fulham won 1-0. “We didn’t do anything we weren’t already doing. Nothing. Maybe we controlled the small details that hurt us but the idea was the same. The players were very clear what the manager asked, very firm: it was just a question of time. You have to be patient. If you watch Fulham, you see the manager’s idea: have the ball, construct attacks well, show maximum intensity constantly with and without the ball. Aggressive, ambitious.”

Chances are coming for García but goals aren’t. He has started every league game but not scored or assisted since the opening day. Yet if there is a sense of a man feeling the pressure, that’s not repeated here; instead there’s an evenness, an ease. And a smile when another, more welcome, stat is raised: he has made more off‑the‑ball runs into the area than anyone. Something to hold on to, perhaps, a process that will pay off. “Really?” he says. “Well, I’m happy for people to see that a striker can help the team without the ball. In the end, it’s about following the manager’s instructions.”

In the end, though, teams will be judged on results and strikers on goals, price too. He knows: this is what he does, the path he chose. “Pressure depends on how you look at it, really,” García says. “Football is [very psychological]. It happens fast: it doesn’t give you time to enjoy good moments or linger on bad ones. It’s a rollercoaster – up there, down here. It’s not flat, but you maintain balance, work daily, improve: go in, focus, repeat.

“It’s not that I don’t ‘care’ about the price, it’s that the market is the market; it doesn’t depend on me. Calmness always, perspective … I know strikers are measured by goals but, well, the days I don’t score I’m completely sure I will be able to walk off the pitch and say that, no matter what happened, I helped my team, doing a load of other things. It is what it is.”

It is also time. A head peers around the door, the bus is going. García holds out a hand, apologises again. “It’s been a pleasure,” he says.
