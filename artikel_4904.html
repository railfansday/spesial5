It Just Got Easier to Get Peptides in the US. Here's What Could Come Next.

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Spoiler: More access doesn’t necessarily mean more safety.

ON WEDNESDAY, THE U.S. Food and Drug Administration announced a new policy that makes six experimental peptides more readily available. According to a document released by the HHS, the government will not penalize specialized pharmacies for making and supplying these popular substances to patients with a prescription. The peptides: BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500.

This does not mean these particular peptides have been approved for use by the FDA. In fact, none of them have gone through the rigorous human testing required for FDA drug approval. And the new policy is not intended to be permanent. Instead, it allows temporary access as the FDA decides on a longer-term plan for the six peptides. It also includes the caveat that compounders report “serious adverse events, and product quality issues to FDA and to the States where the products are compounded,” according to the document.

This topic has been red-hot since July, when the FDA held an advisory committee meeting to consider whether to add these peptides to a list of substances that U.S. compounding pharmacies are allowed to manufacture and supply. These substances are already widely used and obtained on the gray market. If people are going to use them, advocates have argued, it would be safest if those substances came from licensed pharmacies and are used with physician oversight.

Critics, on the other hand—including FDA scientists—are concerned that the government may be opening the floodgates to under-studied and potentially harmful drugs. These substances, they note, have not been fully vetted for safety and effectiveness. And some of these peptides are already being promoted and sold on the basis of scientific claims that experts say are unsupported or questionable.

Here's what could come next, if you're thinking about taking one of these six compounded peptides.

Some parts of the current conversation played out at last month’s PepMed conference in Washington, D.C. A sign pointing guests to the registration table featured a stylized gold peptide overlaid on the white dome of the U.S. Capitol. PepMed’s organizer, the American Academy of Peptide Medicine (AAPM), founded just last year, has already become a major voice in this debate.

In advance of the FDA’s July meeting, AAPM submitted hundreds of pages to a crowded public docket, including information about each of the seven peptides then under review. Several of its advisory board members spoke during the meeting’s public comment sessions. And the group appears to have the ear of the administration. PepMed speakers included the assistant secretary for health, Admiral Brian Christine, and a host of prominent MAHA figures.

The September event opened with an on-the-record media hour, featuring a dozen speakers, including physicians and owners and CEOs of compounding pharmacies and telehealth companies. A recurring theme was the danger of the gray market, where peptide quality can be difficult or impossible to verify and consumers often make treatment decisions without medical oversight—“this is not a victory for patient safety,” said Ricardo Rossello, PhD, a U.S. congressional delegate for Puerto Rico and AAPM advisory board member who co-founded Beijing Prosperous Biopharm, a medical research company.

Many of the speakers also lamented the failure of the U.S. health care system to fully meet the needs of patients, and some of the day’s speakers, including Alex Tatem, MD, an Indiana-based urologist and men’s health specialist with a large YouTube following, presented experimental peptides as part of the solution. “Our established medical system is very much a sick care model,” said Tatem, who serves on the AAPM’s medical advisory board. It’s important to help patients in a health crisis, he told me in an interview. But people want to be proactive about disease prevention. In his view, the peptides under FDA’s consideration can play a role by expanding “the toolkit that’s available to providers.”

At the FDA’s July advisory committee meeting, the agency’s scientists advised against allowing broad access to these peptides, in part because they have not been fully vetted for safety and effectiveness in the kind of long-term, rigorously designed human studies typically required for drug approval.Even at the conference, some doctors acknowledged the evidence gaps. Abud Bakri, MD, works in California as a physician who specializes in caring for hospitalized patients. He also runs a separate medical practice focused on preventive and longevity medicine, and he serves on the Enhanced Games Independent Medical Commission. His interest in peptides has been long-standing enough to predate his medical career.

Bakri, now 33, has been a powerlifter since he was 17. He explained that he has a genetic condition that may increase his risk for ligament injuries. He first used BPC-157 almost a decade ago, and believes the peptide helped him recover faster, though he acknowledges that this is hard to prove definitively, given the absence of well-designed human studies.

If just a fraction of the rat and Petri dish research holds up in humans, BPC-157 could be an incredibly helpful drug in the ICU, he told me at the conference, during an interview at an outdoor dining area. But ideally, he says, its effectiveness should be proven before it’s used in this setting and potential side effects should be better understood. “If I’m going to have an honest conversation with a patient,” he said, “I need to be able to fully elucidate the risk, which I can’t for some of these compounds.”

After the conference, I spoke by Zoom with Thomas Kremen, MD, a physician at UCLA Health who works for several athletic teams, including the Los Angeles Chargers and Lakers. Kremen and his colleagues recently reviewed the evidence behind six popular experimental peptides. He and his co-authors seemed unimpressed. “It’s probably not a strong stance to be recommending these things,” he told me.

Would it be accurate for physicians to tell their patients that a peptide like BPC-157 heals tissue?

“Maybe if they're talking about a rat in some study,” he said. “But I think if you're making a claim, you better be able to cite the literature that supports that.”

Right now, not only is there a dearth of high-quality clinical evidence that peptides work and are safe, unsupported claims are being made about these compounds.

For instance, among the documents that AAPM submitted to the FDA’s public docket was a white paper claiming that two experimental peptides—epitalon and BPC-157—are endogenous, indicating they are naturally produced by the body. Similar descriptions can be found on websites for telehealth companies and online peptide vendors suggesting their products are synthetic versions of naturally derived peptides.

Epitalon is a lab-made peptide based on an extract that researchers in the Soviet Union obtained from cattle brains. Terry Roberts, PhD, a researcher who studies epitalon at Brunel University of London, said it’s unclear whether humans produce this peptide. As far as he’s aware, no studies have explicitly described its presence in humans.

And BPC-157? The peptide was discovered in gastric juice, said Vince Luca, PhD, a researcher at the Moffitt Cancer Center in Florida. Yet 35 years after its discovery, nobody has found a gene that encodes for it—it could have come from a microbe or from food. It’s not impossible that the body produces BPC-157, said Luca, but he thinks it’s unlikely. He also says that health care professionals “should know enough not to tell [people] that it's an endogenous peptide” when it is very unlikely to be one. And regardless, he continued, “there are plenty of endogenous peptides that, injected at high concentrations, would have a really negative effect on the body.” In other words, being endogenous alone doesn’t mean it’s safe.

(The American Academy of Peptide Medicine’s press team did not respond to queries asking how the group determined that BPC-157 and epitalon are endogenous.)

“My prediction is that those who are already using the gray market will continue to do so,” said Ryan Beattie, director of business development at Adaptog Research & Life Sciences, a UK-based company that sells experimental peptides. “So I think in the initial term, people will use compounding pharmacies in the US. What I then predict will happen is people will then get used to it, get more aware of it—and then switch to the gray market because of the cost.”

He may be right. For this story, we spoke with two peptide users who had been living with chronic pain, and after trying physical therapy as well as other treatment approaches, turned to the Wolverine stack, a combination of BPC-157 and TB-500. Both said they felt the peptides helped them—and neither was sure they’d switch to compounded versions, which are more expensive than the gray market and aren’t covered by insurance. One of them, Charles A., asked, “What are the hoops and what are the costs going to be?”

A third person, who asked to be referred to as James—the name he uses in online communities—tried an oral version of BPC-157 four years ago and said he hasn’t been the same since then. Shortly after taking the experimental compound, he felt anxiety followed by sensory and emotional numbness that still hasn’t gone away. He also developed an autoimmune condition. Online, he found others with some similar experiences. When he looked carefully at the published research, he concluded that it didn’t match the online hype. “I felt very foolish afterwards.”

Some scientists are continuing to investigate these peptides. At Moffitt, Luca is currently studying BPC-157 and he’s keeping an open mind.

Over the next few months, he and his colleagues plan to run a variety of very basic experiments in which they add the peptide to human cells and see how those cells respond. The results could provide additional insight into what, precisely, this molecule is doing in the body—and they may help explain why users have reported everything from striking benefits to lasting harm.

Perhaps one day, BPC-157 will prove to be an effective drug, said Luca. But for now, his advice is to wait on using it and other experimental peptides: “I just worry that people are a little too cavalier in how these are handled.”

Sara Talpos is a Michigan-based journalist covering biomedical research and public health. She’s written for publications including STAT, Science, and Undark, where she’s a contributing editor.
