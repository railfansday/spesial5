Can Silicon Valley Build A Safe Class 8 Truck? Early EV Semi Fires Expose The Experience Gap

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

If you drove through the East Bay area of California on Tuesday night, you probably got caught in a nightmare. A Tesla Semi traveling down Interstate 880 in Hayward caught fire during the evening commute. There was no crash, no multi-vehicle pileup, and no obvious collision. The driver noticed something was wrong, pulled over near Highway 92, and got out unharmed.

Then the lithium-ion pack did what high-energy battery chemistry does when it gets pushed past its thermal limits… By the time crews finished foaming down the wreckage, all that remained of the tractor was its rear axles and a pile of ash.

The California Highway Patrol shut down both directions of the freeway out of concern over toxic chemical smoke. Northbound lanes eventually opened back up, but southbound lanes stayed closed for nearly ten hours while fire crews managed the blaze and let the pack burn itself out. When the sun came up, all that remained of the tractor was its rear axles and a pile of ash.

This is the second time in just over two years that a single Tesla Semi has shut down a major California highway for half a day. Back in August 2024, a Tesla-operated Semi drifted off Interstate 80 near Emigrant Gap, hit a tree, and caught fire. It took 50,000 gallons of water, air drops from Cal Fire, and a 15-hour closure to clear the road.

Whenever this happens, the anti-EV crowd immediately pounces and claims electric trucks are rolling time bombs. That’s a lie, as we’ve repeatedly explained here, but the knee-jerk reaction from tech enthusiasts, which claims this is just normal trucking business, is equally disconnected from reality.

Something deeper is going on here, and it highlights an important cultural divide between Silicon Valley and the century-old world of heavy-duty commercial trucking. Instead of trashing people and pointing fingers, let’s look at a deeper lesson the industry can learn from this.

First, let’s look at the numbers before anyone starts cherry-picking data.

According to the National Fire Protection Association, US fire departments respond to roughly 15,000 large truck fires every single year. That is about 40 diesel rigs catching fire every day across the country. Blown turbochargers spray oil on hot exhaust manifolds, wheel-end bearings seize at 70 mph, and dragging drum brakes ignite recap tires. Diesel trucks burn all the time, so fire is not a phenomenon unique to electric trucks.

The difference is how they burn. When a diesel tractor catches fire, the local volunteer fire department can usually knock it down with an engine tank and some foam in 45 minutes. A heavy tow truck drags the scorched chassis off the road, sweepers clear the debris, and traffic starts moving again before the morning rush hour is ruined.

When an 800-plus kilowatt-hour commercial battery pack enters thermal runaway, the playbook goes out the window. The cells generate their own oxygen as they break down, turning the battery into a self-sustaining furnace. First responders cannot just drown it and walk away. Their only choice is to pump tens of thousands of gallons of water on it for hours or stand back in defensive mode to keep it from spreading while half a county gets gridlocked. At best, all they can do is hopefully cool it down enough to get it off the road before it might catch fire again.

Then there is the sample size problem. There are roughly 4 million Class 8 commercial diesel trucks on American roads. By contrast, the active Tesla Semi fleet is estimated somewhere between 150 and a few hundred early-production and pilot tractors.

Mathematically speaking, you cannot calculate a clean failure rate on an N of two. A single flat tire that catches fire and spreads to a battery pack can swing the statistical safety record of a 100-truck pilot fleet by 50%. But you also cannot ignore it when two trucks in a tiny pilot fleet cause catastrophic, 10-to-15-hour interstate shutdowns.

So, instead of being lazy and relying on just the numbers, we need to look for more context.

This is not uniquely a Tesla problem. It is an electric startup problem.

Remember Nikola? In the summer of 2023, several of their Tre battery-electric trucks caught fire, including one at their Phoenix headquarters and another in customer testing. Nikola initially hinted at foul play, but third-party investigators quickly found the real culprit: a coolant leak inside the battery packs supplied by Romeo Power had caused an internal electrical short. Nikola had to issue a voluntary recall on all 209 trucks they had built, halting sales and dragging every pack back to the shop to fix the design.

Startups approach trucking like consumer electronics. They design from a clean sheet, prioritize maximum aerodynamic efficiency, integrate components into custom monolithic structures, and move fast.

But, when you take the “move fast and break things” approach, the stakes are a lot higher in the trucking world.

Class 8 trucking is a brutal environment that eats clever ideas for breakfast. A big rig does not live the gentle life of a passenger car in sunny California. It flexes under 80,000 pounds of gross weight, takes violent torsional twisting over uneven loading docks, endures constant low-frequency road battering, and rattles across millions of frost heaves.

If there is a slightly loose coolant quick-connect, an undersized wire harness, or a busbar that flexes half a millimeter under torsional load, commercial duty cycles are like Liam Neeson in Taken. If you’re a problem of any kind, the duty cycle will find you, and it will kill you.

This brings us to the traditional truck builders. Why aren’t we seeing Freightliner eCascadias or Volvo VNR Electrics burning down to the rims on I-80? They’re electric, too, right?

It’s not because traditional truckmakers possess magical, fireproof battery chemistry. They buy lithium-ion cells from the same tier-one suppliers as everyone else. In fact, if you dig into public regulatory filings, other manufacturers have run into the exact same terrifying failure modes that bit Nikola.

In 2023, Volvo and Mack filed safety recall reports with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) covering hundreds of electric trucks. What was the defect? Their battery supplier had two separate manufacturing errors: coolant lines that were not fully locked into place, creating the risk of conductive fluid leaking across cells, and over-torqued internal fasteners that could snap off and rattle around inside the pack as loose metallic debris. Both defects created direct short-circuit and fire risks.

Why didn’t you read about those trucks burning down on the highway? Because the so-called legacy manufacturers have spent a century building institutional safety systems.

They caught this problem when a small fire actually broke out in a battery pack in the factory. The fire stayed contained within the pack, but Volvo didn’t try to hide it or explain it away. Instead, they did what the old school companies do, even if it slowed them down. Within a week, there was a stop-ship order on the packs and trucks already on the road were called in for a swap.

Traditional truckmakers move at a glacial pace compared to Silicon Valley, and tech fans love to criticize them for it. But, that slow pace comes from decades of scar tissue earned in battle. When an old school builder sees an internal assembly error or gets a strange sensor reading in fleet monitoring, their reflex is not to push an over-the-air firmware patch and hope for the best. They trigger stop-ship orders, use their huge dealer networks to ground the fleet, file Part 573 defect notices with the feds, and tear the battery packs open in a service bay.

Their systems caught the defect in the shop before it had the chance to catch fire on the freeway.

There is also an important philosophical divide in how these trucks are packaged.

Tesla chose the moonshot approach. To hit a 500-mile range target while hauling full freight, they needed maximum energy density and minimal tare weight. That led them to build a massive, integrated, structural battery pack that sits low between the frame rails. It is an engineering marvel in packaging efficiency, but it is also a monolithic block of energy. If one section suffers an internal failure, the entire pack is at risk.

Traditional builders like Volvo and Freightliner (Daimler Truck) took a far more conservative, regional-haul route. Instead of packing nearly a megawatt-hour of energy into a single tightly integrated structural assembly under the cab, they use smaller, lower-capacity modular packs—with Volvo literally hanging separate battery enclosures off the outside of the frame rails where diesel saddle tanks normally sit.

Hanging heavy battery boxes on the side of the chassis is terrible for maximum range and adds unnecessary weight, and may leave them more vulnerable to collisions. But, from a safety and service standpoint, it is brilliant. The packs are physically isolated from each other. If one module suffers a mechanical puncture, a coolant leak, or a thermal event, the damage is far easier to contain, inspect, and unbolt.

Silicon Valley brought the heavy trucking industry kicking and screaming into the electric era. Without Tesla pushing the envelope on megawatt charging and long-range electric haulers, diesel truckmakers would still be dragging their feet with compliance pilot programs, if even that.

Building an electric truck that can pull 82,000 pounds is not just a software problem, and it’s not just a battery chemistry problem. It is a mechanical durability problem that has to survive millions of miles of rough roads, rough drivers, and unpredictable operating conditions.

Experienced truck builders know how to build a chassis that survives that abuse, but they were slow to embrace the battery. Silicon Valley knows how to build the battery, but they are still learning how punishing the road really is.

Until those two worlds finally learn from each other, fire crews are going to keep having very long nights on the interstate. Hopefully more cross-pollination and experience eventually lead to improvements for all sides of the industry.
