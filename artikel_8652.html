Stranger Things Day slipped past earlier this month. Fans mark it every November 6, the anniversary of the night in 1983 when Will Byers rode his bike home from Mike’s basement and never made it.

One minute he was pedaling through Hawkins, Indiana, and the next he was gone, swallowed by a dimension running parallel to his own. Same streets, same house, just darker, colder, and coated in a black rot that fed on everything it touched.

If you missed the rewatch and the Eggo waffles, you have 354 days until the next one. You have 44 until December 31.

Your retirement savings took the same trip Will did. Nobody stole the money. It got relocated. The IRS built a gate decades ago, and your 401(k), your IRA, and your SEP all got pulled through it. The balance shows up in your statement. Touching it before 59½ costs a ransom.

By the time Stranger Things Day rolls around again on November 6, 2027, the 2026 tax year will be closed and filed. Every move in this article that opens an exit, the conversions, the contributions, the rollovers you make or wisely skip, has to happen before the ball drops on New Year’s Eve. The IRS doesn’t hand out extensions on the calendar.

Stranger Things needed five seasons to explain the Upside Down: what it was, how it touched the real world, where the gates opened and closed. Viewers watched the characters stumble through it for years with Christmas lights and homemade gear because no one who understood the place ever sat them down and drew it out.

That is the average physician’s relationship with the tax code.

Thousands of pages, scattered across decades of amendments and rulings and bills with names like SECURE Act 2.0 and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Medical school skips it. Residency skips it too. And the advisor managing your portfolio, assuming you have one, probably maxed your employer 401(k), mentioned a backdoor Roth in passing, and called it a day.

The exits are sitting right there in the code, seven of them, penalty-free and legal, like the hidden passages in the Creel house. Physicians retire years later than they want to, or burn out before they get there, because nobody drew them the map.

This is the map, updated for 2026, a year when the rules changed in your favor for once.

Season one gave us the Demogorgon, a faceless predator that dragged people into the Upside Down and fed on them. The whole town lived in fear of a thing it didn’t understand.

Pull money from an IRA or 401(k) before 59½ and the IRS adds 10% on top of ordinary income tax. For a physician in a high bracket, the combined hit can eat close to half of whatever comes out.

The Demogorgon doesn’t negotiate. Burned out at 57, practice imploded, three kids in college and a cash crunch right now? It takes its cut regardless.

What the residents of Hawkins never learned was that the monster had weaknesses. Enough heat and it retreated. Eleven could stop it cold. The penalty has weaknesses too, seven of them, and the rest of this piece walks through each one.

Eleven could move objects, close gates, and stop the worst things the Upside Down produced. She’d spent years locked in a government lab, and most people had no idea she existed.

The assumption bleeding W-2 physicians dry is that a Solo 401(k) belongs only to private practice owners and full-time contractors. Any physician with self-employment income and no full-time employees qualifies.

The hospitalist picking up two weekend locum shifts a month qualifies, as does the employed cardiologist who took a single paid speaking gig and the academic who cashed a CME honorarium check.

The size of that income sets how much you can contribute. It never decides whether you’re eligible.

For 2026, total contributions to a Solo 401(k) cap out at $72,000 per person. Inside that structure lives the Mega Backdoor Roth: after-tax contributions to the Solo 401(k), converted to Roth either in-plan or by rolling them out to a Roth IRA.

A tax-free bucket that grows without the government touching the back end, with contribution basis you can reach when you need it.

You and your spouse both work in a practice with no other full-time employees? Each of you can put in up to $72,000, which means $144,000 a year flowing into Roth. Let that run five years and you’re sitting on a pool of penalty-free basis the IRS has no further claim on.

The 2026 wrinkle that tilts the math further: under SECURE Act 2.0, anyone who earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages from an employer last year and makes catch-up contributions to that employer’s plan must now make them as Roth.

The pre-tax catch-up option is gone for high earners. The government forced your hand on $8,000 of after-tax money. The Solo 401(k) lets you do the same thing deliberately, at scale, with $72,000.

Most physicians never open one because most content written for physicians stops at the employer 401(k). Setting one up takes an afternoon. Leaving it unopened while the income to fund it is already landing in your checking account is an information problem, and information problems are cheap to fix.

Season two introduced the Mind Flayer, a massive psychic entity that didn’t kill its victims outright. It possessed them. Its hosts acted against their own interests, convinced the whole time they were doing the right thing.

Physicians who botch the backdoor Roth rarely make obvious errors. The pro-rata rule has possessed them, and they don’t know it.

The backdoor Roth, done right: make a non-deductible contribution to a Traditional IRA ($7,500 in 2026, $8,600 if you’re 50 or older).

Confirm you hold zero pre-tax IRA dollars anywhere under your name, meaning no SEP-IRA, no rollover IRA, no forgotten traditional IRA from residency parked at a custodian you haven’t logged into since 2015. Convert. No income limit, no penalty.

The pro-rata rule wrecks the whole thing the moment pre-tax IRA dollars enter the picture. The IRS won’t let you convert only the after-tax portion. It blends every IRA you own into one pot and taxes the conversion proportionally. One forgotten pre-tax account turns a clean transaction into a partial tax event.

Career transitions are where physicians get exposed. Leaving academics and rolling the 403(b) into a rollover IRA.

Switching from group practice to 1099 work and parking the old 401(k) “temporarily.” Starting a new employed role months before the new plan opens its doors. Each one plants a pre-tax IRA landmine that quietly breaks the backdoor Roth for years.

The fix is to roll every pre-tax IRA dollar into the Solo 401(k) before running any backdoor conversion. The Solo 401(k) accepts incoming rollovers and clears the pro-rata calculation entirely. Close the gate before the Mind Flayer gets through, and the rest of the plan stays intact.

Season three ended on what felt like a win. The gate closed, Billy sacrificed himself, Hopper appeared to die, and Hawkins seemed to heal. Then season four arrived and Vecna had been working the whole time, building and feeding while everyone celebrated.

Physician burnout runs on the same script. Roughly half of physicians report burnout in Medscape’s annual surveys, which makes it less a risk factor than a coin flip. We acknowledge it at conferences, tape mental health flyers to the break room wall, and go back to the 60-hour weeks we call resilience.

A financial plan that only works if you never burn out is a prayer dressed up as a spreadsheet.

Enter the Rule of 55, one of the most useful provisions most physicians have never heard of. Separate from your employer (quit, retire, get laid off) during or after the calendar year you turn 55, and you can tap that employer’s 401(k) with no 10% penalty. Income tax still applies. The Demogorgon, though, is gone.

Dr. Addams burns out at 56. Dr. Smart at 57. Both qualify, provided they left the employer 401(k) alone.

That proviso is where the plan dies. Roll the 401(k) into an IRA before you separate and the Rule of 55 no longer applies, because the provision covers employer-sponsored plans only. Once the rollover clears, the window shuts for good.

A second move widens the window. Old 401(k) balances from prior employers that now sit in rollover IRAs can be consolidated into your current employer’s plan before you separate. Leave at 55 or later and the Rule of 55 covers the entire consolidated balance.

Some plans refuse incoming rollovers, so check with the administrator. For a physician who has collected balances across three hospitals and two practices, this can multiply the penalty-free pool available the day they decide they’re done.

If you’re in your early 50s and have so much as daydreamed about an early exit, stop rolling employer 401(k)s into IRAs. Leave them where they sit. You’re protecting an option.

Season four revealed that the Upside Down had a full tunnel network connecting distant points across Hawkins. It had existed all along. The kids who survived did so partly because they found the tunnels early enough to use them.

The Roth conversion ladder is the tunnel system.

In any low-income year, meaning any year your taxable income sits well below normal physician earnings, convert a chunk of pre-tax retirement money (Traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k)) to a Roth IRA. Pay income tax on the converted amount at that year’s rate.

Five years from January 1 of the conversion year, those dollars become accessible penalty-free, regardless of your age.

2026 is an unusually good year to run conversions. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the lower TCJA brackets permanent and locked in the 20% pass-through deduction for physicians with 1099 income. Conversions done at today’s rates cost less than they would have under the old sunset schedule, and Congress rarely improves things twice in a row.

Each conversion year runs its own five-year clock. Convert in 2026 and those dollars open January 1, 2031. Convert again in 2027 and that tranche opens January 1, 2032. Run conversions every year and you build a rolling pipeline, a new tranche unlocking every January.

The years physicians routinely waste: the sabbatical between positions (a six-month gap is one of the most valuable tax-planning windows of an entire career), parental leave with no 1099 income, the ramp-up after joining a new practice, the W-2 to 1099 switch, the wind-down to part-time locums before full retirement.

Size each conversion to fill the 22% or 24% bracket without tripping IRMAA surcharges on Medicare premiums. The tragedy is the physician who takes six months off, earns half a year’s income, files a clean return, and converts nothing. Five figures of lifetime tax savings, gone.

Ted Weschler’s story makes the point. One of Warren Buffett’s deputies at Berkshire Hathaway built a Traditional IRA worth north of $100 million, then converted the entire thing to Roth and paid a reported $28 million in tax to do it.

Every dollar in that account now grows and distributes tax-free. The mechanics are identical whether you’re converting $50,000 or $50 million.

In season three, the Russians ran a secret facility beneath Starcourt Mall, funded and staffed outside every rule the people upstairs lived by.

The Solo 401(k) participant loan is your private underground operation.

Borrow up to $50,000 or 50% of the account balance, whichever is less, at any time and for any reason. There’s no credit check or underwriter, and no waiting period. You approve your own loan. The rate runs around prime, and every dollar of interest flows back into your own account, so you’re paying yourself.

The physician use cases are specific: a down payment on a rental property during a cash-lean year, bridge funding during a practice startup before revenue normalizes, or covering a surprise expense without selling appreciated holdings in a taxable account and triggering capital gains.

Employer 401(k) loans carry a risk the Solo 401(k) deletes. Leave your job with an outstanding employer loan and the full balance comes due by your tax filing deadline; fail to repay and the IRS reclassifies it as a distribution, taxable plus the 10% penalty. You cannot fire yourself from your own plan. That risk doesn’t exist here.

The series finale didn’t end through brute force. It ended because the characters finally understood the rules of the dimension well enough to find exits nobody had used.

SECURE Act 2.0 added penalty exceptions that read, with only slight exaggeration, like admission criteria. Physicians should recognize them on sight. Few have seen them applied to their own accounts.

The terminal illness provision stops physicians cold the first time they hear it. If a physician certifies that a patient’s condition is expected to result in death within 84 months, that patient can tap any retirement account penalty-free, with optional repayment within three years if the patient recovers. You write this certification regularly. It applies to your own accounts in exactly the same way, and almost no one knows it.

The claim that retirement accounts lock money away is far less true than it was five years ago. These accounts now double as a safety net. A physician who dismisses them as untouchable before 59½ is running on stale data.

In the finale, Hopper and Murray prepared to detonate a bomb to destroy the machine opening the gate. Bluntest instrument available, and at that moment, exactly the right tool.

IRS Rule 72(t) is a bomb. Understand what you’re detonating before you light the fuse.

Under 72(t), you commit to Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP) from your IRA or 401(k), penalty-free at any age, for the longer of five years or until you turn 59½. Start at 40 and you’re locked in for nearly two decades. Start at 57 and you serve five years.

Three calculation methods exist: Required Minimum Distribution (smallest payments, recalculated annually), Fixed Amortization (highest payments, fixed), and Fixed Annuitization (similar to amortization, also fixed). Most people pick fixed amortization.

The catch is severe. Break the schedule in any way, a missed payment, a modification, a single deviation, and the IRS retroactively applies the 10% penalty to every distribution already taken, plus interest. Seven years into a schedule, that bill can be devastating.

One structural move contains the damage. Before starting, split the IRA into two accounts. Apply 72(t) only to the smaller one, sized to produce exactly the income you need, and leave the larger account untouched. An error then costs you the small account’s history, while the bulk of your savings keeps compounding.

72(t) fits the physician who retires well before 55 with a large IRA and needs predictable income before the Roth ladder finishes building. Right tool for a narrow situation, and a poor first resort.

Good diagnosticians don’t commit to one diagnosis before ruling out the alternatives. They build a differential and sequence the workup around probability, available data, and the patient in front of them.

These seven strategies work the same way. No single provision fits every physician, and the right sequence depends on age, income type, career stage, and how close the exit door really is.

Any age, any 1099 income at all: open a Solo 401(k) and max the Mega Backdoor Roth at $72,000. Over 50 and high-earning? Your catch-up contributions have to be Roth anyway, so lean in at scale.

Any low-income year, whether sabbatical, parental leave, practice transition, or a W-2 to 1099 switch: run a Roth conversion. The OBBBA rate environment favors you. Fill the lower brackets and start the five-year clock.

Early 50s and still employed somewhere: stop rolling old 401(k)s into IRAs. Consolidate into the current employer plan and protect the Rule of 55 window.

55 or older and separating from an employer: tap the 401(k) under the Rule of 55 before considering any rollover. That window, once lost, does not reopen.

Pre-55 retirement with a large IRA: the Roth conversion ladder is the primary engine, with 72(t) as backup if the ladder can’t bridge the early years.

Any career stage: learn the SECURE Act 2.0 exceptions. Terminal illness certification, disability, unreimbursed medical expenses. The provisions exist, they’re legal, and they apply to you. Read them before you need them.

The most unsettling reveal in Stranger Things wasn’t the Mind Flayer or the Demogorgon or the Russians under the mall. It was Vecna, one entity pulling strings from the beginning, before anyone knew there were strings.

By the time the kids understood what they were up against, years had passed, and some of them never fully recovered.

Dr. Addams didn’t fail because he earned too little. He failed because no one showed him the exits, and by the time he needed one it was too late to build it. His plan was designed for Dr. Lucky, the physician who never burns out, earns steadily into his mid-60s, and retires on schedule into a fully funded life. Dr. Lucky is a statistical outlier. Dr. Addams and Dr. Smart are the population.

The tax code wasn’t written with physicians in mind, and it doesn’t discriminate against them either. The rules work the same whether you trained at a top academic center or a community program, whether you’re W-2 or 1099, whether you’re planning an exit in three years or still convinced you’ll never burn out.

Right now, in November 2026, you have time. The 2026 tax year is still open. Conversions completed before December 31 start a clock that opens January 1, 2031. Solo 401(k) contributions for 2026 can be made up to your business tax filing deadline. The IRS is expected to announce 2027 limits any day now, with Milliman and Mercer both projecting $25,500 for 401(k) deferrals and $75,000 for total Solo 401(k) contributions. More room to build.

April 15, 2027 arrives the same way every year. The gate doesn’t wait for you to feel ready.

The physicians who retire on their own timeline read the map before they needed it. They found Eleven, ran conversions during the low-income years everyone else treated as inconveniences, stopped rolling accounts into IRAs at 52, and walked out of their last job with options.

The Upside Down has exits. All seven have been there the whole time.

Your December Check-Writing Ritual Needs an Upgrade

Most physicians give the same way every year: a few checks go out after Christmas, and the CPA sorts it out in March.

That habit got expensive in 2026. Itemizers now lose the deduction on the first 0.5% of AGI they give away, and top-bracket donors see the tax benefit capped at 35%. The write-off shrinks unless you change how the money leaves your account before December 31.

Give appreciated stock in place of cash and the capital gain never shows up on your return. Bunch two or three years of donations into a donor-advised fund and you clear the floor once. Past 70½? Send the gift straight from your IRA.

Which move fits depends on whether you’ll itemize this year.
