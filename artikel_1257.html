Scotland's World Cup prize must not become a missed opportunity

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Published: 15:23 EST, 23 November 2026 | Updated: 15:36 EST, 23 November 2026

Confirmation that Scotland will co-host the 2035 FIFA Women’s World Cup is an undoubted boost for the game in this country.

Staging a global showpiece with England, Wales and Northern Ireland is indeed a potential game-changer.

The joint bid projected that the competition would attract 3.5billion viewers worldwide. And that 4.5m tickets would be sold across the host nations.

But the key for Scotland will be the impact it has on girls taking up the sport, supporters watching it and professionals playing it north of the Border.

Scotland captain Caroline Weir has already described it as a ‘pivotal’ moment, a ‘real opportunity’ to drive momentum and reach another level, domestically and internationally.

She’s right. It is an opportunity, of the once-in-a-lifetime variety, but a word of caution is in order. Opportunities are not enough in themselves to be transformative.

They have to be embraced, exploited and squeezed for all they are worth so that every last drop of their potential is properly fulfilled.

Star player Caroline Weir believes co-hosting the World Cup could be pivotal for women's football in Scotland

It would be criminal if the powers-that-be got complacent about this priceless gift and made assumptions about its long-term legacy.

More players, more fans and more media coverage would be hugely exciting, but it will all be wasted if not supported by facilities, coaches and the safeguarding that goes with it.

It has happened before. Interest in athletics rocketed after Glasgow 2014, but a poor infrastructure, which continued to rely on volunteers, prevented a grassroots boom.

And how many times have we heard Judy Murray complain that Scottish tennis squandered the opportunity presented to it by her son, Andy?

Scotland’s women played at the World Cup in 2019. Their game has grown since then, but the national side have not been to another major tournament and the domestic product is still a shadow of its English counterpart.

Only a handful of Scottish teams have full-time players. The wages are way behind those on offer down south. And professional men’s clubs could do much, much more to develop the sport. It’s no wonder the Scottish league can’t keep its better players.

The opposite is true in England, where the success of the national team is often attributed to the level of investment by the FA and the backing of sponsors across the country.

The good news is that Scotland have made a start on that front with something called Project 2035, which aims to transform girls' and women’s football over the next decade.

The Scottish government has stumped up an initial £250,000 to help with the 10-year plan, which outlined a number of key objectives earlier this month.

They want to grow participation at grassroots level, increase the number of women working in football - as coaches, leaders and decision-makers - and quadruple the number of fans. The hope also is that Scotland will progress beyond the group stage of at least one World Cup by 2035.

Having those ambitions is one thing; making good on them is quite another. The government, the league, clubs and the ruling body: all should be thrilled by this opportunity but they should also feel the pressure that it brings. For it is their responsibility, their duty, to make the most of it.
