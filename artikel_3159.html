Income loss is stressful; the first steps involve cutting costs and focusing on essentials.

A Redditor recently shared that they were struggling to decide which financial steps to take first after a job loss.

Should they withdraw money from retirement savings to have more cash on hand — but pay an early withdrawal penalty? Or is it better to rely on the severance payout to handle expenses?

Many users responded with good ideas: File for unemployment, try to avoid tapping into retirement savings, and cut expenses as much as possible.

For more ideas, I asked three financial experts how to manage money in the immediate aftermath of job loss.

While it’s tempting to make quick decisions about tapping into a retirement fund or selling a house, it’s better to wait on any big moves, says Parker Wine, retirement planning advisor at the financial firm Daniel A. White & Associates in Lewes, Delaware.

That’s because you need time to digest the situation and weigh the pros and cons of different options, he says. “People can get impulsive and panic, and it’s usually a bad thing to make decisions when you’re panicking,” he says.

Take your time, get second opinions and think through all of your options first, Wine suggests.

If you’re fortunate enough to receive a severance package, wait to sign it until you’ve reviewed it carefully and understand all of the details, says John Rafferty, partner and investment advisor representative at Solomon Financial in Carmel, Indiana.

For example, make sure you know about your health insurance options, such as whether you’ll receive insurance through COBRA or need to take out a new policy in the health insurance marketplace.

Filing for unemployment and checking into any other available workplace benefits — such as help with your new job search — is also an important part of understanding what support to expect in the weeks and months ahead, Rafferty adds.

Creating a bare-bones budget stripped of “extras” like restaurant meals or travel can help the severance payment last as long as possible, Rafferty says.

“Now that you don’t have income coming in, ask yourself, ‘How much can I spend and where?’”

You might be able to cut back on entertainment costs, coffee runs and clothing purchases, for example.

At the same time, it’s important to prioritize the essentials like mortgage payments, rent, utilities and food. Rafferty recommends calling your mortgage company, utilities provider and any other lenders to ask if they have a hardship program for those experiencing financial challenges.

Opting in could give you more time to pay your bills or temporarily lower payments.

It’s hard to stick to a budget that eliminates all entertainment, so Rafferty suggests finding enjoyable activities that come with a low price tag. Hiking, discovering a new park and participating in free community events are all ways to enjoy downtime on a budget.

“Having that release is mentally a big thing so you can stay prepared for the job search,” he says. “Otherwise, it’s easy to get down and feel negative, and we need things to help us feel positive.”

Similarly, connecting with friends and family during this time is crucial, says Laura Adams, accredited financial counselor and financial coach in Wilmington, North Carolina.

People often feel a sense of shame or embarrassment after job loss, and Adams encourages people to talk about their feelings and ask for help if needed. “Financial shame can cause us to make emotional rather than confident financial decisions,” she says.

Starting some kind of side hustle to bring in extra cash can also help bridge the gap between the job loss and your next job, Wine says. Substitute teaching, working at a local public golf course and driving for a rideshare service are all options he’s seen people try.

Doing something new also helps stave off boredom while unemployed, but he cautions that you’ll still owe taxes on any side income you bring in, so plan accordingly.

All three financial experts also emphasized that a job loss is easier to handle with an emergency savings fund you can turn to, and suggested building one up — ideally three to six months’ worth of expenses. But starting small helps, too — while employed, if possible.

The Redditor who posted the question mentioned that, like many Americans, they lacked emergency savings. This added urgency to the question of what to prioritize.
