Expressly Christian Messages by Secretary of Agriculture to Employees Violated Establishment Clause,

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Judge Trina Thompson (N.D. Cal.) today in Nat'l Federation of Federal Employees v. U.S. Dep't of Agriculture:

The Court ORDERS that the Secretary must recognize certain boundaries when communicating religious messages. She "[m]ust stop if a colleague asks [her] to stop," she "[c]annot create a hostile work environment through persistent unwelcome advances," and she "[c]annot use official authority to pressure subordinates." USDA Secretary's Memorandum 1078-019. She may "[d]iscuss [her] religious beliefs with interested colleagues," "[s]hare why [her] faith is meaningful to [her]," "[p]olitely invite coworkers to religious services or events," "[e]xpress religious viewpoints during appropriate conversations." This Court expresses no view whatsoever on what the Secretary can and cannot do in her personal capacity….

On July 4, 2025, Secretary [of Agriculture Brooke] Rollins sent an email from her official email account celebrating Independence Day. The message was sent to all USDA employees. Her message concluded with a written statement, "May God continue to protect the United States of America and may His favor shine over all her land." On November 26, 2025, Rollins sent a Thanksgiving message from her official email account to all USDA employees, attributing the first Thanksgiving in part to "gratitude towards a loving God."

The complaint identifies two subsequent communications as expressly promoting Christianity. On December 23, 2025, Rollins emailed USDA employees a Christmas message containing a video in which she stated that "The spirit of generosity flows from the very first Christmas when God gave us the greatest gift possible, the gift of his Son and our Savior Jesus Christ, who came to free us from our sins and open the door to eternal life. This is the reason for the season…."

On April 5, 2026, she sent an Easter email addressed to "All USDA Employees" which read:

Today we celebrate the greatest story ever told, the foundation of our faith, and the abiding hope of all mankind.

From the foot of the Cross on Good Friday to the stone rolled away from the now empty tomb, sin has been destroyed. Jesus has been raised from the dead. And God has granted each of us victory and new life. And where there is life — risen life — there is hope.

No matter the very real trials and hardships we face, fear and sin and death do not get the last word. Because on Easter morning, "Hell took a body, and discovered God. It took earth, and encountered Heaven. It took what it saw, and was overcome by what it did not see." Now that is reason to rejoice!

And so, like the very first disciples to encounter our risen Lord in the Upper Room almost two thousand years ago, this Easter let us too be alive with hope, full of Paschal joy, and confident in the mission each of us has been called for.

Please know how amazed and grateful I am for the hard work each of you do to support our shared mission here at USDA. I hope you and your loved ones have a truly blessed and happy Easter. May God continue to bless you, your families, and our exceptional country, One Nation, Under God.

At the top of the email, Secretary Rollins included an illustration of a stone rolled away from Jesus' tomb with the words "Christ is Risen" written above the illustration.

Plaintiffs allege that Rollins has not sent comparable messages acknowledging other religions' holidays. They contrast her communications with USDA's November 20, 2025 guidance on religious expression, which provides that officials "[c]annot use official authority to pressure subordinates" regarding religious beliefs and "[m]ust stop if a colleague asks [them] to stop." They further allege that Rollins defended the Easter message publicly and is likely to send similar communications marking future Christian holidays.

Each individual plaintiff received and read the challenged messages. Plaintiffs allege that they are expected to read communications from the Secretary because those communications may contain consequential workplace information. Other messages have addressed agency reorganization, employee relocation, changes to USDA's civil rights program, shutdown planning, and the Deferred Resignation Program; holiday messages have historically included administrative-leave announcements.

The individual plaintiffs allege that the messages convey a preference for employees who share the Secretary's Christian beliefs, make them feel excluded, and pressure them to conceal their own beliefs or appear to share hers. They fear workplace disfavor and retaliation for objecting; the complaint also alleges that supervisors have warned that objections would result in "trouble." Plaintiff Poopatanapong alleges that she no longer feels safe discussing a Buddhist holiday she celebrates; Roberts, an atheist, feels silenced; and Wolfe, a Pagan, feels pressure to appear Christian to advance professionally. alleges that its USDA members likewise received the messages, felt compelled to read them, and experienced pressure to accept the Secretary's beliefs while withholding their own.

The court held that plaintiffs' Establishment Clause were likely violated:

The Establishment Clause protects religious freedom by "command[ing] a separation of church and state." Kennedy v. Bremerton Sch. Dist., 597 U.S. 507, 558–59 (2022) (citing Cutter v. Wilkinson, 544 U.S. 709, 719 (2005)). [Note that this quote is actually from Justice Sotomayor's dissent in Kennedy, though it appears in the Cutter majority. -EV] At its core, this means forbidding "sponsorship, financial support, and active involvement of the sovereign in religious activity." As the Supreme Court acknowledged in Kennedy, the Establishment Clause does not "'compel the government to purge from the public sphere' anything an objective observer could reasonably infer endorses or 'partakes of the religious.'" However, courts must be cautious to ensure that "what might begin as a tolerant expression of religious views" does not "end in a policy to indoctrinate and coerce."

To determine whether government action violates the Establishment Clause, the Court must "focus[ ] on original meaning and history" of the First Amendment. That is, this Court's must be guided by "historical practices and understandings" of the relationship between government and religion in America. Pursuant to this approach, any practice that was "accepted by the Framers and has withstood the critical scrutiny of time and political change" does not violate the Establishment Clause. The analysis does not end merely because a practice has been observed in the past. The history and tradition test requires this Court to look to the "uninterrupted practice" of law in our nation's traditions.

Traditionally, the hallmark of government conduct violative of the Establishment Clause is the presence of some form of coercion upon its citizens.

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, the two Founders most instrumental in the drafting and ratification of the Religion Clauses of the First Amendment, articulated a founding-era concern broader than direct, compelled sectarian practices. Madison, for example, expressed concern that even instances of governmental "recommendation[]" concerning religion "naturally terminate[] in a conformity to the creed of the majority []." Jefferson expressed concern about indirect forms of coercion such as conduct which might invite "some degree of proscription perhaps in public opinion." Most fitting, in his "Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments" drafted in 1785, Madison questioned the ends sought by the promotion of a sectarian underpinning to our country's political foundation: "Who does not see that the same authority which can establish Christianity, in exclusion of all other Religions, may establish with the same ease any particular sect of Christians, in exclusion of all other Sects?" His guidance, contained in the same writings: "Such a Government will be best supported by protecting every Citizen in the enjoyment of his Religion with the same equal hand which protects his person and his property; by neither invading the equal rights of any Sect, nor suffering any Sect to invade those of another." The Founders may have lacked the foresight to account for contemporary technological advances, but the principles embedded in our First Amendment ring as true today. As difficult as the task may be, below, this Court faithfully applies the founding-era's understanding of the Establishment Clause to the modern-day circumstances giving rise to this litigation….

After reviewing and weighing the Establishment Clause jurisprudence, the Court finds that Secretary Rollins appears to exploit her position of authority to convey a religious message beyond mere holiday wishes and amounting to coercion. Here, three factors inform the Court's conclusion that the Secretary's conduct was coercive: (1) her position of authority, (2) the use of her official email to send messages to all USDA employees, and (3) the alienating language contained in the emails to employees who did not share her religious tenets. [Detailed discussion of factors 1 and 2 omitted for space reasons. -EV]

It makes no difference that the emails did not single out dissidents, disparage those who did not share the Secretary's beliefs, or indicate work-related decisions would be influenced by an employees' reaction to the emails. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that "immense social pressure" can result in a finding of coercion. Such pressure is demonstrated here…. While social pressure alone is not state coercion because "mature adults … presumably are not readily susceptible to religious indoctrination or peer pressure[,]" the totality of the circumstances here demonstrates such immense pressure to amount to coercion….

The Court does not purport to regulate the religiosity of the Secretary—nor could it. "A relentless and all-pervasive attempt to exclude religion from every aspect of public life could itself become inconsistent with the Constitution." Nor does this Court generally condemn the expression of religious sentiments. Instead, this Court requires the Secretary to observe her own directives. USDA Secretary's Memorandum 1078-019. The April 5, 2026, and December 23, 2025, messages go too far. They suggest acquiescence that amounts to coercive pressure; a pressure of conformity sufficient to raise an Establishment Clause violation….
