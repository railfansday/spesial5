The S&P 500 Just Ended a Run of 155 Trading Days Above Its 200-Day Average. Here's What Followed 34 Similar Runs Since 1950.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The index just ended a 155-session run above that line. What came after the 34 earlier runs of 150-plus sessions?

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 18, 2026 at 11:04PM EST

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.23%) finished Tuesday, Nov. 17, at 7,340.53. That left it a few points under its 200-day moving average (the average of its past 200 daily closes, or around nine and a half months of trading), which was near 7,345.

It was the index's first close below that line since April 7. The next day, the S&P 500 began a run of 155 straight sessions above its average, posting a record close of 7,818.93 on Oct. 6 along the way.

Its worst day since that high was Nov. 5, when it dropped over 4% as chipmakers and other stocks linked to artificial intelligence (AI) sold off. As I write, the index is roughly 6% below its record. That still puts it about 7% above where it closed 2025.

With tech stocks leading the slide, a break like this may look like the market's longer-term trend turning down. Does it normally bring more trouble?

I went through the index's history since 1950 and found 34 earlier times a run of at least 150 sessions above the 200-day average ended. Three months later, the index was up in 26 of them.

My numbers come from Yahoo Finance's record of the index's daily closes since January 1950. These cover only price changes, not dividends. For every day, I averaged the 200 most recent closes, then counted how many sessions in a row the index finished above its average.

The longest run, at 628 sessions, ended in May 1956. Notably, four of the last six breaks happened this decade on Feb. 27, 2020, Jan. 21, 2022, March 10, 2025, and March 19 this year.

After the March break, the index dropped about 4% further in the next seven sessions, closed back above its average on April 8, and was around 14% higher by mid-June.

In half the 34 cases, the index closed back above its 200-day average within five trading days, and in 28 of them, it did so within 20 sessions (about four weeks). But the 1987 break was the big outlier -- the index took 161 sessions to get back above its average.

Across all 34 breaks, the index climbed over the next three months 76% of the time, with a median gain of around 4%. By comparison, a three-month stretch from any random trading day since 1950 finished with a gain in about two out of three cases (67%), with a median gain closer to 3%. In other words, the months after a break have arguably been a bit better than an ordinary stretch.

Still, the exceptions were rough. The worst came after April 4, 1962, when the index dropped about 17% over the next three months.

Another break came on Oct. 15, 1987, two sessions before the crash of Oct. 19, when the index dropped about 20% in one day. Three months after that break, it was still down roughly 15%.

The year after a break looks even more like an ordinary year. Of the 33 breaks that are at least a year old, the index ended the next 12 months up in 23, or around 70%, and the median gain was about 9%. In a normal 12-month period since 1950, it's climbed about 75% of the time, with a median gain of about 11%.

Of course, a break didn't always mean a quiet year. In 11 of the 33 cases, the index slid another 10% or more below its break-day close at some point within 12 months. That set includes August 2007, when the index was down around 13% a year later.

But it also includes February 2020 and March 2025. Both times, the index fell further within weeks, by around 25% through March 23, 2020, and about 11% through April 8, 2025. And both times, it ended the next 12 months higher, up about 28% and 21%, respectively.

So, should a long-term holder of a fund tracking the S&P 500 sell after a break like this? I don't think so.

In most of these cases, selling would've meant missing gains in the months that followed.

And on the day of each break, the ones that turned into deeper drops didn't look very different from the rest. At the median, the index stood around 8% under its high of the previous year in those cases, against about 7% in the others.

I'd keep holding, though I think the index could easily fall more from here.
