Michael Burry's Puts on the Nasdaq-100 Run to April 2027. I'm Not Following Him.

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

The Big Short investor has bet almost all his short book on a tech top within months. Should Nasdaq-100 fund holders hedge too?

Michael Burry is betting that one of the market's best-known tech indexes has further to fall. In an Oct. 11 post on his Substack, the investor famous for The Big Short said almost all his bets against the market were now put options on two things.

One is the Nasdaq-100 index, with contracts expiring in April 2027. The other is the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH -2.99%), with contracts expiring in June 2027. A put gains value as the asset under it drops.

The index has gone his way since then. The Invesco QQQ Trust (QQQ -1.20%), the exchange-traded fund that tracks the Nasdaq-100, trades around $680 as I write this, about 10% under the peak it reached on Oct. 6. And on Nov. 5 alone, the Nasdaq-100 sank 6.4%.

Should investors who own the fund protect themselves as Burry has? I don't think so, and my reasons have more to do with his calendar than with his take on tech stocks.

Michael Burry. Image source: Getty Images.

Burry has argued that companies are borrowing and spending too much to build data centers for AI. And in October, he said he thought the market might be anywhere from zero to nine months away from a major top. Starting from his October post, that window runs into July 2027 -- past the April expiration of his Nasdaq-100 puts and the June expiration of his chip-fund puts.

That gap matters, because a put that expires before the drop comes can expire worthless. In September, Burry closed out his December 2026 puts on Nvidia to avoid losing more of their value to time decay (the steady drain an option suffers as expiration nears).

He's also been trading around the bet. On Nov. 5, the chip fund dropped 11%, and Burry sold 25% of his puts on it. He said the puts he kept were still a big position and that he was still bearish. Later, on Nov. 12, he said he'd doubled his Nasdaq-100 put position and added back to his chip-fund puts.

In other words, Burry isn't just holding insurance. He's making a string of timing calls.

Of course, Burry's two bets overlap more than it may seem. The 10 largest holdings in QQQ make up around 46% of its assets. And four chipmakers -- Nvidia, Advanced Micro Devices, Micron Technology, and Broadcom -- account for about 18% by themselves.

A chip sell-off like the one on Nov. 5 can hit the fund hard. But a falling price isn't the same as falling profits.

Showing how the fund's biggest companies are still growing, Apple (AAPL +0.39%), its largest holding, said its earnings per share rose 14% year over year in its fiscal 2026 fourth quarter (the three months ending Sept. 26), with a slight boost from tariff refunds. Microsoft (MSFT +0.17%), its third-largest holding, grew earnings per share on a non-GAAP (adjusted) basis by 18% year over year in its fiscal 2027 first quarter (the three months ending Sept. 30). That adjusted number leaves out gains and losses on the software giant's OpenAI stake. Growth is slowing at both companies, though. Apple's earnings per share had gained 29% in its fiscal third quarter. And Microsoft's adjusted earnings per share had risen 23% in the quarter before and 22% for fiscal 2026, so 18% was a step down.

But earnings of the fund's two biggest non-chip companies are growing at double-digit rates while its price has fallen around 10% from the Oct. 6 peak. In other words, QQQ's valuation is arguably lower relative to what its largest companies earn than it was a bit over a month ago.

For a long-term holder, buying puts like Burry's means paying for insurance that runs out. If the index holds up through April, the puts might expire worthless, and their cost comes right out of the holder's returns.

And if a big fall does arrive, the holder still has to choose when to sell the puts. That's the same sort of timing call Burry has been making.

Someone planning to hold a Nasdaq-100 fund for the next decade probably doesn't need to be right about April 2027.

In the end, Burry might be right that tech stocks have further to fall, and a day like Nov. 5 could come again before his puts expire. But his puts are a bet on timing, while a fund holder's result mainly depends on whether the companies in the fund keep growing their earnings. Right now, the biggest ones are.

For investors who already own a Nasdaq-100 fund, I'd keep holding it and skip the puts, even if that means riding out a few more tough days.
