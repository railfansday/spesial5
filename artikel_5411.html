The housing cliff is here.

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A mortgage CEO says the market dropped sharply in October, with lock volume down 51% YoY. MBA applications fell 42%, and NAR pending home sales hit a record low, while Fannie, Freddie and FHA report strong capital and earnings.   AI Summary

Something is happening in the American housing market that should be setting off alarm bells in Washington. Yet the response from policymakers has been either remarkably misguided or mute.

Earlier this year, our mortgage company was setting production records, in spite of the poor pace of existing home sales. We’ve grown considerably over the past several years, expanding our reach, reducing our costs to manufacture and investing heavily in technology.

Then the market fell off a cliff. Not a seasonal slowdown. Not an understandable contraction. A cliff.

In October, our lock volume declined 51% compared to October 2025. Think about that. A company that was setting records this spring, with a larger and more efficient operating platform than a year ago, experienced a 51% year-over-year collapse in locked production.

Trust me, we’re not alone. The fraternity of IMBs may be fiercely competitive, but it also understands that a falling tide lowers all boats.

The Mortgage Bankers Association’s weekly Mortgage Applications Index is down 42% year over year as of October 30. Mortgage Capital Trading (MCT) reported September mortgage locks down 27.25% year over year.

The National Association of Realtors (NAR) reported September pending home sales declining 11.3% year over year, with its Pending Home Sales Index plunging to 66.0, the lowest reading in the index’s entire history, dating back to 2001.

But here’s the problem. Both MCT’s and NAR’s figures reflect September activity.

By the time those reports reach policymakers, lenders have already experienced October’s deterioration and are projecting November.

If any other industry of this magnitude were experiencing a contraction this severe, it would dominate headlines. Legislators would be demanding answers. Industry leaders would be called to Washington. A task force would be formed, and we surely wouldn’t be talking about “considering” how to cancel mortgage insurance more proactively for loans that haven’t even closed yet. I’ve been originating for 21 years. You know how many times I’ve had a client reach out because they were having difficulty canceling PMI? Zero.

The warning signs come before the closings. If you’re looking at existing home sales or September’s data, you’re sitting comfortably in the caboose while the front of the train has gone over the edge.

Housing doesn’t begin at closing. It begins with consumer interest, credit inquiries, mortgage applications, rate locks and purchase contracts. These are our leading indicators. And they’re flashing red. When apps disappear, locks follow. When locks disappear, closings follow. And when closings disappear, the financial consequences spread across an enormous industry.

Downsizing has already begun. Again.  These decisions are painful. But responsible leaders must act, not hope. Mortgage lenders cannot continue carrying rising healthcare costs, salaries and operating expenses into a production decline this severe, particularly as we enter the fourth and first quarters, our slowest seasons. We’ve been in a housing recession for four consecutive years. But what’s happening now is worse.

Here’s the contradiction I find most troubling.

While the mortgage industry contracts, the institutions charged with supporting housing finance are reporting extraordinary financial results. Fannie Mae earned $4.2 billion in the third quarter, bringing its net worth to $119.6 billion. Freddie Mac earned another $3.2 billion, with net worth reaching $81 billion.

That’s $7.4 billion in quarterly profits and more than $200 billion in combined net worth. And what is the Director saying? He’s bragging about it. Bragging.

Meanwhile, FHA’s most recent annual report showed its Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund holding $188.9 billion in capital, with an 11.47% capital ratio, more than five times the statutory minimum. FHA has never been as richly capitalized as it is today.

Strong balance sheets are important. Taxpayers deserve protection. Nobody is suggesting we abandon prudent risk management. But how do we reconcile this financial strength with today’s housing market?

Consider this: Fannie Mae reported that more than 70% of its third-quarter guarantee-fee income came from loans and related securities issued in 2022 or earlier.

The agencies can continue generating enormous earnings from their existing portfolios even as new mortgage activity contracts. That’s an explanation for their profitability. It is not a justification for ignoring the affordability crisis.

In 2024, Fannie and Freddie collected an average of 65.2 basis points in g-fees on new single-family acquisitions.

FHFA should be evaluating those fees against actual credit risk, capital requirements and the substantial recurring earnings generated by existing guarantees. Even modest, responsibly calibrated reductions could lower borrowing costs if passed through to consumers.

Congress should repeal the 10-basis-point guarantee-fee surcharge originally imposed in 2011 to fund an unrelated payroll tax extension. If you’ve gotten a conforming loan since 2012 and you still have it, 10 basis points of your rate is going to pay for a 2-month payroll tax cut that happened 14 years ago. By the way, the CBO said the cut would cost $30 billion. We’ve repaid $56 billion.

These aren’t silver bullets. But they’re tangible, measurable actions that can reduce costs without abandoning responsible risk management.

I’m not suggesting that government agencies should sacrifice financial stability to rescue mortgage companies. I’m suggesting something far more fundamental.

The success of America’s housing finance system cannot be measured solely by the profitability of the institutions that support it. It must also be measured by whether American families can afford to participate in it.

Right now, those two measurements are telling very different stories.

Washington needs to recognize what’s happening in real time, engage directly with industry leaders and take meaningful steps to improve affordability.

Because every month spent admiring backward-looking financial results is another month that businesses must downsize, housing transactions disappear and families are priced out of homeownership.

The housing cliff isn’t something we’re approaching. We’re already falling off it. And the time to act is now.
