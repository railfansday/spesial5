Luc Besson Jokes That He Could Make the Next ‘Terminator,’ but Only if the Terminator ‘Sat Down and Said He Missed His Mom’

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Luc Besson rejects the idea that he reinvented action cinema. He simply used to have the freedom to experiment.

“When you don’t have a lot of experience, you have all this freedom. You aren’t controlled by big studios and 50 people telling you why you can’t do something because it costs too much. For about 20 years now, people with money have taken control of cinema. “When Modigliani wanted to paint women with long necks and black eyes, that’s what he did. We need to take this freedom back because we are simply killing art. We’re turning it into something that can be sold, like yogurt or socks. There are four crucial things in the lives of men and women: Water, air, love and art. Without them, we’re dead.”

He added, “Now, we have replaced everything with money. And it’s going to kill us all.”

Besson was speaking at Torino Film Festival, where he received the Stella delle Mole award for lifetime achievement. Earlier that day, he deadpanned, “I saw the list of all the people who have received it, and I didn’t understand why I hadn’t received it too. I’m not used to receiving awards in my own country, so I’m happy to receive one abroad.”

Celebrated for “The Big Blue,” “Léon: The Professional,” “The Fifth Element” and “Lucy,” Besson was honest about his preferences.

“Some directors are very good at making films about their neighbors – I don’t know how to do that. I like extraordinary stories. I make films to wander, to disappear and explore other worlds. I don’t want to tell the story of my neighbor. I want to tell the story of a guy who drives a taxi in the 23rd century,” he said, referencing Bruce Willis’ role in “The Fifth Element.”

“These characters are always a little bit broken by society, always on the margins. Also, I like to explore the strength of women and the weakness of men. If I was asked to make another ‘Terminator,’ I’m not interested. I like Schwarzenegger a lot, but I’d only make that film if the Terminator sat down and said he missed his mom.”

Besson singled out some of the actors he has enjoyed working with the most, from Gary Oldman to ex-spouse Milla Jovovich and star of “Dogman” Caleb Landry Jones.

“He’s the genius of the decade. He’s a very simple, very sober actor. He doesn’t go out, doesn’t do anything. But when he’s on set, it’s just wonderful. He’ll try everything,” he revealed.

During the presser, Besson said, “If I could make all my films with [Caleb], that would be fantastic.”

“As for choosing actors in general, imagine you have to cross the Atlantic in a small boat with just one other person. Choose your partner well, or it will be really hard. Now, I work with actors who are, above all, good people. I don’t want a star surrounded by 15 people.”

Currently finishing an animated project that he produced and which will be released next year, Besson continues to collaborate with other directors.

“If someone else could do it as well or better than me, I don’t need to do it myself. I couldn’t have done ‘Taken’ any better. But ‘The Big Blue’ was different. My parents were divers and I spent 15 years studying dolphins, so I wasn’t going to leave that to anyone else. ‘Dogman’ was the same thing. It was a story I was afraid someone else would ruin,” he recalled.

“Transporter” was another hit for him as a producer.

“One day, an agent I knew introduced me to a young actor, and I had a drink with Jason Statham. He seemed photogenic. I told him that if I had an idea, I’d call him. I got on a plane and came up with the idea,” he said.

“We wrote it in a month and financed it in three. Louis Leterrier [who directed with Corey Yuen] was an assistant who wanted to start directing. I asked: ‘Are you ready?’ Hollywood doesn’t know how to do it anymore.”

Recalling his beginnings, he said, “We were a group of young people who couldn’t get films made because the big studios controlled everything. So we said: ‘Fine, we’ll make them ourselves.’ Suddenly, there were no rules. For 30 years, we did whatever we wanted.”

“Unfortunately, it’s getting harder. That’s a real shame because the films haven’t exactly improved, that’s for sure.”

Reportedly working on a new sci-fi project, Besson still advocates the kind of cinema that allows people to escape reality.

“At 14 or 15, I lived far from the city. I could see cows outside of my window, and I felt a strong urge to get away, so I started writing about a taxi pulling up outside my house and taking me off into space. I started writing ‘The Fifth Element’ at 16 because it was my personal way of escaping.”

“Imagination is a muscle: the more you train it, the stronger it gets. It lets you explore worlds that don’t exist, which is wonderful, because what does exist isn’t that great. Especially right now.”
