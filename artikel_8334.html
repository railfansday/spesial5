Face Plant on Unlucky Day 13 — John Muir Trail Day 13

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Last night my card-playing neighbors stayed up talking.  Even after they fell asleep, I somehow stayed awake despite being bone tired.  Finally, I fell asleep, woke up, peed, slept, woke, peed, slept, woke, peed.

Eventually it got cold enough to where I had to use my sleeping bag liner for the first time in several days.  Then I dreamed I was back in high school, dealing with some obnoxious kid who was trying to blackmail me, when I opened my eyes and it was light.

Not just a little gleam on the horizon either, it was full-on 6:30 a.m. summer-in-the-mountains light.  For me that is late to get up, because I am so slow.  I got up and started on breakfast and the rest of the morning routine right away.

At a certain point, Sally came along and I was close enough to the trail to spot her.  She had stopped to camp last evening at a much lower elevation, but she obviously started her hiking day well before I did.

We chatted and she agreed that I could use pictures and descriptions of her in my blog.  When someone appears, especially in pictures, I always try to get their permission in that way.

After she left, I went down into a rock-lined gully to use the cathole I had dug the night before.  While I was down there, I noticed a couple of bottles wedged into the rock out of sight.   It seemed someone had been drinking and didn’t want to take the bottles home.  My initial impulse was to pack out the bottles.  Then, on reflection,  I realized I didn’t have the energy to carry extra bottles out as a good Samaritan.  So, regretfully, I just left them where they were.

Back in camp I finished packing up but was dismayed that my 6:30 wake-up led to an 8:30 departure.  Why am I SO SLOW??

This thought was nagging at me as I ascended, feeling really annoyed with myself.  Then I came across a guy coming down the slope and we conversed for a bit, and I mentioned that I was annoyed.  Hearing it come out of my mouth, aloud, I regretted my attitude.  I spoke to myself, “Stop being annoyed, because what good does it do anyway?  It’s a beautiful day.  Enjoy it!”  And it was.  A beautiful day.

At a lovely stream crossing like this I gave up my annoyance and put on my gratitude!

I resumed my ascent and realized from the navigation app that the pass was still almost four miles away.  It was a good thing I travelled as far as I did the previous day, but I hadn’t exactly set myself up at the base of the pass.

I had seen plenty of snow patches nestled into shady spots on the mountainsides, but none of those spots abutted the trail.  When I finally came across one on this ascent I could not resist writing in the snow and making the last snowball of the season.

Even I cannot read (or remember) what I wrote! It’s all water now…

The approach seemed to take a really long time.  At one point there was a big boulder field and I got lost in the rocks.  I saw a guy also lost behind me, who looked like he might have followed me into this mess.  Just when I thought I might have to retrace my steps some unknown distance, I saw the vague line of a trail on the opposite side of a relatively flat rock-strewn valley.

So, I just picked my way, boulder to boulder, with a few backtracks where the terrain seemed too challenging, aiming steadily for the trail I had seen.  The going was slow, but the direction was sound, and before long I was back on trail.

Soon the path started to ascend more steeply and steadily, and I passed a number of lakes.  The last one before the pass, Helen Lake, is known for its scenic beauty.   So, when I got to the first lake, I asked a passerby, “Is this Helen Lake?”

The guy replied, “No, it’s two more further up.”

Going further up, I finally saw it. The lakes are beautiful; the mountains are beautiful.

At every moment, it is just jaw dropping.  You can enjoy a view, then hike for 20 yards looking down at your feet, then look up and it’s a whole new incredible vista!  The water, the mountains, the rocks, the trees (well, when you’re below tree-line), the everything – all dazzling.

It feels pristine as well, as though you might be the first person to see it all.

Finally, after hiking up beyond Helen Lake, the final stretch to get to the pass was a bit anticlimactic.  The redeeming factor was the iconic John Muir Trail Hut.

John Muir Pass Hut, built by the Sierra Club in 1930, right at the top of the pass

I took some pictures inside, including a creative selfie with the camera looking up at the ceiling.

I loved that ceiling! It was fun to imagine the people painstakingly laying the stones to be secure against collapse…

One group of southbound hikers went dashing up a nearby knoll.  Since the pass was at 11,955 feet they wanted to get above 12,000 feet.  For those of us headed northbound that altitude was no big deal, but it did signal that the passes up ahead were going to be a little lower.  Lower in elevation, but not necessarily easier.

Amy was at the pass as was Sally.  It’s funny that I’ve gotten to know a couple of women on this trail, but not really any men.  Not that I know either of them all that well.

Sally took off soon after my arrival; I think she had been there for a while when I got there.

I didn’t stay very long either; I just had a few snacks and then started heading down.

Before leaving, Sally asked me, “Where are you headed?  What is your goal for today?”  I was not really sure.  I replied, “I would like to get as close as possible to the Muir Trail Ranch (MTR) today, so that tomorrow I have as short a hike as possible to get there.”  I was thinking about landing in the 6-to-10-mile range for the next day’s hike for my resupply.  It meant this day would be a fairly long one.  I was hoping my wife had landed me a booking at MTR, so that I might get a shower and a night in a bed.

The trail headed down from the pass onto a long, long plain at high altitude between the mountains.  It made for pretty fast travel, because a lot of it was a flat and even trail, but it just seemed to go on forever.  Coming down from the mountain, the trail goes north for more than four miles.  Then it turns and goes down a long valley to the west.  Somewhere near the end of that valley was my destination tomorrow — the Muir Trail Ranch.

Not far below the pass I encountered a six-mule train led by a young woman on a horse.  I pulled over to let them by and took a video of their passing.  The mules were carrying supplies and backpacks and one woman as a tourist passenger.  I chatted briefly with the leader as she passed and then called out to the passenger, “A real horsewoman there!  You’re looking good!”  Indeed, the black hat sporting the tall feather made her look the part.

See the feather in her hat? I thought that was especially jaunty!

The long trail route was dry and exposed to the sun.  The saving grace was that there were scattered clouds that would cover and uncover different parts of the sky.  So, it wasn’t a blasting sun the whole time.  The downside was that I put my sunglasses on in the sun, took them off in the shade, put them on, took them off, put them on, took them off, etc.

The high flat valley in a moment when I took my sunglasses OFF

I kept drinking water to make sure I was hydrated.  Eventually the trail dropped down to the bottom of the valley and crossed Evolution Creek.  At that point, the creek is both very wide and very shallow.  There was a line of steppingstones all the way across it.  As I made my way over, steadied by my hiking poles, I thought, “This has got to be the longest crossing via steppingstones I have ever traversed!”  But the rocks were stable and the passage was not difficult.

The “Long Walk” steppingstones; if you look carefully, you can see the couple on the other side that I walked past.

I probably should have filled up my water supply right after crossing.  But there was a couple hanging out on the other side of the river, and I guess I didn’t want to inhibit their “romantic moment” by stopping at that site.  So, I continued on.

Before long I realized my mistake.  My water was getting a low, but I didn’t want to stop hydrating because I was going fast and working hard.  Eventually, as I drank, the level became less and less.  I did not want to run out of water.  I was near a lake, but I also did not want to detour for a refill.

Getting a bit worried, I asked passing southbounders about the water sources ahead of me.

I don’t know why I even bothered to ask, because I got such divergent answers.  It was similar to the night before, when I asked people about how many campers were by the trail.  This time I asked, “How close is the nearest water source?”  One woman, who was birdwatching with her husband, directed me to the lake.  Fair enough.  But others responded:  “Oh, it’s very far away.” or “Oh, it’s just about a mile.”  One woman said, “There’s a stream going down, and I think there’s some branches.”  She even offered me some of her own water, which I found very generous, but I declined, saying, “No, no, no, I’m not that bad.  I still have a little water.  I’m just wondering how far I have to go.”  I hoped I would not regret my pride!

I realized that if someone asked me how far back on my route the last water source was, I might have struggled to remember.  It is sort of “out of sight, out of mind” I guess.

As I left the area of lakes, the views of the valley to the west were inspiring.  I could see how far I would descend right away before the trail leveled off again down below.

As I dropped into shade of the forest again, I saw a person busy at the side of the trail.  She was filling her water bottle from a little trickle.  Yay!

She was Australian, and she was just finishing up.  Somehow, we started talking about the wild onions growing in that damp site.  My friend Sam had eaten some “wild parsnip” identified by his guide on a camping trip.  It turned out to be hemlock, and Sam got very sick and the guide actually died!  So, I was somewhat leery of trying something unknown, but she seemed very confident.

Finally I said, “Okay, I’ll try one.”   She pulled up a few, but most lacked a very good underground bulb end.  They did look a lot like green onions, which was reassuring.  Then she found one that seemed better, and I tasted it.

Oh my God, the power of the onion!  It was super onion-y, but tasty too.   Still, one was enough.  Especially since I wasn’t totally sure it was not a “poisonous cousin” in the onion family.

My Australian friend moved on and I filled my water bottles while enjoying that beautiful spot in the shade.  I had my late lunch too and by the time I left there, it was three o’clock.  I checked the AllTrails app and was disappointed to realize the Muir Trail Ranch was still 12 miles away.   To reach the center of my 6-10 mile target for the next day, I figured I should do another four miles.

As I started down the hill below the water source, there was an abandoned, very attractive lilac patterned bandana that someone dropped or left at the top of the trail.  It seemed weird, a rock was holding it down, but there was nobody around.  I told the first person I saw going up about it, and I said, “If it’s still there when you get to that spot, you should take it.”  The person I told was wearing a nice bandana herself; I thought she might appreciate the lilac one.

The elevation drop was quite steep.  Further down, I talked to another couple.  They were  going up.  For them, talking to me was a blessing, because they could rest a little bit without feeling guilty.  They told me he was 68 and she was 66.  I replied, from my lofty 69-year-old perch, “Oh, you’re just whippersnappers!”

I continued, going steeply down and then more gradually as I reached the bottom of the drop-off.  The trail was getting easier, but I knew I was getting tired.  I said to myself, “Take it easy George, it’s late afternoon and you’re tired.  Yes, you have a target, but slow down and be careful; this is when accidents are more common.  There is no need to rush.”

I was not rushing.  Earlier in the day, at high elevation, I had been pushing the miles.  But now I was taking my time and being careful.

Or so I thought.  I reached the bottom of a low hill, and I turned onto a nice, gentle path with a slight downhill that seemed very clear.  The next thing I knew, BAM!  I had fallen suddenly and violently face-first.

It happened so fast that I am not completely sure, but I believe my toe tripped on some tiny bump in the path.  It was the kind of a trip that happens frequently, and 99 times out of a hundred, you recover, you catch yourself.  Only the 100th time, which was this time, I couldn’t.  I just went down hard on my face.

I lay there for a few moments assessing what had happened.  My first worry was a head injury.  I had encountered a rock with my face, but it had not hit my head, and it had only slightly scraped my face.  That was all right.

I had bashed my left ribcage, but in that moment, I was not yet aware of it.

What I did know, was that on the way down, I felt a sharp pain in my left wrist.

A year before I injured myself on the Long Trail by tripping into a face-plant on an easy, light downhill sloping trail.  The parallels were unmistakable.  Even the type of injury was similar, breaking a finger on my left hand on the Long Trail and injuring my left wrist on the John Muir Trail.

So, I lay there for a moment thinking, “What is this?  The Long Trail all over again?”

Finally, I started to pick myself up.  A couple of guys came along a few minutes later.  They didn’t see the accident, but they asked, “How are you?”

I replied, “Well, actually, I’m not so good.  I am pretty shaken up.”

They were super sympathetic, and I appreciated that enormously.  One of the guys even gave me a big stash of Ibuprofen, which I knew I was going to need.  I was already feeling aches and pains from the fall.  Fortunately, my knee was not affected.  Since it was already injured, additional damage could have had serious consequences for my mobility.

Around that time, a park ranger showed up with someone else.  She wanted to check my permit.

When I saw her, I told her about my fall.  Then I said, “You’re probably wanting to look at my permit, right?”

She replied that she was and I continued, “Well, I have it.  And I would be so grateful if you would just take my word for it, because it’s in my pack and I just had this accident and I’m kind of all shook up.”

To my surprise, She said, “Okay.  I just need to get some information from you.”

With that, she took my name and the day that I had come onto the trail and… maybe other things I can’t remember.   She added kindly that I could come to the ranger station if I were feeling bad.

I thanked her, and my Ibuprofen benefactor, and we parted ways.

It was demoralizing; worse still, I tripped once or twice after that.  I caught myself each time, I didn’t fall down, but I could not understand why I was trying so hard to be so careful and still tripping.  I guess I was really tired.

For a moment I thought I should just make camp right away.  But the Muir Trail Ranch was still 11.1 miles away.  I was in shock to an extent, and not feeling my injuries very acutely.  Perhaps it was better to hike now, and leave a shorter distance for the next morning, when I might be in a lot more pain?  After that brief internal debate, I decided to press on.

Continuing down, I thought I might go to McLure Meadow.  That’s a famous open meadow area under granite peaks, and it even has a ranger station.  But it still felt too early when I got there.  I pressed on.

There were a series of camp sites by the river in Evolution Valley.  Unfortunately, the first of these was  full of people.   There were some pack animals there too, and I think the crowd may have been stockmen.  I did not stop to find out.  I worried that all the camp sites might be as crowded.

The next camp site was perfect.  There was nobody there.  Nobody.  I was able to take my pick of the four vacant spots.  I leisurely set up camp and soaked my dinner.  It was beef stroganoff again, which I’d hated earlier, but this time it tasted pretty good.  I topped off my water containers by filtering from the stream.

As I walked, the pain in my left chest increased.  I felt it was not a broken rib, though that was possible, but more like a pulled pectoral muscle.  If so, the Ibuprofen would help with  that  too.  It would be a big test to see in the morning how my wrist and my chest were doing.  Hopefully I would be able to move and hike without too much pain or disability.

The best news came from my sweet wife, who was able to book me into the Muir Trail Camp for the next night.  I had a cabin, Tent Cabin Three.

I would have a place to stay the next night that was not my tent, I could do laundry, I could shower, eat hot food, and I could charge my electronics.  The charging was important as I was almost out of juice.

Alone in my four-tent camp site, I closed my eyes and went to sleep.
