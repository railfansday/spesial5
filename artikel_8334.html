Historic Drought Looms Over Arkansas Duck Opener. What Few Ducks Show Up Face Heavy Pressure

If you asked any serious waterfowl hunter to name their dream duck-hunting destinations, Arkansas would be at the top of nearly every list. Its classic flooded hardwoods and bayous along with the state’s rich rice and grain fields, lure massive flights of puddle ducks and geese to its hallowed waters every year. This has made Arkansas stand out above other southern states as the beating heart of the Mississippi Flyway.

Yet in 2026, Arkansas’ waterfowl season is suddenly looking bleak. A historic drought has many duck hunters worried ahead of the Nov. 21 opener, as the serious lack of water could bring duck hunting to a standstill. Duck hunters on both public and private land may have some serious decisions to make to safeguard ducks and future duck seasons.

For 14 months Arkansas has been without any serious rainfall, leaving rich croplands and, more importantly for ducks, wetlands, drier than they’ve been in decades. Every corner of the state is under a severe drought status, with some areas being more than 20 inches behind in cumulative rainfall totals.

To put the drought in perspective, this is slated to be the state’s third-driest October through January since record keeping began in 1895.

This leaves the Arkansas Game and Fish Commission with very little water to spare, to the point where the agency expects it will only be able to flood around 40 of its more than 70 moist-soil public-land hunting units.

“In my 50 years of duck hunting in Arkansas, I don’t remember it ever being as dry as this,” says Brent Birch, founder of Arkansas’ Greenhead magazine. “It’s been this way all summer and well into the fall. And even with the prospects of a Super El Niño, we have nothing the forecast that says it’s going to resolve anytime soon.”

Without an incredible amount of rainfall in a short amount of time, Birch and other waterfowlers around the state are worried not only about not having places to hunt, but that the ducks may not even show up.

“So far, we are seeing a few groups of gadwalls, pintails, and other early migrating ducks but not in anything close to their usual numbers,” Birch says. “And when it comes to incoming mallards, you can pretty much forget it. It’s just too dry for them. The fact is the closer it gets to 2027 under these conditions, the worse the entire duck season is going to be. And if we don’t start getting some wet weather soon to push ducks down here, we’re looking at a poor outlook for the entire season.”

The reason that Arkansas waterfowl hunting is so good is that it lies at the north end of the historic feeding grounds for ducks and geese in the Mississippi Flyway. Once the cold sets in around their spring and summer breeding grounds in Canada, the birds come south to binge on Arkansas’ rich wetlands throughout the winter. However, with the current drought, the fear is that the incoming ducks will be forced to fly elsewhere.

“Ducks coming south to Arkansas aren’t like ducks in northern states,” says Ducks Unlimited Arkansas regional biologist Jake Spears, who lives in Jonesboro. “Northern birds are more apt to feed in dry fields so long as they have a body of water to roost on nearby. However, in the Southern U.S., they stop feeding in fields and primarily feed in water. They look for things like water plants and waste grains in flooded fields, which Arkansas currently just doesn’t have.”

Spears worries that many ducks are going to fly to Arkansas and then be forced to turn northward again or keep driving south.

“The ducks need food so they may go to Louisiana, or Kansas, or Missouri — wherever they can find that food source,” says Spears. “Which is going to leave both public-land duck hunters and even most hunting clubs just completely out of luck.”

Private-land duck-hunting clubs are a popular option for many Arkansas hunters and visiting nonresidents, as even during droughts, most clubs can pump water to create duck habitat and attract birds. The 2026 drought has been so bad, however, that many clubs won’t even have the water to pump.

This is especially troubling to some public-land waterfowl hunters, who worry that the private hunting clubs that can still pump water may end up attracting what few ducks do make it to Arkansas. This could result in a season that benefits the haves over the have-nots.

“I think hunting clubs are the only thing that’s going to make Arkansas appealing for ducks right now,” Spears says. “Most of the smaller rivers, sloughs, and backwaters we have on public land can be walked across in flip-flops right now. So the clubs that have the option to flood fields and create habitat are where the birds are going to be. But with the water table so low, clubs with the ability to pump will be few and far between, so any ducks that do show up during the season are going to have to be babied quite a bit.”

The fact is that if the drought continues in Arkansas, duck hunters on both public and private land alike are going to have to weigh their priorities. Drought conditions are going to be tough on ducks that migrate through Arkansas, and hunting pressure will make surviving the migration harder even for birds that aren’t killed.

This leaves duck hunters with a choice to make.

“It’s going to come down to responsible management on all sides because if we want to maintain the ducks we have, we have to be careful with them,” Birch says. “There are several components to management. It’s not just hunting and the noise of guns, we must be careful disturbing the ducks in any capacity. Your vehicle is an issue because you’re going to bump ducks going in and out of your hunting area, and you’re going to have to be real careful with how many days you hunt and how many hours you hunt in a day, because you’re going to have to let the birds rest.”

With Arkansas’ duck season lasting a total of 51 days with two relatively short splits, hunters looking to limit out consistently are going to be putting serious pressure on what will already be a limited resource.

So instead of hunting hard, both Birch and Spears recommend self-imposed bag limits and time on the water so that the resource can continue to both be conserved and shared.

“My suggestion to any duck hunter in Arkansas is that if you have ducks on your property, take care of them,” says Spears. “With the limited resources, they aren’t going to be able to handle a lot of pressure. You’re going to have to limit how much you hunt and how many birds you shoot, and not be afraid to cut your hunt short. Get a few birds and then leave so the ducks can get back to the water to feed.”

While there is hope that conditions will change, the fact is that the whole of Arkansas’ 2026 duck season may be a nonstarter for many hunters. That short-term pain, however, could help future duck seasons look a little brighter. Spears and Birch are hopeful for a future where water and the waterfowl will eventually return, filling the skies and waterways like flowing veins and once again making Arkansas the beating heart of the Mississippi Flyway itself.

“We need to be good stewards of the resource,” urges Spears. “Hunters should do everything they can to keep [ducks] around and not shoot them off what water we do have.”
