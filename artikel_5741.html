New Medicare Payment Model for Back Pain, Heart Failure Riles Docs

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

— Physicians will face payment cuts for subpar performance in treating those conditions

Physician groups are seeking a delay for a new Medicare demonstration model debuting January 1 that mandates doctors who treat low back pain and congestive heart failure (CHF) patients meet performance standards or face payment cuts.

The Ambulatory Specialty Model (ASM) will affect doctors who treat as few as 20 patients with back pain or 20 patients with heart failure. Those physicians will automatically be placed in the model, which will apply to specialists in any of 235 geographic areas nationwide that have been selected for the 5-year demonstration program.

According to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), the model is aimed at addressing the fragmented care that chronic disease patients often receive. "ASM will promote preventive care and more effective upstream chronic disease management by rewarding ASM participants ... for improving patient health outcomes and coordinating with primary care providers," the agency said on its website. "Successful outcomes include reducing avoidable hospitalizations and unnecessary procedures, improving patient experience, and lowering costs to Original Medicare."

Under the model, "ASM participants will receive a positive, neutral, or negative payment adjustment on future Medicare Part B claims for covered professional services," which includes any service covered by Medicare for any Medicare patient -- not just patients with low back pain or CHF -- according to CMS, with payment adjustments ranging from -9% to +9% in the first 2 ASM years, increasing to -12% to +12% by the final year.

An individual doctor's payment increase or decrease would depend on how well that doctor performed on a set of outcome measures compared with their peers, rather than being measured against a pre-set standard benchmark -- an approach known as a tournament model.

Physician groups have several concerns. "As proposed, the model guarantees that most participating physicians will see their payments cut no matter how well they perform on the measures," John Whyte, MD, MPH, CEO and executive vice president of the American Medical Association (AMA), wrote in a comment letter to CMS when the model was proposed last year.

Instead of achieving Medicare savings by reducing physician payment rates, the AMA urges CMS to help physicians in reducing avoidable Medicare spending on hospitalizations and other services, the letter stated. The AMA also suggested the model should be voluntary and that "CMS should also set a performance standard in advance instead of using a 'tournament' approach."

Physician practices that have already begun preparing for model implementation are running into problems, according to a joint letter sent on Oct. 27 to CMS Deputy Administrator Abe Sutton, JD, and signed by the AMA as well as the American Hospital Association, the American Medical Group Association, the Medical Group Management Association, and the National Association of Accountable Care Organizations, among others.

"These delays and setbacks include, but are not limited to: delayed receipt of the final participant list; delayed access to the ASM portal; challenges with logging into, accessing, or delegating authority within the portal; and receiving timely help and guidance from ASM subject matter experts," the letter said. In addition, the final ASM participant list wasn't published until Sept. 15, about 2 months later than CMS originally said it would be, leaving participants only 3 months to prepare.

Due to these and other difficulties, "the undersigned strongly urge [CMS] to delay ASM's launch until all operational and technical infrastructure is available, tested, and ready for the model to start," the letter said. "If [the agency] maintains the January 1, 2027, start date, participants should not be subject to downside financial risk for the initial performance period while operational infrastructure stabilizes."

That immediate downside risk is new for a CMS demonstration model, according to Jennifer Hananoki, JD, senior counsel at the Holland & Knight law firm in Washington, D.C.

"The risk there is no on-ramp here," Hananoki said in an online interview. "A lot of times, when CMMI [the Center for Medicare & Medicaid Innovation] is putting in place new models, especially models in areas where there hasn't been an alternative payment model in the past, they'll have a ramp-up period --  a period of upside-only risk" where practices get paid more for doing well but don't suffer penalties if they perform worse than average. "But that doesn't exist here. The risk [of penalties] really starts almost immediately."

The lack of a firm benchmark is also a problem, she added. "They need to see the benchmark in advance. They need to see how they would have performed in the past in order to understand -- 'Hey, for some reason my costs are below the median or above the median, and I need to figure out why because I need to continue that trend forward, or I need to make adjustments.' And so, there's concern that some of these really fundamental aspects of what makes for successful alternative payment models, all those ingredients might not be there yet."

Another way the ASM isn't like other Medicare physician payment models -- such as the Merit-Based Incentive Payment System, or MIPS -- is that it is meant to be money-saving rather than budget neutral, said Harold Miller, president and CEO of the Center for Healthcare Quality and Payment Reform in Pittsburgh, and the author of a report on the ASM.

"This is not budget neutral," he said in a phone interview, explaining that CMS is taking a 15% cut of the money saved with physician penalties, instead of putting all of that money into bonuses for higher-performing physicians, as MIPS does. Basically, CMS is saying, "'For every $100 we'll take away, [physicians] will get $85 back,'" he said.

There's also a concern that more doctors could be added to the participant list over time, said Jeffrey Davis, a senior policy advisor at Holland & Knight, in an online interview. "They did release a participant list the first year with 5,500 folks," said Davis. "But they could add more depending on if they want to expand the geographical regions or even, you know, [add] the new physicians that come into that region."

Rather than delay the program, CMS should abandon it entirely, Miller suggested. "It doesn't solve a problem. If we're stuck with it, don't make it mandatory," he said, "and have a predefined performance score. That would make this a less bad program."
