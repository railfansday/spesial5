A beginner's checklist for a first reef or wreck dive: 10 things to settle on land

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn how to plan your first wreck or reef dive, from certification depth limits and dive shops to buddy plans, reef etiquette and safe ascents

Most of what happens on a first wreck or reef dive is settled before anyone steps off the boat. The site, the operator, the depth, the gas plan and the exposure suit are all chosen on land. By the time a new diver descends, the dive is largely following decisions already made.

Many newly certified divers notice a gap once their course ends. Training dives take place under an instructor's direct supervision, with a set plan and a controlled site. A first fun dive works differently. The diver and a buddy share responsibility for the plan, even when a guide leads the group. Knowing how to plan your first wreck or reef dive turns that responsibility into a short series of practical choices.

Reefs and wrecks draw beginners for different reasons. A coral reef offers marine life, color and, at many popular sites, a gradual slope that lets divers pick their depth. A shipwreck offers history and structure, and it often attracts fish that shelter around the hull. Wrecks also bring hazards that reefs usually lack. These include silt that clouds the water, sharp metal, loose cables and enclosed spaces that need extra training to enter.

The 10 steps below follow the order in which most of these decisions come up. They start with the limits of an entry-level certification and the choice between a reef and a wreck. They move on to picking a dive shop, reading conditions and choosing a wetsuit or drysuit. Next come the buddy plan and the separate etiquette that applies at wreck sites and on living reefs. The final steps cover gear servicing, health, flying after diving, gas management and the slow ascent that ends every dive.

None of these steps replaces formal training or the advice of a local dive professional. Agencies such as PADI, SSI and NAUI set their own standards, and those standards differ in places. Dive computers and local rules can add further limits. Treat the guidance here as a beginner reef dive checklist and a primer on wreck diving for beginners. Confirm the details with the operator and the agency that issued your card.

An entry-level Open Water Diver certification from agencies such as PADI, SSI and NAUI typically qualifies a diver for planned dives to about 18 meters, or 60 feet. A first wreck or reef site should sit inside that range.

That figure is common agency guidance, not a universal rule. Exact limits vary by agency and by country. The British Sub-Aqua Club's entry-level Ocean Diver qualification, for example, sets its limit at 20 meters.

Younger divers face tighter limits. PADI's Junior Open Water certification restricts divers aged 10 and 11 to 12 meters, or about 40 feet.

The limit follows from the training itself. Open water courses teach skills at modest depths, and the card certifies a diver for conditions similar to the course. Going deeper raises gas consumption and shortens no-decompression time. It also increases the effects of nitrogen narcosis. Later courses give divers supervised practice in managing those effects.

Before booking, read the card. Confirm which agency issued it and at what level. Dive operators generally accept cards from the major agencies. A PADI-certified diver can usually book with an SSI shop, and the reverse. Operators will ask to see proof of certification, either a physical card or a digital version.

A logbook adds useful detail. It records how many dives a diver has made, at what depths and in what conditions. Some operators ask about recent experience before assigning a site or a guide group.

Recent practice counts alongside the card. Most entry-level certifications do not expire, but skills such as mask clearing and buoyancy control fade without use. Divers who have been out of the water for a long stretch can take a refresher program, such as PADI ReActivate, before a trip.

For sites deeper than 18 meters, the usual next step is an Advanced Open Water course. PADI's version qualifies divers to 30 meters, or about 100 feet. It includes a deep dive and a navigation dive alongside other specialty dives. Many wrecks rest below entry-level limits, so check a site's depth against the card before choosing it.

A shallow reef is usually the better setting for a first dive after certification than a shipwreck. Reefs tend to have open water overhead and fewer structural hazards. That leaves a new diver room to settle in and a direct route to the surface.

Shallow reefs also sit in the brightest part of the water column. Water absorbs red light first, so reefs near the surface keep more of their natural color than deeper sites. The extra light also helps a diver keep track of a buddy and judge distance from the reef.

Most reef dives have nothing overhead. A diver who needs to end the dive early can make a controlled ascent straight up. Open Water Diver training is built around that kind of direct access to the surface. A reef site keeps that margin intact.

Wrecks add hazards that open reefs generally do not. A wreck's depth is set by where the ship came to rest, not by what suits a beginner. Some well-known wrecks lie at or beyond the depths an entry-level card covers.

Fine sediment settles on and inside wrecks. One careless fin kick can lift it into a cloud that cuts visibility within seconds. Steel hulls also corrode and break apart over the years. That leaves jagged edges, loose plating and scattered debris.

Entanglement is another concern. Old cables and rigging can hang off a wreck. Wrecks attract fish, and fish attract anglers. Lost fishing line and netting often end up snagged on the hull. Any of these can catch a hose, a fin strap or a diver's arm.

Task loading matters as well. A newly certified diver is still building buoyancy control, tracking gas use and staying close to a buddy. A reef lets attention stay on those basics. A large structure adds navigation and hazard awareness on top of them.

Reefs are not free of risk. Current, surge over shallow reef tops and marine life that should not be touched all need attention. A sheltered reef dived with a guide limits that exposure.

Wrecks make a reasonable next step after more logged dives. Some ships were deliberately sunk as artificial reefs and cleaned of hazardous materials beforehand. Shallow, protected wrecks can be viewed from the outside once basic skills feel routine.

Book your first wreck or reef dive with an operator affiliated with a recognized training agency, such as PADI, SSI or NAUI. A suitable shop gives a full pre-dive briefing and sends a guide or divemaster into the water with new divers.

Agency affiliation means the shop's instructors and dive professionals hold ratings from that agency. They are expected to work to its published standards. Shops usually display the affiliation on their website and at the counter. PADI, for example, uses designations such as PADI Dive Center and PADI 5 Star Dive Resort.

A divemaster is a professional rating that sits below instructor in most agency systems. On a guided dive, this person leads the group and knows the layout of the site. The divemaster also watches for anyone who is struggling. For a newly certified diver on an unfamiliar site, that supervision adds a second set of eyes beyond the buddy pair.

Ask the shop how many divers each guide leads. A smaller group leaves the guide more attention for each person. Ask whether newly certified divers are grouped together or mixed with experienced divers headed to more demanding sites.

Rental equipment needs its own set of questions. Ask when the regulators were last serviced. Ask whether you can check the fit of the mask, fins and buoyancy compensator before the boat leaves. Worn straps, cracked hoses or a sticking inflator are reasons to request a replacement.

Find out how the site is reached. A boat dive usually involves a giant-stride or back-roll entry and a ladder climb on the way out. A shore dive means walking in with full gear, sometimes over rocks or loose sand.

Ask about the operator's emergency setup before booking. Find out whether the boat carries emergency oxygen, a first aid kit and a radio. Ask whether the crew follows an emergency action plan that includes contact with the nearest recompression chamber.

Expect the shop to ask questions of its own. Most will want to see a certification card before the dive. Many ask divers to complete a medical questionnaire and sign a liability release. Some operators also require proof of dive accident insurance, such as coverage from Divers Alert Network. These checks let the shop match each diver to the dive it plans to run.

Conditions at a dive site change by season, and sometimes by the hour. Ask the operator about the current, visibility, surf and water temperature before you book. A site that suits a new diver one week can be a poor choice the next.

Current shapes how much effort a dive takes. A mild current can carry a diver gently along a reef. A strong one demands more kicking and uses up air faster. It can also pull a group apart.

Some operators run drift dives. On a drift dive, divers ride the current while the boat tracks the group and collects everyone at the end. That style calls for extra skills, such as deploying a surface marker buoy. A calm, sheltered site with little water movement is the usual choice for a first outing.

Visibility describes how far a diver can see underwater. Plankton blooms, river runoff, recent storms and stirred-up sediment can all reduce it. Poor visibility makes it harder to keep track of a buddy and a guide. It can also cause disorientation, because the surface and the bottom become hard to judge. Ask how far divers have been able to see on recent trips. That answer is more useful than a site's general reputation.

Surf matters most for shore dives. Walking through breaking waves with a tank on your back and fins in hand takes coordination. A fall in the surf zone can damage gear or cause injury. Boat dives avoid the surf line, but a rough sea surface can still bring seasickness. It can also make climbing back up the boat ladder harder.

Water temperature at the site can shift with the season and with depth. A thermocline is a layer where the water turns noticeably colder, and divers can pass through one on the way down. Knowing the expected temperature in advance helps with packing and rental requests.

Operators cancel or move dives when conditions turn unsafe. That is ordinary practice, not a failure of the trip. Booking a few days in one destination leaves room for a bad-weather day. A flexible schedule also removes the pressure to dive in conditions beyond a beginner's training.

Choose your exposure protection based on the water temperature at the dive site, not the air temperature on the boat. Warm tropical water often suits a thin wetsuit. Cooler water calls for a thicker wetsuit, a hood and gloves, or a drysuit.

Water draws heat away from the body far faster than air at the same temperature. A diver can feel comfortable at the start of a dive and grow chilled before the end, even in water that feels mild on entry. Cold makes it harder to concentrate, handle equipment and follow the dive plan. All three matter on a first outing at an unfamiliar site.

A wetsuit works by trapping a thin layer of water against the skin. The body warms that layer, and the neoprene foam slows the loss of heat. Thicker neoprene contains more insulating gas bubbles, so it keeps a diver warmer. Neoprene also compresses as a diver descends, which reduces its insulation at depth.

Fit matters as much as thickness. A suit that is too loose lets cold water flush through at the neck, wrists and ankles. That constant exchange undoes much of the insulation. A suit that is too tight restricts breathing and movement. Rental suits come in a range of sizes. Trying on more than one before the boat leaves helps avoid a poor fit underwater.

Thickness also changes buoyancy. More neoprene floats more, so a diver in a thicker suit needs more lead weight to descend. Anyone switching from a thin suit to a thick one, or adding a hood and vest, should do a buoyancy check at the surface before the dive.

The cut of the suit is another choice. A shorty covers the torso with short sleeves and short legs. A full-length suit also protects the arms and legs from cold, stings and scrapes. Semi-dry suits use tighter seals at the openings to limit flushing.

A drysuit keeps the diver dry inside a sealed shell, with insulating layers worn underneath. It handles differently from a wetsuit, and training agencies teach its use in a separate specialty course. Diving one without that training raises the risk of buoyancy problems, including air shifting into the legs and an uncontrolled ascent.

The local dive operator can say what suits divers typically wear at that site in that season.

A buddy dive plan is a short conversation held before descending. It settles the maximum depth, the turnaround point, the minimum gas reserve, hand signals and what happens if the pair gets separated. Both divers should be able to repeat it back before either one enters the water.

The maximum depth for the dive comes first. It should match the site briefing and stay within the training and experience of the less experienced diver. Write it on a slate if memory might slip once underwater.

The turnaround point tells the pair when to head back toward the exit, the boat's mooring line or the shore. It can be set by elapsed time, by a landmark on the site or by a gas pressure. Settling it in advance removes the need to negotiate underwater, where conversation is limited to gestures.

The minimum gas reserve is the pressure at which each diver begins to end the dive. Operators often state a figure in the briefing, and both buddies should use the same one. Each diver also needs to know where the partner's alternate air source sits and how it releases.

Hand signals need a quick rehearsal on deck. Most recreational training agencies teach a thumb-up gesture to mean go up or end the dive, not OK. The OK sign is a circle formed by the thumb and forefinger. Divers who trained with different agencies or in different countries should compare their signals. That includes the signs for low on air, out of air and a problem with an ear or equipment.

The lost-buddy procedure covers separation, which can happen quickly in low visibility or a crowded group. The protocol commonly taught in entry-level courses is to stop, turn slowly and look for bubbles or a light for about one minute. If the partner does not appear, each diver ascends and the pair reunites at the surface. Agreeing on this keeps either diver from searching alone at depth.

The plan ends with a pre-dive safety check done together. PADI teaches it with the acronym BWRAF, which covers the buoyancy control device, weights, releases, air and a final check. Each diver inspects the other's gear. That inspection also shows where the partner's weight releases and inflator sit if an emergency arises.

On a first wreck dive, stay outside the hull and any enclosed space. Swimming inside a wreck, known as penetration, requires extra training such as a wreck diver specialty course, plus dive lights and a guideline.

The main reason is the overhead. In open water, a diver with a problem can make a controlled ascent straight to the surface. Inside a hull, deck plating, bulkheads and collapsed structure block that route. The only way out is back through an opening.

Silt makes that exit harder to find. Fine sediment settles on the floors and ledges of a sunken ship over time. One careless fin kick can lift it into a cloud that drops visibility close to zero. Divers call this a silt-out, and it can happen in seconds.

Disorientation can follow quickly. A ship lying on its side or upside down no longer matches the layout it had afloat. Doorways turn into holes in the floor or ceiling, and stairways run sideways. Without a guideline fixed outside the wreck, a diver can lose track of the way out.

Specialty training addresses these problems directly. Courses teach how to lay and follow a guideline and how to carry primary and backup lights. They also teach fin kicks, such as the frog kick, that direct thrust behind the diver rather than down toward the silt. Students practice these skills under supervision before attempting any penetration.

The outside of a wreck carries its own hazards. Corroded steel can leave sharp, jagged edges. Wrecks attract fish, and they also attract anglers. Monofilament fishing line and old nets often end up snagged on the structure. Many wreck divers carry a cutting tool for that reason. Keeping hoses and gauges tucked close to the body also reduces snag points.

Some wrecks are protected by law. In the U.K., the Protection of Military Remains Act 1986 covers designated military wrecks. In the U.S., the Sunken Military Craft Act of 2004 protects sunken military vessels and aircraft. Many of these sites are war graves.

Rules on removing objects from wrecks vary by country and by site. Taking artifacts can be illegal. Divers on a first wreck visit can look and take photographs while leaving everything in place.

Good reef etiquette comes down to three habits: hold neutral buoyancy, keep your hands off the coral and keep your fins clear of the bottom. Many corals are living animals, and a single kick or grab can break structures that took years to grow.

A coral head is a colony of tiny animals called polyps. Each polyp builds a limestone skeleton. In reef-building corals, algae living inside the polyp's tissue supply much of its food. A thin layer of mucus covers the living surface. Contact from a hand, knee or fin can strip that layer and leave the colony open to infection. It can also snap off branching growth outright.

Buoyancy control is the skill that makes the no-touch rule possible. A diver who is too heavy sinks toward the reef and starts kicking or grabbing to stay off it. A diver who is too light drifts upward and then overcorrects. Make small adjustments to the buoyancy compensator and use your breathing to fine-tune depth. Hover a comfortable distance above the reef rather than right at its edge.

Trim, or body position, works alongside buoyancy. A horizontal posture keeps the fins level with the torso instead of angled down toward the coral. Bicycle-style kicks and steep head-up postures push fins into the reef. They also stir up sand that settles back onto the coral. A gentle flutter kick or frog kick moves water backward rather than downward.

Loose equipment causes damage that divers often miss. A dangling alternate air source, pressure gauge or camera can drag across the reef behind you. Clip hoses and gauges close to the body before descending. Keep lights, slates and other accessories secured.

The same restraint applies to marine life. Do not feed fish. Feeding changes their natural behavior and can make some species aggressive toward divers. Do not chase turtles or rays, and do not corner animals under ledges. Take photos from a distance. Leave shells, coral and rocks where they are. Many marine parks prohibit removing anything.

Some reef protection starts before the dive. Hawaii banned the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate over concerns about harm to coral. That law took effect in 2021. Palau adopted a similar ban. Check the local rules at your destination. A rash guard reduces how much sunscreen you need to apply.

If you lose control and begin sinking toward the reef, one finger placed on bare rock or sand does far less harm than a kick through living coral. Some sites discourage or ban gloves to remove the temptation to hold on.

Service your regulator on the manufacturer's schedule, test every piece of gear before you travel and skip any dive when a cold or congestion stops you from equalizing. Then plan your flight home around the surface interval your agency and dive computer recommend.

Each manufacturer sets its own regulator maintenance interval. There is no universal rule. The paperwork from the last service usually shows when the next one is due. A regulator that sits unused between trips still needs attention. Seals and O-rings can age even without use.

A home check a week or two before departure catches problems while there is still time to fix them. Inspect each hose for cracks, bulges or worn fittings near the ends. Breathe from the second stage and the alternate air source on a full cylinder if one is available. Look over the mask strap and fin straps for perished rubber. Charge or replace the dive computer battery, and confirm the unit powers on and displays correctly.

Rental equipment deserves the same scrutiny on arrival. Ask the shop to explain its computer or gauges if they differ from the ones you trained with.

Sinus and ear health decides whether a dive can happen at all. On descent, equalizing moves air into the middle ear and sinuses to balance rising water pressure. Congestion from a cold, an allergy or a sinus infection can block those air spaces. Forcing a descent against a blocked ear risks barotrauma, which can include a ruptured eardrum.

Decongestants carry their own risk. If one wears off during a dive, expanding air can become trapped in the ears or sinuses on the way up. Divers call this a reverse block. With a stuffy head, the conservative choice is to sit the dive out.

Flying soon after diving raises the risk of decompression sickness. The cabin of a commercial jet is pressurized below sea-level pressure. That lower pressure can let nitrogen still dissolved in the body form bubbles. For a single no-decompression dive, 12 hours at the surface is a commonly cited minimum before boarding.

Training agencies differ in their exact recommendations. Repeated dives over several days call for a longer wait. Many divers leave a full day or more between their last dive and their flight. The dive computer's no-fly time and the agency's guidance should set the final schedule. Placing the last dive early in the itinerary, rather than on departure morning, keeps that interval from being squeezed.

Watch your submersible pressure gauge throughout the dive and rise no faster than about nine meters (30 feet) per minute. Pause for three minutes at roughly five meters (15 feet) before surfacing. Training agencies commonly teach these figures, but exact rates and stops vary by agency and dive computer.

The submersible pressure gauge, often shortened to SPG, shows how much gas remains in the cylinder. Check it every few minutes and before any change in depth. Regular checks keep the reading from catching you off guard.

Depth changes how fast that reading falls. At 10 meters of seawater, the surrounding pressure is about double the pressure at the surface. A breath taken there draws roughly twice as much gas from the tank as the same breath at the surface. Cold, exertion and nerves push consumption higher. A cylinder can empty faster than a new diver expects.

Plan to reach the surface with a reserve still in the cylinder. Start the ascent early enough to keep it. That reserve covers problems such as a slow ascent, a strong current near the boat or a buddy who runs low. A buddy in that position may need to share gas from your alternate air source.

Speed matters most during the ascent. Rising too quickly raises the risk of decompression sickness. Nitrogen absorbed at depth can come out of the body's tissues faster than the body can release it safely. Holding your breath while rising can injure the lungs as the air inside them expands. Breathe normally the whole way up.

Most dive computers sound an alarm or flash a warning when the ascent rate is too fast. Follow the computer when its guidance is more conservative than the general rule. Air in the buoyancy compensator device, or BCD, expands as you rise. The gas bubbles in a neoprene wetsuit expand too. Vent small amounts of air from the BCD to keep the ascent under control.

The safety stop is a precaution on dives that stay within no-decompression limits. It differs from a required decompression stop, which applies to dives that exceed those limits. The pause gives the body extra time to release nitrogen before the final stretch to the surface.

Holding steady at the stop takes practice. An anchor line, a mooring line or a sloping reef gives a visual reference for depth. Watch the computer or depth gauge rather than guessing. Once the three minutes pass, cover the last few meters slowly. Look up as you near the surface to check for boats overhead.
