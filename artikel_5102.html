How Coldcard’s $112 million Bitcoin exploit slipped past 15 security reviews

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Independent researchers repeatedly examined Coldcard's firmware without discovering the vulnerability that ultimately enabled widespread Bitcoin theft.

A security flaw in Coldcard's Bitcoin hardware wallets went undetected for more than five years despite at least 15 independent security research and review efforts.

Coinkite, the wallet's manufacturer, published its postmortem on Nov. 24, detailing how a firmware defect introduced in March 2021 weakened the recovery phrases generated by its devices, enabling attackers to steal customers' Bitcoin without accessing their hardware wallets.

An Aug. 14 analysis by Galaxy Research identified with high confidence 1,778.84 BTC, worth $112.7 million at the time, stolen from more than 8,600 addresses. Galaxy estimated that losses could reach 2,417 BTC, or $153 million, if additional suspected thefts were confirmed.

Coinkite said it had matched approximately 300 loss-related support tickets to 236 customer accounts, representing about 0.2% of its estimated user base. However, the company acknowledged that the actual number of affected customers was likely higher.

The vulnerability originated from a firmware configuration error that caused Coldcard devices to generate recovery phrases using a weaker software-based random number generator instead of the intended hardware component.

The devices continued producing apparently valid recovery phrases, allowing the defect to escape routine security and functionality tests.

Coinkite's postmortem estimates that the defect substantially reduced the randomness protecting recovery phrases across five Coldcard models, including the Mk2, Mk3, Mk4, Mk5 and Q.

Attackers exploited that weakness to reconstruct private keys offline and transfer Bitcoin from affected wallets.

Between 2021 and 2026, Coldcard underwent at least 15 independent security research and review efforts, including two paid third-party product reviews. Its publicly available firmware repository also had 54 contributors.

One commissioned review in March 2022 specifically examined random-number generation and related security measures without discovering the defect.

More recently, independent security researcher Rob Hamilton used OpenAI's Cybersecurity Harness to examine Coldcard's firmware between May 1 and June 26.

The AI-assisted review generated 85 potential security findings, several of which prompted fixes, but failed to identify the seed-generation vulnerability.

A separate June 17 review by security researcher Nick Farrow also examined weak random-number generation and concluded that the relevant implementation was sound.

Coinkite attributed these failures to a gap in its verification process. Existing checks established that the hardware random-number generator was present in the firmware but failed to confirm whether the software actually used it to create recovery phrases.

The company identified the defect on July 30, following the first reported thefts, and released corrected firmware the following day.

Security firm Keylabs subsequently verified the fix across four firmware releases for Coldcard's Mk5 and Q wallets.

Coinkite has also introduced additional safeguards that combine user-provided randomness with multiple hardware sources when generating new recovery phrases.

However, these changes cannot strengthen recovery phrases created using the defective firmware, leaving previously affected wallets exposed even after their owners install the latest updates.

Coinkite said wallets using securely generated external recovery phrases were unaffected. Owners who incorporated at least 50 fair, independent and private dice rolls during their original wallet setup also remained protected if they followed the prescribed procedure.

The company advises other affected users to generate new recovery phrases using verified firmware and transfer their Bitcoin to the resulting wallets.

Meanwhile, law enforcement continues investigating the thefts, and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security has opened a separate case.

Coinkite has appealed to whoever controls the stolen Bitcoin to return the funds. Nearly four months after the exploit, however, the company has yet to decide whether it will compensate affected customers.
