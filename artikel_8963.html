Don’t Let a Flat Tire End Your Ride. Here’s What I Carry in My Ride-Rescue Kit

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

A practical guide to building a flat kit that gets you home without overloading your bike.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

There are so many options out there when setting up a flat tire kit. During my time in bike shops I sold every flat kit you could imagine—from the basic prepackaged flat repair kits to repair kits that could fix almost anything on the bike. When you have the right pieces of gear, it can make the difference between an easy roadside fix and finding a plan B to get home.

Building the right kit can make the difference between fixing the bike mid-ride and needing an unexpected pickup. One key is bringing the things you need and not what you don’t—then knowing how to use the tools in your kit.

Even on bikes you’ve had for a while it can be worth revisiting what’s sitting in your flat pack. Below I’ll walk you through what’s in my basic flat kit, why I pick those pieces, and why you should intermittently check out your flat pack to prevent any roadside surprises.

The tire levers are the foundation of any good flat repair kit—after all, you won’t be able to fix a flat very well if you can’t get the tire off and back on. I’ve gone through many different lever options over the years in my own flat kits, as well as from my time running a bike shop. The tire levers I swear by are the Pedro’s yellow tire levers. They are burly enough to be reliable and durable—I’m rarely worried about snapping a Pedro’s lever even on a tight tire.

The one thing many people don’t think about with levers is that sometimes you drop them or put them down when you’re trying to manage the roadside repair. The Pedro’s levers are easy to find thanks to their bright yellow color.

One runner-up is the tire lever from Muc-Off. They are similar in size, durability, and come in bright and easy to find colors. Personally I have not had the same amount of time using Muc-Off levers compared to the Pedro’s levers. One final tip: don’t use metal levers. Yes, they are burly, but they can easily damage rims when trying to get a tire off—especially carbon.

The traditional tube you may have gotten the last time you stopped in your local shop while picking up a tube to fix a flat at home is a great option to put in your flat pack, but there are other options. TPU and latex tubes are popular because of their light weight and small packed size.

I typically go with a standard tube on most of my flat kits. This is because I typically ride all year round and some of those lighter and more packable materials for tubes are more sensitive to temperature. If you’re a fair-weather rider, however, it can be worth considering a TPU or latex tube. Just remember to double-check that both the tube and valve size are correct for the wheels you are using.

One note on checking out your tubes: check to make sure the tube is in good condition every few months. I was on a ride recently where someone got a flat and his multitool was against the tube in his saddle bag. The multitool rubbed a hole into the side of the tube making it useless. He said he had not inspected that tube in at least a year. Luckily we had an extra tube for him and could keep rolling, but a quick check could have prevented him pulling a tube from someone else’s flat kit.

Similar to tire levers, I’ve used many different inflators over the years. My favorites to use are either the PDW CO2 inflator or the Muc-Off Inflator. These two options are durable and are easy to use with gloves on—which is especially helpful as we get into some cooler temperatures.

I carry two cans of CO2 because I’ve run into several experiences before where one ends up not being enough. It could be because I used one to try to add a burst of air in the hopes my tubeless system would fill the hole with sealant and I could go on my way. I’ve also had user error experiences where I simply didn’t get the canister on correctly and most or all of the air rushed out of the canister. In any case, they don’t take up much space, but having a backup is a key piece of the flat kit.

There are other inflation options you can go with too. Electronic pumps have been growing in popularity over the last few years, with test editor Dan Chabanov recently calling out the Cycplus AS2 Ultra Electric Inflator as his inflator of choice. I typically add a frame-mounted pump on longer rides, because these can’t run out of battery and as long as you can still pump you won’t run out of available air.

You’d be surprised what you can fix with a few zip ties. I’ve used these packable and inexpensive additions to fix issues like broken bottle cage bolts and broken cable and housing guides. I’ve had friends who have reattached things like derailleurs and saddle bags with a few strategically placed zip ties.

When I ride I typically carry a $10 bill and a single dollar bill. I know $11 seems like a random amount of money to carry on the bike. But the single and the ten serve two different purposes. A single dollar bill works as a great tire boot if you have a large gash in your tire. Simply put the dollar bill inside the tire against the gash before putting the tube in. If you run tubeless you can apply a little sealant from inside the tire to the dollar bill to help it stick better against the tire. Then when you inflate your tube, the pressure of the tube inside the tire will keep the dollar bill in place. The dollar bill will protect the tube from the elements and the road surface outside the tire.

The ten-dollar bill is your backup plan. I’ve been on rides before where I underestimated the amount of hydration and nutrition I needed to get through an effort. Having a few dollars for a gas station or convenience store refuel stop has dragged me out of the hole more than once. Most places these days take digital forms of payment like Apple Pay, but if you’re riding in more remote areas cash can be king.

A multitool that has the basic Allen keys and screwdriver that you’ll need is essential. If it has sizes 4, 5, and 6 you’ll be able to take care of most roadside fixes or adjustments you might ride into.

I personally like to bring a multitool with a chain breaker because I’ve had to fix a chain more than once on a ride. If fixing a chain is something you want to be prepared for on your ride, consider bringing a quick link that is compatible with your chain. They are another small and relatively inexpensive addition to your flat kit that can save plenty of headaches. A broken chain doesn’t have to be the end of your ride if you have the tools and the knowledge to piece it back together.

Different bikes have different ways to carry all this stuff. Some bikes have internal frame storage, some saddles have integrated mounts for certain bags, and some bikes have dropper posts that are only compatible with select saddle bags. On really short road rides I’ve even just thrown the basics of my flat kit in a jersey pocket. Personally, I typically put my flat repair kit into a frame bag, saddle bag, or utilize the internal frame storage so I can keep the extra weight and bulk off my body. If you have any essentials in your flat kit that I don’t include in mine let me know in the comments below.
