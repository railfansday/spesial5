An internet devoured by AI. Surveillance at every traffic light. Global leaders who seem determined to bring about dystopia. At the end of a year like 2026, it’s tempting to conclude that there’s little room for idealism. Don’t give in! After all, this year we also saw a new generation of filmmakers challenge Hollywood, teenagers take on AI companies, and scientists make once-unimaginable breakthroughs. So for the first time, we’re publishing a WIRED Yearbook: our very subjective, occasionally ridiculous, always enthusiastic collection of the people, institutions, inventions, and moments that offered optimism in 2026. From the ecstatic night the Knicks won the NBA championship to the surprising ease with which New York absorbed the chaos of the World Cup to the record-low violent crime—it’s been a huge 2026 for the greatest city in the world. WIRED first sat down with Zohran Mamdani in 2025, when he was still just a candidate for mayor. A year later, as the midterm elections loomed, we returned.

KATIE DRUMMOND: We talked last year about social media as a tool for campaigning. How are you thinking about social media now as a tool for governance?

ZOHRAN MAMDANI: You have to continue to recognize the fact that for many New Yorkers, this is how they engage with the world. It is yet another medium that has to be treated with the same legitimacy and respect as traditional mediums.

What’s your point of view on the data center movement? Why do you think that specific issue has become such a flash point?

The movement to oppose data centers is one that gives me a real sense of hope.

It’s a bipartisan fight. What you’re seeing from Americans across the country is an opposition to what these data centers would mean for their day-to-day lives, what they would mean for their electricity rates, what they would mean for the environment. It’s also a repudiation of what has been governing politics for the last few years when it comes to AI, which is: Our risk, their reward.

Last year I asked you if technology was making democracy stronger or weaker. You said weaker. What about today?

I have family members sending me AI videos of me, thinking that they’re real. One complimented me on a 45-minute debate that I had with a European member of parliament. I was like, “This is not me.” There is a real crisis of faith as to what is real right now.

What do you hope the Democratic party looks like in, say, 2036?

I hope that party looks like one that realizes the only majority in this country is the working class, and fights for it. An ambitious version of the party cannot only be one that we find in history books. We need that to be a living, breathing politic. The people who run it have to be the American people.

In early August, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez announced that she was freezing her eggs. “Usually I keep my private life quite private,” she said in an Instagram video, before opening up about the process and sharing videos of the self-administered hormone injections.

This wasn’t an extraordinary event—more women than ever are making the same decision—but the internet exploded anyway. “This desire to start having children late in life is completely baffling to me,” wrote the far-right commentator Matt Walsh on X. “Totally backwards and ridiculous.”

Republicans weren’t all on the same page: Katie Miller, wife of the man recently promoted to White House chief of staff, defended AOC, writing on X, “If one woman wants to preserve her right to have children, which means more than having none—we should celebrate that.”

It is true that the Republican party has been intensely focused on women’s fertility. The expectation of motherhood, along with the proliferation of pro-natalism, has become core to the party. For conservative commentators to bash the New York representative felt incongruous at best. The White House itself reportedly didn’t love Walsh’s take.

Meanwhile, in an interview with ABC, Ocasio-Cortez said it was important to “normalize” the procedure at a time when “this administration is denying reproductive care to women across the country.”

As the 37-year-old congresswoman considers a presidential run in 2028, expect more of this. Conversations about her body, her health, and her possible maternal well-being will be front and center no matter what. She could have 100 children, and Republicans would spend time complaining about their names. —Leah Feiger

Nothing says rock and roll like a marketing machine. Elvis had Colonel Tom Parker. The Rolling Stones had Andrew Loog Oldham. Geese has video clippers.

Social media users sensed something inorganic was happening on their For You pages after Geese released their album Getting Killed in September 2025. That and the band’s meteoric rise this year drove accusations that a “psyop” was afoot.

As WIRED wrote in April, the Brooklyn-based four-piece’s takeoff had indeed been somewhat staged: Digital marketing agency Chaotic Good Projects had been clipping and posting short videos of Geese content across a network of TikTok accounts to drive the band’s engagement. “It was low-effort TikTok posts that were used to boost the algorithm,” as frontman Cameron Winter put it to Rolling Stone. “They showed us samples, and it made me feel better, because they were so shitty.”

Magazine profiles and good Pitchfork reviews don’t give bands the juice they used to. Now many chart-toppers take off on TikTok first. But that also means competing with scores of other content for ears. Chaotic Good and its network of clippers aren’t so much running a “psyop” as they are trying to meet audiences where they are. And FYI, the agency prefers the term “trend simulation.” —Isabella Ward

For the greatest reversal of fortune this year.

The only word that accurately described the scene outside New York’s Madison Square Garden on the night of June 3, 2026, was “pandemonium.” Game one of the NBA Finals had just ended some 1,600 miles away in San Antonio, where the New York Knicks had beaten the host Spurs. Thousands of fans flooded out of the watch party the Garden had hosted to express their primal joy. Among the revelers was 23-year-old MD Ahnaf Hossain, a Queens native who beelined to a film crew from the prediction-market giant Kalshi. Clad in a red and orange Hawaiian shirt, Hossain improvised a poem that made the surrounding throng go wild: My mayor Muslim / My bagels Jewish / My Christian Dior / Knicks in four!

Those words, conjured in the heat of an ecstatic moment, changed Hossain’s life overnight. By the time he woke up the next morning, his performance had become the internet’s new favorite thing—a concise encapsulation of New York’s unconditional love for the city and the Knicks, who hadn’t won an NBA championship in more than half a century.

Kalshi was quick to latch onto the viral sensation, shooting a follow-up video in which it gifted Hossain a Christian Dior scarf. The company also arranged for Hossain to attend game four at the Garden, which came on the heels of the Knicks’ first loss of the series. After watching the Knicks triumph in an epic comeback, Hossain again waded into the Midtown chaos to deliver a tweaked poem that accounted for the new mathematical realities of the series. He kept the first two lines the same, but changed the last two to embrace the third major Abrahamic religion: The Pope’s on our side / Knicks in five!

Hossain, who earned a finance degree from the Rochester Institute of Technology in 2026, has worked hard to capitalize on his unexpected fame since the Knicks did, indeed, win the title in five games. He launched a project called City of Us that sells a $29 T-shirt emblazoned with “New York in 5” and which aims to host watch parties during the upcoming NBA season. He has also starred in branded content for businesses such Tex’s Chicken & Burgers, for whom he delivered a bespoke poem about the glories of crispy chicken wraps.

One of Hossain’s favorite parts of his wild ride has been the support he’s received from fellow Bangladeshis, who make up a huge percentage of his Queens neighborhood. “I get love from my fans and that’s awesome, but then getting fans from my own people just feels completely different,” he says. That love may soften as the 2026 NBA season fades from memory, but “Knicks in five!” might just be perfect enough to endure. —Brendan I. Koerner

Either way, the Coxon saga shows: If you’re going to quit, you might as well do it in spectacular fashion. —Lauren Goode

Calling it “El Niño”—Spanish for “the little boy”—might be the understatement of the year. Perhaps that’s why reports have started calling it “supersized” and even “Godzilla.” Signs point to it being the strongest El Niño in centuries.

It started the way they all do: with hotter-than-normal waters in the central and eastern tropical Pacific. That leads to more evaporation and more thunderstorms in the region, plus a shift in trade winds that allows more warm water to roll from west to east. As the cycle continues, changes ripple throughout the entire atmosphere. During an El Niño, some regions—such as the southern tier of the US—tend to see more rainfall, while others—such as the Pacific Northwest—are more prone to drought. Same this time around, only worse.

One reason for this year’s record-breaking events? Climate change, says Christopher Callahan, a professor at the University of Indiana who studies environmental shifts. The first modern, extra-strong El Niño was recorded between 1982 and 1983; since then, there have been four more. New studies based on coral records show the intensity of El Niño over the past millennium shooting up like a hockey stick. “I just remember going like, ‘Holy shit, this is very strong evidence,’” Callahan says of a paper that came out in August.

While some El Niño impacts—like fewer Atlantic hurricanes—are beneficial, it’s overall a costly phenomenon. In 2023, Callahan led a study that modeled the damages associated with the 1982–83 and 1997–98 El Niños and found they led to $4.1 trillion and $5.7 trillion in global income losses, respectively. Scientists believe El Niño 2026– 27 could be more intense than those two by a wide margin.

Researchers are also examining how the costs associated with El Niño will shift as the planet heats up. The answer isn’t heartening: El Niño events could potentially knock $84 trillion off the global economy this century. Maybe we’d prefer Godzilla. —Brian Kahn

At an AI conference in Paris over the summer, a line for the main auditorium wrapped around the building. Nobody was being let in; the room was already packed. Onstage sat Arthur Mensch, cofounder and CEO of the French AI lab Mistral.

Unable to compete with OpenAI or Anthropic in performance, Mistral had previously been written off as an also-ran. But in 2026, as Europe grew ever-more anxious about its reliance on American technology, the lab found its raison d’être.

Mistral markets itself as the obvious alternative: a Europe-based lab whose models—most of which are published under an open-source license—cannot be switched off remotely. “If you use a closed model, there is no guarantee it will still be there tomorrow,” Guillaume Lample, cofounder and chief scientist at Mistral, told WIRED.

As the face of Europe’s sovereign resistance to American AI hegemony, Mensch issues dire warnings with nonchalance. “The alternative to open source winning is actually a pretty dark world,” he told the packed conference room. “You’re giving way too much power to companies that are going to become statelike.” The pitch is working. In September, Mistral raised roughly $3.5 billion in funding, the largest-ever round by a private European tech company. —Joel Khalili

For days on end, volunteers sat outside the Whipple Federal Building—site of much ICE activity—and logged every vehicle that came or went. Less than six months later, the Justice Department went after DAMN, charging 15 members and associates of the coalition with conspiracy to impede federal officers. Prosecutors alleged the coalition had ties to antifa and in August revealed via court filings that Homeland Security had been sending undercover agents to activist meetings for months. Claude’s nemesis appears to be a stunningly capable open-weight model from China called GLM.

The latest version, GLM-5.3, was released by Z.ai, a Beijing-based AI company, in August. Few maladies strike fear like pancreatic cancer. It’s a brutally lethal disease; the vast majority of those diagnosed won’t live more than five years. Though it’s a relatively rare form of cancer, accounting for around 3 percent of annual diagnoses in the United States, it’s the third-highest driver of cancer deaths.

But in 2026, a new treatment from the California-based biotech company Revolution Medicines offered a dose of hope. Rolled out in August under the brand name Rasonque, daraxonrasib is an oral therapy that works by inhibiting the signaling activity of a specific protein called RAS, mutations of which are responsible for the vast majority of pancreatic cancer cases. After collecting his Nobel Prize in Stockholm late last year, Omar Yaghi returned to the United States in a restless mood. The 61-year-old chemist was thrilled to have been recognized for his pioneering work on metal-organic frameworks, crystalline structures that are built from a combination of metal ions and carbon-based molecules. And he was excited that his startup, Atoco, was testing a machine that uses these frameworks to harvest water from the atmosphere, an innovation that could completely transform the world’s driest regions. But Yaghi also feared he’d hit a dead end at UC Berkeley, where he’d been a professor since 2012. In addition to feeling constrained by the school’s bureaucracy, he was frustrated by how hard it had become to secure visas for gifted foreign students, the lifeblood of his lab. As an immigrant himself, Yaghi was troubled by the Trump administration’s virulent nativism.

“The rhetoric about immigrants is not helping good people do their job,” says Yaghi, a naturalized American citizen originally from Jordan. “I must say that for the first time in my life, I felt that I didn't have protection of the law.” So 45 years after he first came to the US as a teenager who barely spoke a word of English, Yaghi decided to find a new country in which to pursue his goal of “solving ambitious problems.”

Germany was at the top of Yaghi’s list, but none of the universities there seemed willing to provide him with the resources he wanted. He was instead lured to China: In July, Tsinghua University appointed him to lead a new institute devoted to using AI to accelerate chemical research. Yaghi says he was drawn to the opportunity because he’s a firm believer in AI’s potential to “shorten the time between discovery and commercialization.” Having spent a quarter century perfecting his water-harvesting technology, largely through arduous trial and error, he is optimistic that AI can help him get his next invention to market in a fraction of that time.

Yaghi’s move to Tsinghua was viewed as a harbinger of China’s scientific rise. And after just a few months on the school’s Beijing campus, Yaghi says he has been impressed by his colleagues’ brilliance and drive. “I feel like my scientific environment wants to get better, wants to improve, wants to move forward,” he says. “The attitude is more of a let's-get-it-done type.” Atoco is now about to start selling its first water-harvesting system, capable of producing up to 1,000 gallons per day in optimal conditions. The company is also developing machines to capture atmospheric carbon, a key to slowing the pace of climate change. These technologies use relatively little energy because Yaghi’s frameworks bond with carbon-dioxide molecules.

Yaghi still has great affection for the US, where he started his academic journey at a New York community college in the 1980s. He hopes the country will awaken to the need to protect the innovation system it built over many decades—a system he describes even now as “the envy of the world.” Science “is part of our civilization, and we should not take attacks on science very lightly,” he says. “It is something that society needs to value.” —Brendan I.
