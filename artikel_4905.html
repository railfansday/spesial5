You've seen enough GRWM videos to know that plenty of people are incorporating lymphatic drainage tools into their morning routines. If you also grapple with regular facial puffiness, it's fair to wonder if lymphatic drainage tools could help.

These tools are marketed to encourage the flow of lymph, which is a clear, watery fluid that supports fluid balance and immune defenses in your body. The idea with lymphatic drainage tools—which include gua sha stones, facial rollers, dry brushes, and more—is to mechanically encourage the movement of lymphatic fluid to reduce puffiness and fluid retention.

Dermatologists say there's something to this. "Your lymph system helps move excess waste and fluid throughout the body," explains Joshua Zeichner, MD, director of cosmetic and clinical research at Mount Sinai Hospital. "Lymphatic massages help enhance activity of the system to circulate excess fluid and depuff."

Doctors may recommend lymphatic drainage massage to people with health conditions like lymphedema or to post-surgical patients, says Ife J. Rodney, MD, founding director of Eternal Dermatology + Aesthetics. For everyone else, the data is less clear on whether lymphatic drainage offers much benefit.

"For everyday facial puffiness, gentle massage may offer temporary improvement, though research on gua sha is limited," Dr. Rodney says. Note the "temporary" part of what she said. Despite what you may hear on social media, lymphatic drainage won't permanently alter your cheekbones and jawline, although Dr. Rodney says this may help your skin look fresher and your features look more defined. "Think of it as an optional self-care step, not a requirement for healthy skin," Dr. Rodney says.

The best type of lymphatic drainage tool for you depends on where you plan to use it on your body. "These types of massages can be performed anywhere, but are extremely common on the face," Dr. Zeichner says. "Oftentimes, a smooth, but firm tool like gua sha is used."

A roller is another option for facial lymphatic drainage, and is usually considered more gentle than gua sha. For lymphatic drainage other than the face, some people reach for dry brushes and vibration plates.

For gua sha, Dr. Rodney suggests choosing a tool with smooth, rounded edges with no sharp spots, chips, or cracks. "It should be comfortable to hold and easy to clean," she says—and that applies to all lymphatic drainage tools. For gua sha and rollers, "you don't need an expensive crystal," Dr. Rodney says.

Dry brushes should also have natural fibers, since synthetic can feel harsh against the skin. Overall, "a smooth finish and gentle technique matter more than the price tag," Dr. Rodney says.

There's no exact weight for a lymphatic drainage tool that's best for everyone. Instead, it should feel substantial in your hand—but not too heavy or too light. You want to be able to comfortably use your tool, and allow the tool to apply pressure vs. heavy force from you.

They may temporarily reduce puffiness for some people, but evidence for at-home tools and broader health benefits is limited. "For everyday facial puffiness, gentle massage may offer temporary improvement," Dr. Rodney says. Dr. Zeichner agrees. "Especially if you wake up puffy in the morning after drinking too much alcohol, not having enough sleep, taking in too much salt, or during your menstrual cycle, lymphatic massages can be a useful tool," he says. Just keep in mind that the effects are temporary.

Every type of tool is slightly different. "The goal of a lymphatic massage is to improve the body's drainage system," Dr. Zeichner says. "That is why the massage should be performed in a particular way." If you're massaging your face, the pressure should start near the center of your face, before moving out and down toward your collarbones. This follows the natural lymphatic flow, Dr. Zeichner says.

"A few minutes is enough to start," Dr. Rodney says. "Keep the pressure light—bruising is a sign to stop, not a sign it’s working."

Dermatologists recommend taking a pass on lymphatic drainage tools if your skin is irritated, infected, sunburned, or broken. (That includes inflamed acne, eczema, or rosacea flares, per Dr. Rodney.)

Dr. Rodney also suggests checking with your doctor if you take blood thinners, bruise easily, or have blood clots, heart or kidney failure, lymphedema, or a history of cancer treatment or lymph node removal. "After cosmetic procedures or surgery, get clearance from your treating clinician," she says. "Persistent or painful swelling needs an explanation before it needs a massage."
