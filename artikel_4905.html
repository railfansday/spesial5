The 11 Best War Movies About Brotherhood, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Ahead of Netflix’s The Mosquito Bowl, we’ve pulled together a list of gripping films that show how bonds between men survive—and sometimes break—under fire.

DIRECTOR PETER BERG had never made a World War II movie before The Mosquito Bowl, but he certainly knows a thing or two about football. The director of Friday Night Lights has returned to a world of cocksure young players with their whole lives ahead of them in dire need of rousing their morale—only now it’s the true story of the football game between two World War II Marine regiments at Guadalcanal on Christmas Eve 1944. There’s another connection: both The Mosquito Bowl and Friday Night Lights are adapted from books by author Buzz Bissinger.

Berg’s new Netflix film dramatizes the stories of football players John McLaughry (Nicholas Galitzine), Tony Butkovich (Ray Nicholson), Bob Bauman (Bill Skarsgård), and Dave Schreiner (Tom Francis), who traded the NFL draft for military service. After enduring years of the Pacific campaign, they realize how valuable the team spirit and camaraderie of football can be as the Battle of Okinawa looms ahead of them.

The Mosquito Bowl underlines the devastating impact WWII’s casualty count had on an entire generation of young athletes, and the film unites its twin focuses of war and football by their mutual sense of brotherhood. The deeply felt connection between soldiers is the lifeblood of historical war films; when done convincingly, a squad’s well-earned devotion and loyalty add rich melodrama to the action. Brotherhood is just as compelling when it is subverted—like the respect felt between soldiers on opposing sides, or when hardened, broken combatants struggle to express the empathy and compassion they feel. The best war movies know that brotherhood endures after the fighting stops, but in different forms—softer, stranger, when it’s hard to reconcile the memories of war with a world that continues as normal.

To mark the Thanksgiving release of The Mosquito Bowl on Netflix, we ranked the most gripping and rousing war films about brotherhood.

An ensemble of actors you'd typically see in a "Young Stars of Tomorrow" profile immerse themselves in a U.S. Navy SEAL platoon and recreate a real-time Iraq War firefight that co-director Ray Mendoza was a part of. Opening with the platoon jubilantly dancing to "Call On Me" streamed through their base's crappy internet, Warfare quickly adopts a stripped-back and meticulous play-by-play of how one slice of warfare was actually experienced. You get a sense of brotherhood from the practiced, reflexive interactions between SEALs, and their interdependence also helped build the film, as Mendoza and co-director Alex Garland drew from the testimonies of his fellow soldiers when writing the script. The stylistic choices—like a thundering sound mix and infrequent use of satellite photography—add an extra layer of tension to Warfare's depiction of courage under fire. It’s one of the most visceral movies in recent memory about modern war.

Before he brought us high-octane visions of robot cops and ambitious showgirls, Paul Verhoeven directed a handful of smash hits in his native Netherlands, including this Golden Globe-nominated war epic about the diverging fates of Dutch students during the Nazi occupation. Led by a supremely dashing Rutger Hauer, Soldier of Orange begins in 1938 and follows the wartime trajectory of Erik Lanshof, an initially neutral but increasingly principled aristocrat who protects his Dutch Resistance friends and distances himself from fascist collaborators. Before long, his entire school group has pledged one type of loyalty or another. Observed over a period of time, war reveals everything, and Erik’s blossoming heroism emerges as a result of his friendships all changing—for better or worse.

Forgive us for taking a creative approach to the headline so early in the list: Hell in the Pacific features no battles or squadron camaraderie, and takes place on a deserted island with only two characters—an American pilot (Lee Marvin) and a Japanese army captain (Toshiro Mifune)—who don’t understand a word the other person says. Even without comprehension, the American and Japanese soldiers find a hundred ways to rile each other up, including shock tactics, sabotage, pranks, and even makeshift imprisonment. Gradually, they realize each other’s company is the strongest connection to their starving humanity available to them, even if they’re affected in a way they can’t explain. Free from the rhetoric and politics of war, Marvin and Mifune play men who slip into a state of brash, stubborn childishness, and a brotherly kinship forms in spite of their inherent opposition.

Clint Eastwood and Steven Spielberg sound like an odd pair to collaborate on a war picture, but Eastwood’s pared-down, melancholic visual style is a terrific fit for this post-Band of Brothers war movie. Flags of Our Fathers wasn’t received as positively as its Japanese-language companion film Letters from Iwo Jima, but it’s a robust and affecting watch—splitting its runtime between the overwhelming Battle of Iwo Jima and its aftermath, where three soldiers celebrated as the iconic “flag-raisers” spent the final year of the Pacific Campaign on a spiritually draining propaganda tour. Flags of Our Fathers takes the essential themes of war cinema—brotherhood, sacrifice, heroism—and juxtaposes them with the United States military manufacturing their own national images and narratives in real time. The tension between the brutal experience of being a brother-in-arms and the sanitized version promoted by war’s survivors makes for a powerful watch.

It’s as if director David Ayer (Suicide Squad, Bright) made a WWII movie to hammer home two points: tank warfare was a nightmare, and soldier hazing was way more hardcore than what you see in Band of Brothers. In the final months of the war in Europe, Wardaddy (Brad Pitt) rolls his bloody and battered tank unit slowly toward Berlin, and the arrival of fresh-faced pencil-pusher Norman (Logan Lerman) gives the hardened squad a chance to berate and bully the newcomer about all the ways he is soft, and war is hell. As their fellow brothers-in-arms are increasingly blasted apart into viscera, Wardaddy’s take-no-shit squad bolsters their unsentimental unity, and each of the five traumatized brothers rely on each other to watch their back and keep them sane on a frontline that might as well be the border to hell.

Spike Lee’s bracing film about the scars of Vietnam takes a familiar concept—a jaded band of veterans who try to pull off a heist—and spins it in a thorny, unpredictable direction. Four older Black veterans (led by an intense, captivating, and Oscar-snubbed Delroy Lindo) return to ‘Nam to recover the CIA gold their squadron hid during the war—aside from a delayed payday, it’s also the ultimate memorial of their late squad leader Norman (Chadwick Boseman). Marked by years of bitterness and PTSD, the “Bloods” retrace their steps while juggling painful reflections on how the war left them angry and guilty. When they're ambushed in the jungle and have to pick up guns again, we're reminded that Lee is never satisfied sticking in one narrative mode for too long, but the jagged catharsis of cranky, once-loyal soldiers confronting their wounds is far more visceral to watch.

When Private Alex (Harry Styles) says, “We're regimental brothers, mate,” in the belly of a fishing trawler on Dunkirk Beach, he’s not acting nobly—he’s making the case for throwing a soldier he thinks is German (he’s actually French) to the Nazis surrounding them. So much of Christopher Nolan’s war film about the perilous evacuation of Allied troops from France’s northernmost beaches centers on division: soldier feuds, breakdowns in communication, and a nonlinear narrative that takes place over a week or an hour depending on who’s in front of the camera. But Dunkirk’s staggering disaster and survival action builds to an overwhelming climactic sense of unity. Across every mini-arc and timeline, Nolan shows us countless pairs of soldiers, pilots, and civilians who stand together as symbolic brothers.

After the famously gnarly and disorienting D-Day beach landing sequence—which has influenced every WWII action movie since, including The Mosquito Bowl—Saving Private Ryan settles into the most iconic band of brothers story outside of… well, Band of Brothers, co-created by this film’s director and star. Tom Hanks is the grouchy uncle leading a reluctant squad to rescue Private James Francis Ryan (Matt Damon) from the front lines after his brothers were killed in action. The squadron's trudge through France is fueled with banter, grumbling, and the type of resentment that stews between siblings who hate when someone receives special treatment. Steven Spielberg’s gift for balancing movie-star gravitas and off-the-cuff charisma is on full display in the ensemble, but no gruff, hot-tempered soldier has a response to Private Ryan's justification for not returning to his mom: “Tell her that when you found me, I was here, and I was with the only brothers I have left.”

The only war film on this list to be set before the 20th century and at sea, the Napoleonic Wars-era Master and Commander has a reputation for being a “dudes/gentlemen rock” classic: the reputation still holds up. Under the command of Captain Jack Aubrey (Russell Crowe), the HMS Surprise wages war against a French frigate that taunts the crew across the seas. Majestic naval battles aside, much of Master and Commander has a warm hangout tone, focusing on the kinship between Aubrey and ship’s surgeon Maturin, their interest in classical music and botanical science, and revelrous suppers in the captain’s quarters. It's impossible to watch Peter Weir’s sea epic and not pine for the brotherly loyalty (maybe not the seasickness or cannonball attacks) that Aubrey inspires here. Nothing will make you feel the romantic thrill of adventure like Master and Commander.

Pick your favorite scene from The Deer Hunter: the riotous wedding party, the spontaneous Frankie Valli singalong over a game of pool, or the exacting game of Russian roulette played by prisoners of war. They each shine a light on the ordinary, working-class brotherhood that was completely transformed by the Vietnam War. This Oscar-winning epic builds complex, traumatized characters (played by Robert De Niro, John Cazale, and Christopher Walken) who reckon with never being able to experience their relationships and community in the same way again. The Deer Hunter paints such a disturbing and fragile picture of the “before and after” of Vietnam, as each brotherly bond becomes charged with alienation and despair. Notorious for its multiple “death game” sequences, The Deer Hunter excels in making its harrowing portrayal of war and PTSD genuinely heartbreaking.

One of the best things to come out of legendary Hong Kong filmmakers John Woo and Tsui Hark falling out was the future director of Hard Boiled and Face/Off leaving the iconic A Better Tomorrow series to make this searing war story. Beginning in the late ’60s, it focuses on three close friends (Tony Leung, Jacky Cheung, Waise Lee) whose smuggling ambitions dunk them headfirst in the brutality and desperation of the Vietnam War. Bullet in the Head blends the epic sweep of New Hollywood war epics (Apocalypse Now, The Deer Hunter) with Woo’s “heroic bloodshed” toolkit, which meant electric action spectacle and bold, tragic melodrama. An underrated operatic beast in Woo’s stacked filmography—not to mention a personal one, as Woo used the film to express his reaction to the 1989 Tiananmen Square massacre—the action is always in service of a high-stakes tri-friendship that can only take so much passion and betrayal before the treasured brotherhood breaks.
