From maintenance windows to DNS faults: 10 causes of a bank app outage

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 causes of a bank app outage, from bad software updates to cloud failures and cyberattacks, explain why your banking app sometimes stops working

A banking app is a window, not a vault. The screen on a phone shows balances and sends payment instructions, but almost none of the actual work happens there. Each tap starts a request that travels through a chain of systems, and the bank controls only part of that chain.

The chain has several layers. The app on the phone talks to the bank's servers through application programming interfaces, or APIs, which pass requests back and forth in a structured format. Those servers draw on the core banking system, the ledger of record for accounts, balances and transactions. Around them sit outside vendors that handle tasks such as identity checks, one-time passcodes and fraud screening. Many banks also rent computing capacity from cloud providers or run their own data centers. Card payments and transfers move over shared payment networks the bank does not run. Connecting all of it is the public internet, which depends on the Domain Name System and on routing protocols to deliver traffic to the right place.

A fault in any one of those layers can produce the same result for the customer. The app freezes on a loading wheel, rejects a login or shows a generic "something went wrong" message. From the outside, a broken card network and a failed server update can look identical. That is one reason a bank app outage can be hard to diagnose, both for customers and for the bank's own engineers working to restore service.

Banks cause some outages on purpose. They take systems offline to install security patches or upgrade software, often at hours when fewer people are using them. Other outages come without warning: a release with a bug, a rush of customers on payday, a hardware failure in a data center or an attack meant to overwhelm servers. Some are partial. A customer may see a balance but be unable to move money.

The slides that follow explain 10 distinct causes of banking app problems, from scheduled maintenance to cyberattacks and aging infrastructure. Each one describes how the mechanism works and what it tends to look like on a customer's screen. They are a selection of mechanisms rather than a complete list, and they are not ranked.

Scheduled maintenance is downtime a bank chooses. Banks switch systems off on purpose to apply security fixes, upgrade software or migrate data to new platforms, and they often warn customers days ahead.

Not every change can be made while a system is running. Updating a database structure, replacing hardware or switching to a new version of core software can require pausing new transactions. A short pause keeps records consistent. It prevents a payment from being half-recorded in the old system and half-recorded in the new one.

Banks usually book this work for overnight hours or early weekend mornings in their home time zone, when activity is lightest. For a bank with customers abroad, a quiet 2 a.m. window at home can land in the middle of the working day somewhere else.

Planned work does not always switch off the entire app. A bank may leave balances and transaction history on screen while it pauses transfers, bill payments or new account applications. This setup is known as read-only mode, because data can be viewed but not changed. At other times, login itself is disabled, and the app shows a message that service is temporarily unavailable.

Notices typically appear in several places. Common channels include a banner inside the app, a message on the bank's website, an email to customers and posts on the bank's social media accounts. Some banks also run a dedicated service status page that lists current and upcoming disruptions. Many notices give a start time and an expected end time for the window.

A maintenance window can run longer than announced. If an upgrade step fails, engineers may need to reverse the change and restore the earlier version before services return. That recovery work stretches the outage past the time customers were told to expect.

For customers, the practical effect comes down to timing. A transfer or bill payment started just before a window opens may wait in a queue until systems come back online. A posted notice is often the quickest way to tell planned downtime apart from an unexpected failure.

A bank's core banking system is its master record of accounts, balances and transactions. When that core fails or slows down, the app cannot show or move money, even if the app itself is working normally.

A core system does more than store numbers. It opens and closes accounts, posts deposits and withdrawals, calculates interest, applies fees and tracks loan repayments. It is the official record of how much money each customer has.

The mobile app keeps little of that data on the phone. When a customer opens it, the app sends a request through the bank's servers and connecting software to the core. It then displays whatever comes back. The same core typically serves online banking, branch terminals, ATMs and call center staff. That shared dependence offers one clue to a core problem. Several channels tend to fail at the same time, not just the app.

Many cores do not finalize every transaction the instant it happens. Some work is posted in batches, often in an end-of-day run that brings the books into line. Between runs, a bank may track activity in a separate layer. That layer records pending items and adjusts the available balance a customer sees. If it falls out of step with the ledger, the gap shows up on the customer's screen.

The symptoms are often subtle. A balance may look stale, or stop updating after a purchase. A transaction can sit as pending longer than usual, appear twice, or vanish and return. Transfers may be accepted on screen but held before they move. In a deeper failure, the app may show an error in place of a balance. It may also switch to a view-only mode that hides payment options.

A core is hard to switch over to a backup. A website can run on many identical servers, and traffic can shift to a healthy one. A ledger must stay consistent. Two copies cannot both accept transactions without risking conflicting balances. Before a backup takes over, the bank has to confirm that every recorded transaction has carried across.

That caution also shapes recovery. After service returns, staff may need to reconcile transactions that arrived during the disruption. Balances can look wrong for a while as that work finishes, even though the app appears normal again.

A bank app can break the moment new code goes live. A faulty release can stop logins, block payments or scramble what appears on screen, whether it is pushed to the bank's own servers or to the app on customers' phones.

Two kinds of release matter here. Server-side changes update the systems the app talks to behind the scenes. A bank can deploy them at any time, and every customer feels the effect at once. It does not matter which version of the app sits on a given phone. A bug introduced this way can reach the whole customer base within minutes.

App releases work differently. A new version of an iPhone or Android app goes out through Apple $AAPL's App Store or Google $GOOGL Play. Each person installs it on their own schedule unless automatic updates are switched on. As a result, one customer can have a working app while another, on a newer or older version, sees errors. Support teams often ask which version a customer is running for this reason.

Not every bad deployment involves broken code. A configuration error can do similar damage. Examples include a wrong setting, a timeout set too short or a database address pointing to the wrong place. Changes like these are small and quick to make. That speed also makes them easy to get wrong.

When a release goes wrong, the usual first response is a rollback, which restores the previous working version. Server-side rollbacks can be fast if the team planned for them. App rollbacks are harder. A developer cannot simply pull a version back off phones that have already installed it. The fix usually arrives as another update.

Database changes complicate matters further. If a release altered how data is stored, reverting the code may not reverse that change. The older code may then fail to read the newer data format.

Engineering teams use several practices to limit the damage. A staged rollout sends a change to a small share of users first, then widens it if error rates stay normal. Google Play supports staged rollouts by percentage of users. Apple offers a phased release that spreads automatic updates over seven days.

Feature flags let a team ship code with a new function switched off. The team can then turn it on, or off again, without publishing a new release. Canary deployments route a slice of server traffic to the new version while the rest stays on the old one.

Testing catches many problems before launch, but a test environment is not an exact copy of production. Real customer data, real traffic and the wide variety of phones in use can expose bugs that tests missed.

For customers, timing is the clue. Problems that appear right after an app update, or that affect only people on one version, point toward a bad release. A wider system failure tends to hit everyone regardless of version.

A bank app can slow down or fail when too many customers use it at once. Predictable peaks such as paydays, benefit payment dates, tax deadlines and holiday shopping can push demand past the capacity a bank's systems were built to handle.

Many of these spikes follow the calendar. Direct deposits often post early in the morning, and many customers open their apps soon after to check that the money arrived. Government benefit payments also arrive on fixed dates. In the U.S., the federal income tax filing deadline usually falls in mid-April. In the U.K., the online deadline for Self Assessment returns is Jan. 31. Tax payments cluster around both deadlines.

Heavy load shows up first as slowness. Each server can only handle so many open connections and database queries at the same time. Once those limits are reached, new requests wait in line. If they wait too long, they time out, and the app displays a generic error.

Some banks use autoscaling, which adds server capacity automatically as traffic rises. It has limits. New servers take time to start, and a sudden spike can outrun them. Autoscaling also tends to cover the front-end layer that handles app requests, not every system behind it. A database or core ledger with fixed capacity can become the bottleneck. Extra front-end servers can then send it even more requests.

To protect those downstream systems, banks use rate limiting and queueing. Rate limiting caps how many requests a system accepts in a given period and turns the rest away. Customers often see this as a "try again later" message. Queueing holds requests and processes them in order. That can delay a transfer without losing it.

The app may still open normally while transactions fail. Balances and recent activity can be served from a cache, a stored copy of recent data that is cheap to deliver. A payment or transfer has to reach the systems that actually move money. Those are the systems under the most strain during a surge.

Retries can make a surge worse. When an app is slow, customers tap refresh or try a payment again, and software may also retry failed requests on its own. Each attempt adds load, a pattern engineers call a retry storm. Many apps space out automatic retries, a technique called exponential backoff, to limit the effect. Banks also load-test their systems and plan capacity around previous peaks. A peak larger than any seen before can still exceed that plan.

A bank app can go dark when the computing infrastructure underneath it fails. Banks host their services in their own data centers, on outside cloud platforms or on a mix of both. A power, hardware or network fault in any of those places can take the app down with it.

A data center is a building filled with servers, storage and networking equipment. It depends on a steady supply of electricity, cooling and network connections. Facilities usually carry backup batteries and generators to ride through a grid failure. If a cooling system breaks or a switch to backup power fails, servers may shut down to protect themselves. Every service running on that hardware stops with them.

Cloud providers such as Amazon $AMZN Web Services, Microsoft $MSFT Azure and Google $GOOGL Cloud rent this kind of capacity to customers. They divide their infrastructure into geographic regions. Each region contains several separate facilities, often called availability zones, with their own power and networking. A bank that spreads its app across multiple zones can usually survive the loss of one. A fault that hits an entire region is harder to escape. The same is true of a shared service the provider runs across regions.

Redundancy is the standard defense. A bank may copy data continuously to a second site and keep standby systems ready to take over. That handoff is called failover. It does not always work cleanly. A backup site may lack the capacity to carry full traffic. Data may not have finished copying when the primary site goes down. A bank also has to confirm that balances are consistent before it resumes moving money. That check can stretch out recovery.

Shared dependence creates a different kind of risk. When many companies rely on the same provider, one fault can disrupt unrelated businesses at once. A customer may find a bank app, a streaming service and an airline website failing on the same morning. That pattern often points to shared infrastructure rather than any single company's code.

Many banks run hybrid setups. They keep core records in their own data centers while running the app's front end, analytics or customer messaging in the cloud. Each link between those environments is another point of failure. If the connection between the cloud-hosted app and the bank's on-premise systems breaks, the app may open but fail to load accounts.

To a customer, a hosting outage looks like most other failures — a spinning wheel, an error message or a blank screen. Status pages run by the bank or by the cloud provider are often where an infrastructure problem is first confirmed.

A banking app relies on outside companies for jobs such as identity verification, one-time passcode delivery, fraud screening and account data aggregation. When one of those vendors fails, customers can lose access to login or a single feature, even while the bank's own systems run normally.

These connections run through application programming interfaces, or APIs. An API is a defined way for one piece of software to request data or an action from another. The banking app sends a request to a vendor's API, waits for a reply and then decides what to show the customer. If the reply never comes, arrives late or returns an error, the app has nothing useful to display.

Login is one place where this dependency becomes visible. Many banks send a one-time passcode by text message or email as a second step after a password. That message usually passes through a messaging provider and then a mobile carrier before it reaches the phone. A delay anywhere in that chain can mean the code expires or never arrives. The customer then cannot finish signing in.

Identity checks are another pressure point. Opening an account or adding a new device can trigger a check with an outside identity verification service. Some of these services compare a photo of an ID document with a selfie. If the service is unavailable, the app may stall at the verification step or ask the customer to try again later.

Fraud screening works in the background. Some banks pass login attempts or transactions to an outside fraud detection system that scores the risk before approval. When that scoring service times out, the bank has to decide whether to block the activity or let it through without a score. A bank configured to default to caution blocks it. The customer sees that as a refusal or an error message.

Data aggregation links run in the other direction. Budgeting apps, lenders and investment platforms often connect to bank accounts through an aggregator that pulls balances and transaction history. When the aggregator's connection breaks, the outside app shows stale data or asks the user to reconnect. The bank's own app may show the same account correctly the whole time.

The common thread is a split between where the failure happens and where the customer notices it. The bank's servers and ledger can be healthy while the broken part belongs to a different company. That split can also slow the repair. Bank support staff may be able to confirm the problem, but restoring service depends on the vendor.

Card payments and bank transfers travel over networks that sit outside a bank's own systems. When a card network, a payment processor or a transfer system has a fault, payments can fail even while the bank app opens and shows balances normally.

A single card tap or swipe passes through several separate parties. The merchant's terminal sends the transaction to the merchant's payment processor and acquiring bank. From there, it travels over a card network such as Visa $V or Mastercard $MA. The network routes it to the issuing bank, which holds the cardholder's account and decides whether to approve the payment.

That approval step is called authorization. It takes seconds and confirms that the account exists and has enough funds or available credit. Settlement comes later. It is the process in which money actually moves between the banks involved, usually in batches. A fault at the authorization stage shows up at once as a declined card. A fault at the settlement stage may surface later, as a transaction that stays pending longer than expected.

Each link in that chain is a possible point of failure. The location of the fault shapes who is affected. A processor problem can stop one group of merchants from accepting cards while shops using a different processor carry on. A card network problem can hit cardholders at many banks at the same time. An issuer problem is limited to that bank's own customers.

Bank transfers run on separate rails. In the U.S., many direct deposits, bill payments and account-to-account transfers move over the Automated Clearing House network, known as ACH. In the U.K., the Faster Payments system carries near-instant transfers between accounts at participating banks. If one of these systems slows or stops, a transfer submitted in the app may be accepted but not arrive on time.

Payment network disruptions therefore often look like an app failure. The app may log in, display balances and let a customer start a payment. The problem appears only at the final step, as a decline, an error message or a transfer stuck in processing. That error shows up inside the bank's app, even when the fault sits with a network several steps away.

Banks cannot repair an outside network themselves. They can pause affected features, post service notices and use backup routes where those exist. Card networks also offer a fallback called stand-in processing. When an issuer's systems cannot be reached, the network can approve or decline transactions on the issuer's behalf. It does so within limits the issuer has set in advance.

A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack floods a bank's systems with junk requests until real customers can't get through. Banks may also take their own apps offline on purpose to contain a suspected breach. Either way, the customer is locked out, but the cause behind the error screen is very different.

The "distributed" part is what makes these attacks hard to stop. The traffic does not come from one computer. It arrives from thousands of machines at once. Those machines usually belong to a botnet — a network of hijacked devices that an attacker controls remotely. Infected home routers, security cameras and other internet-connected gadgets often serve as recruits. Their owners rarely know it.

Attackers aim at different layers of a bank's setup. Some attacks try to saturate the network connection itself with sheer volume. Others target the protocols computers use to open connections, using up a server's capacity to track them. A third kind mimics ordinary app behavior, such as repeated login attempts or balance requests. That last type is harder to spot because each request looks legitimate on its own.

Banks defend against floods mainly by filtering traffic before it reaches their servers. Specialist mitigation services route incoming traffic through scrubbing centers. Those systems drop requests that match attack patterns and pass clean traffic onward. Content delivery networks spread load across many locations, so no single site absorbs the full flood. These tools reduce the damage. A large or novel attack can still slow an app or knock it offline for a time.

Intent and composition separate an attack from a legitimate rush of customers. A payday spike is real people trying to complete real tasks. A DDoS flood is manufactured traffic built to exhaust resources, and most of it never completes a transaction. On the customer's screen, though, both show up as the same stalled loading wheel.

Not every cyberattack outage comes from a flood. A bank that detects a possible intrusion, such as malware on internal systems or unauthorized access, may deliberately shut down online and mobile services. Isolating affected systems stops an attacker from moving further. It also protects customer data while investigators work. Ransomware, which encrypts files and demands payment for their release, can force the same kind of shutdown.

Outages tied to a breach tend to last longer than flood-driven ones. A DDoS attack usually ends when the traffic stops or is filtered out. An intrusion requires forensic work before anything comes back online. Restoring service means confirming the attacker is gone, checking that data has not been altered and rebuilding systems from clean backups.

A DDoS attack on its own does not mean customer accounts have been accessed. The goal of a flood is disruption, not theft. Attackers sometimes use one as cover for other activity, though. Banks therefore continue to investigate after the traffic subsides. A bank's official website and verified social media accounts are where it confirms what kind of incident occurred.

A bank app can be running normally and still be unreachable if the Domain Name System fails to resolve its address. Internet routing errors that send traffic the wrong way have the same effect. In both cases the fault sits between the phone and the bank, not inside either one.

The Domain Name System, or DNS, works like the internet's directory. An app does not reach a server by its name alone. It first asks a DNS resolver to translate a hostname, such as the address of the bank's API, into a numeric IP address. If the bank's authoritative DNS servers go down, or a DNS provider the bank relies on has a fault, that lookup fails. The app then shows a connection error even though the servers behind the name are healthy.

DNS answers are also cached for a set period, known as the time to live. Caching can make a fault uneven. Some users keep working from a stored answer, while others whose cached answer has expired cannot connect. The same mechanism can slow a fix. A corrected record takes time to replace the old one across resolvers.

Routing is a separate layer. The Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, is the system large networks use to tell one another which IP addresses they can reach and by which path. A mistaken or withdrawn BGP announcement can make a bank's addresses vanish from parts of the internet. Traffic can also be steered down a path that leads nowhere. BGP depends heavily on trust between networks. One network's misconfiguration can therefore spread well beyond the network that made it.

Expired certificates and domains cause a related kind of failure. Banking apps connect over encrypted connections that use Transport Layer Security, or TLS, which depends on a valid digital certificate. If that certificate lapses, the app's security checks reject the connection. A lapsed domain registration can break the name entirely.

These faults often hit some customers and spare others. A problem at one internet service provider, one mobile carrier or one region's DNS resolver can leave everyone else unaffected. That is why switching from home Wi-Fi to mobile data sometimes brings a banking app back. The switch sends requests through a different network and often a different resolver. Outage reports during these events can cluster by provider or location rather than spreading evenly across a bank's customers.

Many banks run modern mobile apps on top of systems built decades earlier, joined by layers of connecting software. Those links between old and new are often fragile. That makes every change riskier and every failure slower to diagnose.

Technical debt is the term software engineers use for the accumulated cost of shortcuts and aging design. Ward Cunningham, a programmer, coined the metaphor in the early 1990s. Like financial debt, it carries interest. Each workaround left in place makes the next update harder to build and test.

In banking, much of that debt sits in older programming languages. COBOL, a language designed for business data processing, still runs large amounts of transaction code at banks, insurers and government agencies. Much of that code executes on mainframe computers. These systems were built for an era of branch counters and teller terminals. They were not designed for millions of phones checking balances at all hours.

To bridge the gap, banks add middleware. This is software that translates a request from a modern app into a format an older system understands, then translates the answer back. Over the years, banks stack one layer on another as they add online banking, then mobile apps, then connections for outside fintech services. Each layer is another place where a timeout, a format mismatch or a capacity limit can stop a request.

The fragility shows up during change. An update to one component can break an assumption buried in another component that may not have been reviewed in years. Documentation for older code is often incomplete. Testing every path through a decades-old system is difficult. Some problems surface only after release, when real customers run into them.

Replacing the old systems carries its own risk. A core modernization project can involve moving every customer account and years of transaction history to new software without interrupting service. Banks often run old and new platforms side by side during a migration. That temporarily adds even more integration points. A migration that goes wrong can leave customers unable to log in or see accurate balances.

Staffing adds pressure. Fewer engineers learn COBOL and mainframe operations today than in earlier generations. Many of the specialists who built these systems have retired. When something breaks deep in a legacy stack, the people who understand it may be few. That can turn a short glitch into a longer outage.
