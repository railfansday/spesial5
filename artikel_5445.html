From House charges to a Senate verdict: 10 parts of U.S. impeachment

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn how impeachment works in the U.S., from House charges and articles of impeachment to the Senate trial, the two-thirds conviction vote and what removal means

Impeachment is an accusation, not an eviction. When the House of Representatives impeaches a president or a federal judge, that official stays in office. Removal can come only later, and only if the Senate votes to convict. That distinction is the one most often blurred in headlines, cable segments and social media posts whenever the word starts trending.

The U.S. Constitution splits the job between the two chambers of Congress by design. The House acts something like a grand jury, deciding whether the evidence justifies formal charges. The Senate acts as the court, hearing the case and delivering a verdict. Each chamber has its own vote threshold, its own procedures and its own political pressures. An official can be impeached by the first and still be acquitted by the second.

The process is rare, but it is not theoretical. The House has impeached presidents four times, and three different men have faced a Senate trial as a result. None was removed. Federal judges are a different story: they are the only officials the Senate has ever convicted. Another president left office before the full House could vote at all.

The Constitution sketches the process in a few short clauses. Much of the detail has been filled in by practice. The House writes its own rules for investigating. The Senate sets its own procedures for trying cases. The Constitution never defines the key phrase describing impeachable conduct, and Congress has interpreted it case by case.

Understanding how impeachment works makes those open questions easier to follow. The 10 slides ahead walk through the process in order. They start with what the word means under the Constitution and who can be impeached. They then cover the grounds for charges, how the House opens an inquiry and how it votes on articles. Next come the Senate trial, the two-thirds bar for conviction and the penalties that follow a conviction. The final slides look back at the presidents who have been impeached. They also compare impeachment with two other routes by which a president can lose power: the 25th Amendment and resignation.

Each slide stands on its own. A reader can go straight to the stage of the process that matters most to them.

Impeachment is the formal act of charging a federal official with misconduct. It is not the act of removing that person from office. Under the U.S. Constitution, the House of Representatives brings the charges, and the Senate decides whether they hold up at trial.

News coverage and everyday conversation often use the word loosely, as if it meant being thrown out of office. That reading skips a step. An official who has been impeached has been accused, much as a criminal defendant who has been indicted stands accused. Neither has been found guilty of anything yet.

The comparison to a criminal case holds only in broad outline. Impeachment is a constitutional proceeding run by Congress, not a prosecution in a courtroom. The two chambers set many of their own procedures for it.

The House's authority comes from Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution. That clause gives the House "the sole Power of Impeachment." No other body can bring the charges.

The Senate's role comes from Article I, Section 3. That section gives the Senate "the sole Power to try all Impeachments." Only the Senate can hold the trial, and only the Senate can deliver the verdict.

Both clauses sit in Article I, the part of the Constitution that establishes Congress. That placement puts impeachment in the hands of the legislature. Courts and the executive branch do not bring the charges or decide the outcome.

The split between the two chambers has a practical consequence. An impeachment by the House, on its own, does not change whether the official holds the job. The person stays in office while the Senate considers the case. If the Senate acquits, the official keeps the position. The impeachment itself still remains part of the official record.

This two-stage design is the core of the process. Every later step — the inquiry, the vote on charges, the trial and any penalty — follows from the division of labor written into those two sections of Article I.

The Constitution allows Congress to impeach the president, the vice president and civil officers of the U.S. government. That coverage, set out in Article II, Section 4, also reaches federal judges. Members of Congress, by contrast, are generally not treated as civil officers who can be impeached.

The presidency draws the most attention, but it is one office among several on the list. Article II, Section 4 places the vice president in the same clause as the president. The two highest executive offices therefore face an identical process. Beyond them, the clause extends to the wider category of civil officers serving in the federal government.

Federal judges hold a distinct place on that list. Their position differs from that of elected officials in one basic way. Article III says federal judges hold office during good behavior. That language gives them no fixed term and no reelection campaign to face. Impeachment gives Congress a constitutional route to end that tenure.

The status of lawmakers was tested early in the country's history. In 1797, the House impeached Senator William Blount. The Senate dismissed the case in 1799 on jurisdictional grounds. A dismissal on those grounds turns on whether the Senate had authority over the case, not on whether the accusations were true.

Rank offers no protection under Article II, Section 4. The clause covers the most senior official in the executive branch, and nothing in its text shields any covered officeholder because of seniority. What the clause does limit is the basis for an impeachment. It ties the process to stated grounds, not to the importance of the person accused.

That structure gives the clause two separate filters. The first asks whether a person holds one of the covered offices. The second asks whether the conduct at issue fits the grounds the Constitution names. Both questions apply to every case brought under the clause.

The Constitution allows impeachment on three grounds: treason, bribery and "other high crimes and misdemeanors." It never defines that last phrase. Congress has given it meaning one case at a time.

The list appears in Article II, Section 4. That clause names the officials who can face impeachment and, in the same sentence, the conduct that can lead to it.

Treason and bribery are named outright. The third category works differently. No clause anywhere in the constitutional text supplies a definition, and no list of qualifying offenses accompanies it. A reader looking for a checklist of impeachable conduct will not find one in the document.

That gap leaves interpretation to Congress. When the House weighs articles of impeachment, its members decide whether the conduct described fits the phrase. When the Senate holds a trial, senators reach their own judgment on the same question. Each vote reflects how individual members read the phrase. The same conduct can draw different answers from different lawmakers.

One question has stayed open: whether an impeachable act must also be an indictable crime. An indictable crime is one that could be charged in a criminal court. Scholars and lawmakers have disagreed on this point. One reading limits the phrase to conduct that breaks a criminal law. Another treats it as broad enough to reach conduct that is not a crime. The Constitution does not resolve the dispute.

The two readings lead to different kinds of charges. Under the narrower reading, an article of impeachment would need to describe conduct that a prosecutor could also take to a criminal court. Under the broader reading, an article could rest on conduct such as a misuse of an office's powers, even where no criminal statute covers it.

The disagreement shapes how impeachment debates unfold. A defense that a charge describes no crime is an argument about this phrase. So is a claim that an official's conduct warrants removal whether or not it breaks a criminal law. Both depend on how members of Congress read a single phrase in Article II, Section 4.

An impeachment inquiry begins in the House of Representatives, where any member can introduce a resolution calling for an official to be impeached. The House Judiciary Committee has usually taken charge of the investigation that follows, though the Constitution sets no fixed procedure for this stage.

Filing a resolution is only a starting point. It does not charge anyone, and it does not commit the House to act. A single lawmaker can put one forward, but the chamber decides whether anything comes of it.

The committee's task at this stage is to establish what happened. It holds hearings and gathers evidence, building the factual record that any later charges would rest on.

The full House can also step in directly. Members can vote to authorize an inquiry, putting the chamber's formal backing behind the investigation.

The Constitution is silent on how any of this should be done. It gives the House the power to impeach but says nothing about who investigates, how evidence is collected or what steps must come before a vote. The House fills that gap itself by setting its own rules.

That arrangement means the inquiry stage is shaped by the chamber's choices rather than by a constitutional script. One inquiry can begin with a member's resolution and committee hearings. Another can begin with a floor vote authorizing the investigation. Both paths fit within the House's authority.

The inquiry is also distinct from what comes later. It is a fact-finding phase, not a trial, and it produces no verdict. No official is impeached during an inquiry. Impeachment itself happens only when the full House approves formal charges.

For readers following an impeachment in the news, the word "inquiry" marks this early, investigative step. It signals that the House is examining an official's conduct. It does not mean the House has decided to bring charges, and it does not mean the official faces removal.

The House impeaches an official by approving at least one article of impeachment, the formal written charge against that person. Each article needs a simple majority of House members voting. If all 435 members cast a vote, 218 votes are enough to pass an article.

Each article sets out one specific allegation of misconduct against the named official. A single impeachment resolution can carry one article or several.

The House does not vote on the package as a whole. Members vote on each article on its own. That allows a lawmaker to support one charge and oppose another. It also means a resolution can leave the House with some articles approved and others rejected.

The majority standard is tied to members voting, not to the full size of the chamber. The 218 figure applies only when all 435 seats are filled and every member votes. A vacant seat, after a death or resignation, cannot cast a vote. When fewer members take part, the number needed for a majority falls with them. If 410 members vote, for example, 206 votes would pass an article. A tie vote fails. A majority means more than half of the votes cast.

Approval of one article is enough. Once an article passes, the official has been impeached, regardless of what the Senate later decides.

After the vote, the House names managers to carry the case forward. The managers are themselves members of the House. They act as prosecutors once the matter moves to the Senate. They present the charges contained in the approved articles and argue for conviction.

Only the articles that win a majority travel to the Senate. The House delivers the approved articles to the Senate, which then organizes the trial. A charge the House rejects is not part of the impeachment. The Senate does not weigh it at trial.

A Senate impeachment trial turns senators into jurors who weigh the House's charges and decide whether to convict. House managers argue the case, the official's defense team answers it, and the Senate votes on each article separately.

That jury role begins with an oath or affirmation. Every senator takes one before hearing the case. Outside a trial, senators debate and pass legislation. During one, they sit in judgment of a specific official on specific charges. The affirmation exists as an alternative for senators who prefer not to swear an oath.

Who presides depends on who is on trial. When the president is the defendant, Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution requires the chief justice of the Supreme Court to preside. That requirement applies only to presidential trials. It places the head of the federal judiciary in the chair.

The prosecution comes from the House. Its case is presented by House members known as impeachment managers. In the Senate chamber, those managers lay out the evidence and arguments behind each charge. The accused official is represented by a defense team, which responds to the managers and argues for acquittal. Senators hear both sides before any vote is taken.

The verdict is not a single up-or-down vote on the whole case. Senators vote on each article on its own. The result can differ from one charge to the next. A conviction on any single article is enough to convict the official, even if the other articles fail. Each conviction vote requires two-thirds of the senators present.

Federal courts have left these procedures to the Senate. In Nixon v. United States, decided in 1993, the Supreme Court held that challenges to the Senate's trial procedures were nonjusticiable. In practical terms, that means courts will not hear such disputes. The Senate decides for itself how to run an impeachment trial. The case involved Judge Walter Nixon, not President Richard Nixon.

Convicting an impeached official takes the votes of two-thirds of the senators present, a requirement written into Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution. When all 100 senators vote, at least 67 must vote to convict.

The threshold is measured against attendance, not against the full size of the chamber. An absent senator does not count toward the base used to calculate two-thirds. If 99 senators are present, conviction takes 66 votes. If 98 are present, 65 votes would fall just short of two-thirds, so conviction still takes 66.

The same math shows how few votes it takes to stop a conviction. With every senator voting, 34 votes against an article are enough to defeat it. The blocking number stays at 34 when 99 senators are present, because 66 votes would still be needed to convict. That means a bloc of just over one-third of the chamber can end the case on that charge.

The Senate standard sits well above the simple majority the House uses to impeach. The same two-thirds standard applies whether the accused official is a president, a federal judge or another civil officer.

The higher Senate number has a political consequence. Conviction generally requires votes from senators of both parties. For one party to convict on its own, it would need at least 67 seats, with all 100 senators present and every member of that party voting together.

Seat counts show how much cross-party support a conviction needs. A party holding 50 seats, with all 100 senators present, would need at least 17 votes from outside its ranks. A party holding 60 seats would still need seven. In practice, the side pressing for conviction has to persuade members of the opposing party.

That threshold has never been reached in a presidential case. No president has been convicted by the Senate.

If no article reaches two-thirds, the official is acquitted on every charge and keeps the job.

A Senate conviction removes the official from office. The Senate can also bar that person from holding federal office in the future. The Constitution allows no other penalty in an impeachment case.

Article I, Section 3 limits judgment in cases of impeachment to removal from office and disqualification from holding federal office. The Senate cannot impose a fine, order prison time or seize property as part of an impeachment judgment. An impeachment trial decides whether someone may hold federal office. It does not decide whether that person is punished as a criminal.

Removal comes with the conviction itself. Once the Senate convicts on an article, the official loses the office.

Disqualification works differently. It is optional, and the Senate has decided it through a separate vote. That vote has required only a simple majority. The case of Judge Thomas Porteous shows how the two steps fit together. The Senate removed Porteous from the federal bench in 2010. Then, in its own vote, it barred him from holding future federal office.

The two penalties do different things. Removal ends the official's time in their current post. Disqualification looks forward and closes off future federal office.

Leaving office through impeachment does not end the possibility of criminal charges. Article I, Section 3 states that the convicted party remains "liable and subject to Indictment, Trial, Judgment and Punishment, according to Law." Prosecutors may bring an ordinary criminal case over the same conduct. Such a case would run through the regular courts under criminal law, not under Senate trial rules.

The president's pardon power has a specific limit here. Article II, Section 2 lets the president grant reprieves and pardons for offenses against the U.S., "except in Cases of Impeachment." A president cannot use a pardon to undo an impeachment or reverse a Senate conviction. No pardon can restore a removed official to office or lift a bar on future federal service.

Three U.S. presidents have been impeached by the House of Representatives: Andrew Johnson, Bill Clinton and Donald Trump. The Senate acquitted all three men every time, and none was removed from office.

Andrew Johnson was the first. The House impeached him in 1868, which sent the case to the Senate for trial. Senators acquitted him by a single vote. One more vote to convict would have removed him from office.

Johnson's case stood alone for 130 years. The House did not impeach another president until 1998.

That president was Bill Clinton. The House impeached him on December 19, 1998. His Senate trial ran into the next year. Senators acquitted Clinton on February 12, 1999, less than two months after the House vote. He stayed in office. He remains the only president impeached in the 20th century.

Donald Trump became the third impeached president about 21 years after Clinton. The House first impeached him in December 2019. The Senate acquitted him on February 5, 2020.

The House impeached Trump again on January 13, 2021, roughly 13 months after the first time. The Senate acquitted him one month later, on February 13, 2021. Trump is the only president to face two impeachments. They came in consecutive years, the shortest gap between any two presidential impeachments.

The second trial centered on a single article of impeachment. Senators voted 57 to 43. A majority of the chamber sided with conviction. The tally still fell short of the two-thirds threshold the Constitution sets for a guilty verdict.

Richard Nixon is absent from this list because the full House never voted to impeach him.

Federal judges have a different record. They are the only federal officials the Senate has convicted. The House has impeached 15 federal judges, and eight of them were convicted. The other seven ended without a conviction. No presidential impeachment has ended that way.

Impeachment charges an official with misconduct. The 25th Amendment addresses a president who cannot do the job, and resignation is a voluntary exit. The three are often mentioned together, but they work in different ways and serve different purposes.

The 25th Amendment was ratified in 1967. It governs presidential disability and succession. Its focus is whether the president is able to serve, not how the president has behaved in office.

Section 4 of the amendment allows the vice president and a majority of the cabinet to declare that the president is unable to serve. That decision starts inside the executive branch. Impeachment, by contrast, begins with charges brought by the House of Representatives.

The 25th Amendment is not a punishment process. It does not accuse the president of wrongdoing. It involves no articles, no prosecutors and no trial. An impeachment, on the other hand, is a formal accusation that an official has committed an offense serious enough to justify removal. The 25th Amendment applies only to the president, while impeachment reaches the vice president, federal judges and other civil officers as well.

Resignation works differently from both. When an official steps down, the impeachment process usually ends without a Senate verdict. No senator votes to convict or acquit. The question of guilt is never formally resolved.

Richard Nixon followed this path. He resigned on August 9, 1974. By then, the House Judiciary Committee had approved articles of impeachment against him. The full House never held a floor vote on those articles. Nixon was never impeached by the full House.

Resignation does not always stop Congress, though. In 1876, the House impeached William Belknap, the former secretary of war, despite his resignation. The case went to a Senate trial, and the Senate acquitted him.

Of the three routes, impeachment is the only one that runs through both chambers of Congress. It is also the only one that ends in a vote on specific charges.
