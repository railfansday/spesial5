Sunrise On The Reaping's 10 Biggest Changes To The Hunger Games Book

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Warning! This post contains SPOILERS for The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping.

The newest addition to the Hunger Games franchise, The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, has officially hit theaters, and while the movie's Rotten Tomatoes scores currently reflect the fact that it's a hit, at least in terms of the history of this franchise, fans will no doubt be pretty surprised to realize how much of the book was altered or cut. Reviews of Sunrise on the Reaping have highlighted the film's dedication to the original story, and for the most part, that's fair. The movie is loyal to Suzanne Collins work in myriad ways.

However, that doesn't mean that all the major plot points, scenes, and highly anticipated moments were included. On the contrary, there is a frankly shocking number of alterations to the source material, some of them quite significant, and they really do change the story at various points. From changes to the Katniss and Peeta cameo to a complete overhaul of the book's approach to the violence, here are the 10 biggest changes the Sunrise on the Reaping movie makes.

Although Haymitch suffers many brutalities and losses in Sunrise on the Reaping, easily one of the biggest is the loss of his girlfriend, Lenore Dove. In both the book and the movie, Lenore Dove dies because Snow is seeking to punish Haymitch, and he does so, in part, by having Haymitch unknowingly feed Lenore Dove poison gumdrops. Even worse, Haymitch realizes what is happening before Lenore Dove dies, but he can't do anything to save her; he just sits helplessly as she dies in his arms.

For the most part, those story beats remain the same in Sunrise on the Reaping. However, in the book, Haymitch's grieving looks different. Specifically, in the book, Haymitch ends up spending an entire day sleeping on her grave. The book specifies that he does this from sun up to sun down to sun up again. In the movie, his grief is still incredibly evident, but audiences don't see him unable to move from atop her grave as he does in the book, and that no doubt would have packed an even greater emotional punch.

Along with the way Haymitch grieves being slightly different in the movie, the conversation that President Snow and Haymitch have about Lenore Dove before the Hunger Games begin is altered in the movie. In the book, Snow is only aware that Lenore Dove is Covey after watching the Reaping, and he unsettles Haymitch by asking what her color name is, making it clear that he knows of the Covey (which, Hunger Games fans will immediately understand, is because of his own prior romance with Lucy Gray).

In the movie, however, Snow reveals that he already knows exactly who Lenore Dove is. While it might seem like a small alteration, this does significantly reframe the nature of their conversation. And, in fact, those differences are only all the greater because, in the book, it is President Snow, in this scene, who reveals that Lenore Dove wrote an inscription on the back of the flint striker she had gifted him for his birthday. In the movie, Haymitch already knows.

Yet another change that specifically connects to the Covey, in the book, Haymitch actually gets to see Lucy Gray's Hunger Games, becoming the only person anywhere around his age who has done so. He is actually forced to watch them after he makes it out of the arena, in a sequence that is entirely removed from the movie (for example, after surviving the arena, Haymitch is also forced to attend a party and then is stuck inside of a golden cage while people from the Capitol feed him).

Audiences were hoping to see a shot of Lucy Gray because of this, but she never appears in the movie. This is, honestly, a surprising choice for the movie, as it would no doubt have been very easy to incorporate that footage from The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Sonbirds and Snakes. It actually seemed like a no-brainer to incorporate this, so the decision not to remains a mystery. Perhaps, it was simply a matter of cutting down the time.

Lucy Gray wasn't the only former District 12 victor that appeared in the book; all four actually played a role in the movie. For Katniss Everdeen and Peeta Mellark, in both the book and the movie, that included an appearance in the epilogue, which is set after the original Hunger Games trilogy. The movie largely keeps what was included in the book, with the trio sharing the screen again as proof that they have, finally, truly survived the Hunger Games and the Capitol.

However, fans of the novel will note that there were some changes here. Among the most significant was the removal of Katniss bringing goose eggs to Haymitch so that he could raise them. The novel confirms that this is because Haymitch has shared that his lost love, Lenore Dove, cared for geese. Because of that emotional significance, many fans were expecting to see this moment; however, the film opted not to include it.

Perhaps almost as anticipated as the scene in which Katniss presents Haymitch with goose eggs was the scene from the book in which Haymitch overhears Silka—the career, who is ultimately the one he ends up going head-to-head with in the final fight—crying and drops some chocolate for her. For those who haven't read the book, that might seem like it isn't the biggest deal, but this was actually an incredibly important moment in the book.

Specifically, this moment was meant to remind audiences that even the careers are still just scared kids in the Hunger Games, who are doing what they think they have to do to survive. It's easy to forget that, whether it's on the page or on the screen, because the careers in particular are depicted as so ruthless. That was exactly why this scene mattered, though, and it's a shame that it was cut. As a result, Silka doesn't read as a sympathetic character, at least not nearly to the same degree, and that's a disappointing change.

Yet another major book moment that Sunrise on the Reaping fans will be shocked to see excluded from the movie is the scene in which Maritte (a District 4 career tribute who is barely in the movie) and Maysilee Donner kill three Gamemakers who have entered the arena during the Hunger Games. This is a massive event in the book, for several reasons. For one, this is a monumental and jarring thing for the tributes to do, as its such open rebellion against the Capitol—not shocking in the case of Maysilee, but much more shocking in the case of Maritte.

For another, in the book, this becomes a serious moment of tension between Haymitch and Maysilee, as Haymitch fails to act, and Maysilee, understandably, is furious, pointing out that it should be Haymitch fighting to protect her, not Maritte. Additionally, though, in the book, this action is effectively why Maysilee is marked for death in the Games. In the movie, that's positioned largely as something that's done just to punish Haymitch. That's also a shame, as it takes away some of Maysilee's agency.

Among the more disturbing plot points of Sunrise on the Reaping is that, after District 12's Louella, also called Haymitch's sweetheart throughout the book, dies in the chariot accident before the Games, she is replaced by a body double who insists she is Louella. Of course, she isn't, and that's just the Capitol being more evil than ever, but the tributes have to pretend it is. Ultimately, they call her Lou Lou and accept her as their own.

While that basic arc is kept, many of the major details about Lou Lou aren't. For one, the book reveals that Lou Lou is from District 11 because she recognizes songs from that district. More significantly, in the book, Lou Lou has an ear piece through which the Capitol can listen to and control her and a drug pump attached to her chest for them to subdue her. In the movie, she has neither, which already takes some of the bite out of how horrific the Capitol is. This also means Haymitch doesn't mercy kill her by pulling her pump out in the movie, as he does in the book.

Shockingly, the Sunrise on the Reaping movie also doesn't show the scores of the tributes, despite that being a massive part of the original Hunger Games trilogy and a significant part of the book, nor does it really emphasize sponsors or gifts during the Hunger Games at all. In terms of the former, that is genuinely surprising because Haymitch receiving a 1 as his score is a major plot detail in the book (and proves that the Capitol had it out for him right from the jump, which everyone in Panem knew as soon as that 1 popped up).

Additionally, sponsors and gifts are a huge part of the Hunger Games, and in the Sunrise on the Reaping book in particular, they are prominent in part because Haymitch is consistently sent gifts in the arena (including the chocolate that, in the book, he drops to Silka). This leads to multiple very sweet moments between Haymitch and Maysilee in which they share a picnic, with Haymitch even giving Maysilee the elegant glass he received in one gift.

Coinciding with the absence of the gifts in the arena was the absence of many key moments between Haymitch and Maysilee in the books. Yes, in the movie it's still clear that they become like siblings and have a genuine love for one another, but in the book, they actually say this directly, with Maysilee at one point telling Haymitch that she will be his sister. They also go through myriad other situations together, only some of which is captured in the movie.

Their time together in the arena is actually cut so short that audiences don't even see things like one keeping watch while the other sleeps, because there simply isn't enough time passing on screen for that. This is yet another huge surprise, not only because it's one of fans' most beloved aspects of the book but also because Mckenna Grace and Joseph Zada are incredibly talented actors with plenty of fan backing and great chemistry on screen; it would have been brilliant to see more of these scenes with them.

Finally, the single biggest difference between Sunrise on the Reaping as a book and a movie is the level of blood and gore—and that extends throughout the entire movie. For example, when the squirrels attack Ampert in the book, it is said that only a skeleton remained; there was no flesh or muscle left on him. In the movie, however, he really only has (visually, at least) superficial wounds, and he's certainly not just a skeleton. Likewise, in the book, Louella's head is described as gushing blood, and Haymitch is described as being soaked in it.

As with Ampert, though, in the movie, Louella has injuries, but they are far from gruesome. Perhaps the most notable change in this vein, however, is the fact that Silka decapitates Wellie and holds up her head in the novel. In the movie, Silka kills her, but it is absolutely nothing like this gruesome scene in the book. On the whole, it seems the movie wanted to make the movie more approriate for younger audiences, but that's a true letdown, not only because it goes against what the franchise is meant to show but also because it's frankly essential to The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping.
