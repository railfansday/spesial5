In France, gardeners and farmers have a saying that November 25 – the day Saint Catherine is celebrated – is the ideal day to plant a tree. ("À la Sainte-Catherine, tout bois prend racine.”) That may not apply literally to every tree species, but I can tell you this much: November is an excellent time to plant many kinds of fruit trees as long as the ground is not frozen.

Growing fruit trees is a gift to yourself that keeps on giving. Each tree in your home orchard can provide delicious homegrown harvests for years to come. Planting fruit trees in November offers many benefits. The cooler late-fall weather reduces transplanting stress, and seasonal rains keep the soil moist. That helps the trees establish roots before spring growth begins.

Which fruit trees should top your list? Many wonderful fruit trees are great to plant in November, so choose the one that grows your favorite fruit. But be sure to consider your local climate. Each fruit tree species has its own hardiness zone that indicates its cold tolerance. Some – like apples and pears – also have winter chilling requirements. You’ll also want to consider the mature size of the tree and be certain it will fit into your landscape.

Does life get any better than wandering out to the backyard and pulling a crisp apple right off your own tree and taking a bite? So when you consider planting a tree in November, don’t overlook apples. Apple trees can thrive in USDA hardiness zones 3–8, depending on the variety. You can plant either a bare-root apple tree or a container-grown tree in November, as long as the soil is not frozen, although spring planting is often safer in colder zones 3–4.

There are many apple varieties to choose from. Most won’t fruit reliably on their own, so check their pollination requirements when you purchase your tree. Your tree may require a compatible cross-pollinator that flowers at the same time. For a great pairing, try a ‘Honeycrisp’ apple tree alongside a ‘Gala’ apple tree – both available from Trees.com – which can cross-pollinate and give you two delicious varieties to harvest. If you only have room for one tree, ‘Golden Delicious’ is partially self-fertile and can produce fruit without a partner, although you'll generally get a better crop with cross-pollination.

Plant your apple tree in a full sun location in well-draining soil. You may want to stake the tree to prevent any windrock until its roots anchor it well. You can buy a tree-staking kit from Amazon, but if your area is very windy, a crossbar attached to two posts will provide more support.

Pears feel like a fancier fruit than apples, but the trees are just as easy to grow and maintain. Like pear slices in your salad? With your own pear tree, you can pick sweet fruit anytime during the harvest season. Pear trees can thrive in USDA hardiness zones 3–10, depending on the variety.

Like apple trees, pear trees grow best in a full sun location in well-draining soil. As with most apple trees, pear trees are not usually self-pollinating. That means that you will need two compatible cultivars for reliable pollination and fruit set. However, there are exceptions. Both the ‘Bartlett’ pear tree and ‘Kieffer’ pear tree, available from Garden Goods Direct, are partially self-fertile, although they generally produce better crops with a suitable pollination partner. Kieffer also offers good resistance to fire blight, making it worth considering in areas where this disease is a concern.

November can be a good time to plant a bare-root pear tree in milder regions. The plant is dormant, but its root system can continue to grow during the winter as long as the soil remains sufficiently warm and unfrozen. It is also possible to plant container trees in November. Be sure the planting hole is several times as wide as the root system, but no deeper than the root ball.

If you want a tree with sweet, juicy fruit, consider planting a plum tree. The fruit is delicious when eaten fresh, but also makes great compotes or jams. Plum trees can thrive in USDA hardiness zones 4–9, depending on the variety. November is a good month to plant bare-root plum trees in milder regions, and it’s suitable for container-grown plum trees as well. Just don’t wait too long since you can't plant after the ground is frozen. In colder zones, spring planting is often safer.

Most fruit trees need sunny locations, and plum trees are not an exception. In fact, it’s particularly important to select a good site since plums flower very early in spring. If your tree is in a low-lying frost pocket, the blossoms will be vulnerable to late frosts. You should also pick a site that offers protection from harsh winds. Plant your plum in cool, well-draining soil. Wet, heavy soil can lead to root disease.

Most plum trees require a pollinator, so you’ll need to plant two compatible trees. However, several varieties are self-fertile, so a single tree is enough to produce a harvest. One great option is the ‘Methley’ plum tree, available from Trees.com, which produces sweet, juicy fruit without needing a pollination partner.

Most of us know that a peach tree is a deciduous tree with juicy round fruits. Here’s a lesser-known fact: the same botanical species can grow fuzzy fruits, called peaches, or glossy-skinned fruits, called nectarines. Peach trees generally thrive in USDA hardiness zones 5–8, although this varies by variety. Peach trees, especially bare-root peach trees, can be successfully planted in November, as long as the ground is not frozen. In colder regions, however, spring planting is often safer.

Peach trees need sunny sites. Plant yours where it will get at least 6–8 hours a day of direct sun. This is essential to trigger flower buds and sweeten the fruit. The soil should be well-draining since standing water will rot the tree’s roots. If the tree is grafted, be sure to set the tree in the planting hole so that the graft union sits above the soil line.

Peach trees also need a specific amount of winter cold, chill hours, when temperatures fall within the range of approximately 32°F to 45°F (0°C to 7°C). Standard peach varieties need from 650 to 1,050 chill hours, although you can find low-chill types that need as few as 100 hours.

One great option for colder regions is the ‘Red Haven’ peach tree from Trees.com, a self-fertile variety that produces sweet, juicy peaches and needs around 800–900 chill hours. For warmer regions with mild winters, try ‘Tropic Snow,’ a low-chill variety that needs just 225 chill hours and produces deliciously sweet, white-fleshed peaches.

The American persimmon is among our most overlooked native fruit trees. This species does not get the spotlight given to apple, peach, or pear trees, yet it offers so much: edible fruit, wildlife habitat, and attractive fall foliage. The fruit is an important food source for wild birds and mammals, including raccoons, opossums, and foxes, and it remains available into early winter, when many other food sources have diminished.

Native to the eastern and southeastern United States, these trees are very cold-hardy, thriving in USDA hardiness zones 4–9. You can buy a common American persimmon tree from Plant Addicts to enjoy its fruit and seasonal interest in your own backyard. American persimmons typically have separate male and female trees, but some varieties are self-fertile and can produce fruit without a pollination partner. If you're growing a tree for its fruit, check its pollination requirements before buying.

The trees can grow to between 35 and 60 feet (10–18 m) tall, although they can be kept smaller by pruning. The fruit they produce is orange and initially astringent, but it becomes sweet and delicious when it fully ripens, often around the time of the first frost.

November is a good time to plant an American persimmon tree in milder regions. You cannot plant at this time, however, if the ground is frozen. Find a well-drained area with loamy, organically rich soil in a full sun location. Avoid frost pockets since these trees may be damaged by unseasonable frosts.

Fig trees have a long and somewhat exotic history. They have been cultivated for 12,000 years and are among the earliest domesticated plants. They have deep symbolic meaning as symbols of peace and prosperity, but, perhaps more important to modern gardeners, the trees are tough, drought-tolerant, and resistant to pests and diseases.

Fig trees can thrive in USDA hardiness zones 6–11, depending on the variety, although those in colder zones may need winter protection. For a reliable, sweet-fruited variety, try the ‘Brown Turkey’ fig from Nature Hills, which is well suited to milder regions. Gardeners in colder zones might prefer the ‘Chicago Hardy’ fig, also available from Nature Hills, which can withstand much lower winter temperatures with suitable protection. Both varieties are self-fruitful, so you only need one tree to enjoy a harvest.

If you live in USDA zone 8 or above, it is generally appropriate to plant your tree in November, provided the ground isn't frozen. In cooler zones, you should wait until spring. For November planting, select a full-sun location, ideally against a south- or southwest-facing wall. This helps the tree capture and retain warmth. The soil must be well-draining and slightly acidic. Water the tree deeply right after planting to settle the soil around the roots.

Once the tree is planted, apply a layer of organic mulch around the base, keeping the mulch a few inches away from the actual trunk to prevent rot.
