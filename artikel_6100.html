It's going to be a rough weekend at airports, as the U.S. air travel system rebounds from one of the most significant disruptions in decades.

Thousands of flights have been canceled for Saturday, continuing fallout from the Federal Aviation Administration's move to halt most takeoffs and landings across the country for about four hours Friday evening.

Airlines were cleared to resume service at 8:15 p.m. EST Friday evening, but travelers will face significant disruptions into the weekend — especially Saturday.

As of 4:35 p.m. EST Saturday, flight cancellations for the day topped 5,300, according to data from FlightAware. And cancellations already extended into Sunday, with 200 flights preemptively grounded. Of those, United accounted for the most (48) followed by JetBlue (33), Delta regional affiliate Endeavor Air (22) and American (20).

Nationwide, nearly 13,000 flights have been scrubbed since Friday.

“This morning, the system remains stable, and our airline partners are working through the operational recovery,” FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said at a Saturday morning press conference alongside Transportation secretary Sean Duffy. “Of course, we have crews and aircraft that are out of position.”

That came after most departing flights in the U.S.were grounded indefinitely late Friday afternoon after the FAA reported communication issues impacting air traffic control centers.

The fallout for travelers has been severe, stranding travelers across the country and forcing hundreds of flights to divert.

Airkines have been racing to reset their operations — and get planes and crewmembers back to where they're supposed to be so schedules can get back to normal.

The FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop late Friday afternoon after discovering a communications outage affecting air traffic control.

It happened as crews were working to upgrade critical FAA equipment, apparently overloading the system amid a switch from copper wiring to fiber at one of the agency's facilities.

“This was an FAA system that didn’t meet the moment," Duffy said at the Saturday press conference. "Our contractor didn’t meet the moment, or the equipment didn’t meet the moment and caused the outages.”

The disruption marked only the second time since 9/11 that a ground stop affecting the entire nation had been issued.

All told, airlines canceled more than 9,000 flights on Friday, according to FlightAware. Another 7,000-plus were delayed.

Some international flights and service within Alaska and Hawaii continued, despite the outage.

But the vast majority of domestic departures on Friday evening were affected.

With the ground stop lifted, airlines can now begin the complex process of resetting their operations.

That means getting aircraft, travelers, crews and baggage to the right destinations.

"Our airline partners are working through that," Bedford said Saturday. "So we will still have delays and cancellations today, but that will set us up for a full recovery tomorrow. And based on everything that we’re hearing from our airline partners, they believe that they’ll be able to have full schedule integrity by the close of business on Sunday."

Airlines issued travel advisories in the midst of the travel chaos. These waivers typically allow passengers with flights booked more flexibility to change their itineraries:

If your flight is affected by this weekend's disruptions, stay glued to your airline app — you may be able to search for a new flight and rebook yourself without waiting on hold or in line at the airport.

Don't see a good flight option for days? Keep checking back: with a lot of passengers shifting plans across the country, it's still possible seats will open up.

Passengers whose flights are canceled or significantly delayed are entitled to a refund if they choose not to travel or accept a rebooked flight, regardless of the reason for the disruption.

Under U.S. Department of Transportation rules, a significant delay generally means arriving at your final destination at least three hours late on a domestic itinerary or six hours late on an international itinerary. For passengers who decide to not travel on those flights, airlines must provide refunds to the original form of payment, not just travel credits.

In large-scale travel disruptions like this, rental cars and hotel rooms at or near airports can book up quickly, so it can make sense to act fast if you want to secure a backup option.

If you have airline miles you can use to book a flight for this weekend, that can also be a good self-insurance policy in case the disruption isn't resolved quickly. Just make sure you understand the cancellation and refund rules before booking a backup ticket.

Because this disruption was due to issues outside airlines' control, carriers are unlikely to reimburse stranded passengers for hotel, ground transportation and meal costs.

Some rewards credit cards do offer built-in trip delay or cancellation protection if you used them to book your original airline tickets.

However, it's not guaranteed that those benefits will cover FAA groundings of this nature. Still, save your receipts for any unexpected expenses in case you're eligible for reimbursement.
