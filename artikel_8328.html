10 Real Estate Scams – The Sign That Gives Each One Away

Most real estate scams end the same way, with you wiring money or signing away something you shouldn’t. Whether you rent, buy, sell, or own, check the person, the property, and any payment instructions through phone numbers and records you look up yourself before you sign or send anything.

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center logged 12,368 real estate fraud complaints in 2025, with $275 million in reported losses, up from $174 million in 2024, according to its 2025 Internet Crime Report.

Scammers control every phone number and email address in the messages they send you, so checking through either one only reaches the scammer again. What they can’t control is a county property record, a company website you find on your own, or a number from paperwork you got early in the deal.

Nearly every scheme below also pushes you to pay or sign before you’ve had time to check. That push is your cue to stop, and it’s one of the plainest ways to spot a financial scam of any kind.

Scammers copy a real listing’s photos and description, put their own contact details on it, and post it on another site, often at a rent well below others nearby. Then they collect an application fee, a deposit, or the first month’s rent for a home they don’t control.

The sign is a landlord who says they’re out of the country, can’t show you the inside, and asks you to pay by wire, gift card, or crypto, payments the Federal Trade Commission says you usually can’t reverse. Search the address to see whether the same home shows up under another name or for sale. If you already paid, report the listing to the site that ran it, to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and to your state attorney general.

Criminals break into or imitate the email of someone in your deal, such as your agent or title company, and send last-minute wiring instructions that route your down payment to their account. That’s down payment wire fraud, and any change to wiring instructions that arrives by email or text is its sign.

Before you wire anything, call the title company at a number you already had. If the money is already gone, call your bank at once and ask it to recall the wire, then file a complaint at ic3.gov.

Impostors pose as the owner of a vacant lot, often one with no mortgage, using fake IDs, email accounts, and internet phone numbers. They list it through a real agent, push for a quick sale, and have the money sent to an accomplice, a scheme the FBI warned about in June 2026. Title companies surveyed by the American Land Title Association have seen it tried on vacation homes and rental properties too.

What gives it away is a seller who won’t meet in person or on video, knows little about the property, and will take less than it’s worth to close fast. If you’re buying, mail a certified letter to the owner’s address on the property tax record before closing, so the real owner hears about the sale. If you already paid, ask your bank to recall the money and report it at ic3.gov.

A mover quotes a low price over the phone or online without seeing your belongings, loads the truck, then demands far more before it will deliver. A quote given sight unseen, or a demand for cash or a big deposit up front, is the sign.

Get a binding estimate, a price the mover guarantees, in writing before loading day. On a move across state lines, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) says a mover that won’t deliver after you’ve paid 100% of a binding estimate, or 110% of a non-binding one, has broken federal rules. If a mover holds your things after you’ve paid that much, file a complaint with the FMCSA.

A forger draws up a fake deed that transfers your home to them, files it with the county, then borrows against the home, rents it out, or tries to sell it.

A notice about a loan or a deed change you never authorized is the sign. Ask your county recorder whether it offers a free alert that tells you when anyone files a document on your property, since many counties do. If it already happened, get a copy of the deed from the recorder, file a police report, and call a real estate attorney. Call your title insurer too, since some owner’s title policies pay the legal costs of undoing a forgery.

A company promises to stop your foreclosure or cut your payments, takes a fee up front, and then does little or nothing. Some ask you to sign your deed over to them or to send them your mortgage payments instead of your servicer, the company you normally pay.

Any fee before you have a written offer from your lender gives it away, since the FTC bars these companies from charging until you get that offer and accept it. A HUD-approved housing counselor will help you weigh your options for free. If you already paid, call your servicer right away and report the company to the FTC and your state attorney general.

Roadside signs, fliers, and calls promise a fast cash sale with no repairs. AARP warns that many of these buyers are wholesalers who push owners to sell for less than the home is worth, then resell the signed contract to an investor at a markup.

Pressure to sign fast, sometimes on a phone screen, before you know what your home is worth is the sign. Get an appraisal first, and have a real estate attorney read the contract before you sign. If you’ve already signed, ask an attorney whether you can get out of it, and report the buyer to your state attorney general or real estate commission.

A buyer or renter you’ve never met sends a check for more than the security deposit or the earnest money that comes with an offer, then asks you to wire back the extra. Banks must make deposited money available quickly, but the FTC says a fake check can take weeks to discover, and you repay the bank when it bounces.

Any payment for more than you’re owed, paired with a request to send some back, is the sign. If you already sent money, call your bank or the wire company you used right away to report the fraud, then report it to the FTC.

In loan flipping, a lender talks you into refinancing again and again, charging new closing fees each time. In equity stripping, a lender makes a loan based on your home’s equity, the part you own free and clear, rather than your ability to repay, so missed payments can end in foreclosure.

A lender that pushes another refinance soon after your last one, or a payment your income can’t cover, is the sign. For most refinances and home equity loans on your home, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says you have three business days after closing to cancel in writing. If the three days have passed, file a complaint with the bureau and your state attorney general.

A company calls or advertises with a buyer waiting for your timeshare, a guaranteed sale, or a guaranteed way out of your contract, then demands a large fee before it does anything.

The FTC says anyone who promises a guaranteed sale is running a scam, so a guarantee or an upfront fee is the sign. Ask your timeshare company or resort about its own exit options first. If you already paid, report the company to the FTC and to the attorney general in the timeshare’s state.

Before every deposit, wire, and signature, call the other side at a number you found yourself. Walk away from anyone who won’t wait while you do.
