Andrew Skurka on What It Really Takes to Backpack Alone

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Hi, I’m Andrew Skurka. I’m a former thru-hiker. I was the first person to hike the Sea-to-Sea Route, the Great Western Loop, and the Alaska-Yukon Expedition. For the last 17 years, I’ve been running a guided backpacking service.

If you’re watching this video before December 5th, 2026, and if you live in the Denver metro area, I’d invite you to join us for a live Backpacker Radio event on Saturday, December 5th, at the Skylark Lounge in Denver. You can get tickets here.

In today’s video, I’m going to try to help you become a better solo backpacker.

Backpacking alone is a very different experience than backpacking with a group. Sure, it’s the same scenery, it’s the same conditions, but there are two fundamental things that change when you’re backpacking alone.

The first is that, as a solo backpacker, you need to have much more emotional and mental fortitude than when traveling with a group.

And the second is that you need to really be self-sufficient. There is no safety net. You are entirely dependent on yourself when you’re in the backcountry.

So let’s talk about what that exactly looks like.

I think that the mental piece of solo backpacking starts on the very first day. And there’s this initial period when you step out onto the trail where there’s this excitement and glee about the experience and the trip you’re about to have.

But things kind of quickly calm down. And I find that one of the hardest parts of a solo backpacking trip are those first two days, three days, four days, even a week on a long trip. It could even be like two weeks or three weeks where you’re struggling with this experience of being alone.

I don’t get to backpack alone as often as I used to, but I remember one of the last trips I did do alone is the Pfiffner Traverse, which is a high route from Berthoud Pass to Milner Pass in the Colorado Rockies through the Indian Peaks Wilderness and Rocky Mountain National Park.

Overlooking Iceberg Lakes from the Continental Divide, late-July 2003, sporting GoLite Stride shorts and the original Jam backpack.

And I remember those first couple of days.

It was only an eight-day trip, but it’s been a while since I’d been backpacking alone. I remember getting out there and just how quiet things were and how slow the pace was. And I started to feel doubt about my abilities.

I started to feel guilty for having taken these eight days out of my life when there could have been things that I could be doing at home, or I could have been spending time with my wife or my fur babies. I could have been at home working and trying to get some stuff done in the office.

The sensation of backpacking alone, I probably can’t understate just how foreign this is from our normal life.

In a normal life, it’s pretty hectic, even for someone who tries not to have a hectic life. We are constantly plugged in with phones and television and the internet. We have lots of people to talk to.

Most of us at some point during a typical day get in a car and drive and deal with a bunch of traffic. If you want to go for a run or for a hike, you’re probably sharing those frontcountry trails or those bike paths with other people.

So going into the backcountry where it’s just you experiencing a natural environment at a very slow pace of three miles an hour, it’s very difficult.

I would say the best way I’d describe it is it’s jarring to go from normal life to solo backpacking.

I remember in those early days of the Pfiffner Traverse having all the emotions that I just shared.

And thankfully I’d been on enough solo backpacking trips before, even though it’d been a while, that I knew that the best thing to do is just to stick it out, to just put in the miles, put in the vertical, put in the days, and eventually this experience would feel more natural.

Ideally what happens on a solo backpacking trip, and this is what started to happen to me as the days went on during that Pfiffner Traverse hike, is that I started to feel like there was no better place for me to be, that that’s exactly where I needed to be and that’s exactly what I needed to be doing.

As you get more into a solo backpacking trip, there are a couple of things that happen.

One is that you just get more familiar and comfortable with the solo backpacking experience, that slow pace of life, that quiet, the sensation of being alone and not having anyone to talk to and just being you in the wilderness.

As each day goes on, you also start to feel like you’re earning the experience.

You’re having to push through hard moments, you’re having to make decisions, you’re having to endure the physical rigors of backpacking, and you start to feel like you’ve earned it.

There’s that saying in backcountry skiing that you earn your turns, and it’s similar, where you start to feel like you’ve done what it takes to be out there and to be having this experience.

Another thing that happens as you continue on in the trip is that this goal that you started with, maybe it was hiking from Georgia to Maine or hiking from Mexico to Canada or from Denver to Durango or something maybe much more modest, it may have felt really abstract and almost unachievable at the start of the trip.

But you start to see that this steady progress, this just putting in the hours, putting in the days, maybe putting in the weeks, starts to translate into real progress. And you start to slowly see yourself move along that map and make your way towards your ultimate destination.

And I think ideally on a solo backpacking trip it should start to feel like this is the place where you belong, that this is the place where you want to be, that this is where you should be.

And I’ve always, on my solo backpacking trips, if I’ve given enough time, if I haven’t quit on myself, eventually gotten to that point.

But again, it could take days, weeks, months before you really feel like you’re there.

Once you’ve made it beyond those early days of the trip and kind of stuck it out, what’s going to happen inevitably is that something is going to want to ruin your day.

So maybe you blow the toe box on your shoe, maybe you lose a glove, maybe your camera gets dropped in water. I think I may have just dated myself by suggesting my camera gets dropped in water, but it’s happened.

Maybe something important falls out of a side pocket, like a water bottle.

Or it also could be much more severe. Maybe you have a really scary encounter with a grizzly bear.

Maybe you get stuck high on a ridge in a fierce thunder and lightning storm. Maybe one morning you wake up and there’s six inches of snow on the ground.

Sometimes things can be trivial. Sometimes things can be closer to a matter of life and death.

But the point is that you are going to have challenges along the way.

And this is where it gets difficult to be a solo backpacker because you have no emotional backstop.

There is no hiking partner that you can use as a sounding board. There’s no hiking partner that can sort of talk you off the proverbial cliff. There’s no hiking partner that can offer you extra perspective or extra insight or maybe who’s just willing to hear you out.

Instead, all of those emotions that you experience as a solo backpacker, all those challenges you encounter along the way, you have to deal with them. Those challenges are yours and yours alone.

“Hiking a high route with Andrew Skurka in Colorado.”

Now you may be thinking that when you run into problems, well, I have a satellite messenger and I can at least get some feedback or some advice from maybe a family member or friend.

But I’ll caution you against relying on that too much.

A satellite messenger is not the same as in-person company. They’re not there, they’re not experiencing it.

I think another thing that happens too is that, I hate to say this to you, but as a hiker your canvas is pretty small.

When you think about the things that can ruin your day as a hiker, God forbid that you dropped your water bottle three miles ago and now you don’t have a water bottle.

When you tell that to some person who’s dealing with real challenges like a sick three-year-old or someone who’s in the hospital, they will laugh at you and say that your canvas is small.

There’s no other way around it. But that’s just the lived experience for a hiker. Your life is pretty simple.

Everything that you have is really important and it can be sometimes difficult to put your own situation in perspective.

So back to the satellite messenger thing. Don’t expect a lot of sympathy from your friends and family at home who are living a normal life and who aren’t gallivanting out in the wilderness.

And last thing about the satellite messenger, most family members are not very good therapists, so don’t expect to get really good feedback from them if you’re messaging them looking to help you troubleshoot a problem that you’re experiencing.

So when you have those challenges along the way, you feel that maybe there’s some panic or some sorrow or some frustration or sadness or concern or whatever that might be. All those darker emotions, right?

I have maybe just a few techniques to share with you that might be helpful.

And I’m not going to prescribe things because there are so many conditions that might be creating some mental challenges.

So S-T-O-P. Sit down, think, observe, and plan.

And that’s just kind of like a way of resetting yourself with whatever you might be experiencing.

A second thing, this is one that I will tell my groups a lot.

So long as no one’s airway is blocked, so long as everyone is breathing, and so long as everyone has a pulse, you usually have a little bit of time to deal with whatever you’re dealing with.

So that’s why I tell my groups, after we get through something kind of challenging or maybe when we are in a challenging situation, I tell everyone, “Hey guys, we’re all going to take a smoke break.”

Because that’s kind of the code word for, like, I just need everyone to relax and get their wits about them, bring their heart rate down, bring their breathing down, try to assess the situation for what it really is in a really rational way without getting all emotionally ratcheted up.

One is from a co-guide of mine, Jeff Wall. One of his sayings is always, “There’s always time for a snack.”

When something happens and people are kind of on the verge of getting panicky or maybe are panicking, sit down in a safe spot and just have something to eat.

Now when you do need something to eat, it’s probably unlikely that something like, say, a Ziploc bag full of almonds or a bunch of beef jerky are going to go down real well.

So it’s to have kind of a sugary snack, maybe like some Sour Patch Kids or, if you have a Trader Joe’s nearby, maybe the Scandinavian Swimmers.

Something that’s just kind of going to go down easy, that even when you’re in a heightened state, you can still eat it and sit down and relax and think about the situation that you’re in.

And then I have one final technique for you that you can use too in dealing with difficult situations as a solo backpacker.

So it’s a way to kind of ground yourself and make yourself just feel more present.

It’s five things you can see. It’s four things that you can touch. It’s three things that you can hear. Two things that you can smell. And one thing that you can taste.

And you want to be as descriptive as you can as you’re going through this exercise because the more descriptive you are, the more focused you get on your actual environment.

So I wouldn’t just say, “Well, I can see Jessica behind the camera.”

I would say, “I can see Jessica behind the camera. She has on a black shirt. I can see her cool arm tattoo and her really awesome cheetah-printed pants and her hip black Doc Marten boots.”

So that would be the way that you do that. You walk through the whole process and that would be, again, just a way of calming yourself down in what otherwise could be a tough situation.

“On most of my trips, a pack this large and heavy would be a showstopper. But on this relaxed overnight with my wife, it was perfect. “

So far as the solo backpacker, I have talked about how those early days can be really tough.

And I’ve talked about how the emotional and physical fortitude just needs to be a lot higher than when you’re backpacking with a group.

The last thing I want to talk about or prepare you for as a solo backpacker is the need to be self-sufficient.

As a solo backpacker, you are the lone decision-maker.

You are basically the executive, the one and only. And there’s no one else that you’re getting to share that decision-making burden with.

I will say that making all these decisions, to some it can feel really taxing.

One thing that I always observe when I’m guiding my trips is that I watch individuals who aren’t accustomed to having to make constant route decisions when off-trail.

After a while, like half an hour, an hour, they’re just shot by all of the decisions about, do I go left? Do I go right? Where do I put my feet?

Just those very elementary decisions can become really taxing.

But I actually find that process of being the lone decision-maker, having all that responsibility, I actually find it to be incredibly engaging.

It really helps pass the time when it’s just you and you can just fill your time and your mental energy in basically just getting on with life, doing the very simple things in life.

So a couple of things would be, where are you going to camp? And what time are you going to camp?

How much water are you going to carry to the next water source? Do you think that that water source is going to be reliable? Are you going to count on it?

You are going to have to make a decision about whether to outrun a storm that looks like it’s coming in.

And as I mentioned earlier, if you’re traveling off-trail, all those micro-route decisions, you’re having to do by yourself.

There’s no one around that you can share that burden with.

I don’t want to understate how difficult it can be or what a change it can be to be a solo backpacker and just to be making all the decisions by yourself.

Most of us in life have someone to talk to about things.

So in my personal life, I get to talk to one of my employees, Katie Gerber, about a lot of decisions that we’re making as a company.

I get to talk to my wife about what we’re having for dinner or what our plans are this weekend.

On a daily basis, at my easy disposal, I have a sounding board, I have someone giving me insight, someone giving me perspective, maybe someone who’s just willing to make the decision for me.

And that sort of communal living style, it’s just easier and generally tends to be a lot safer as well.

If you’re having to make decisions by yourself, I do have a couple of tips.

I don’t think I’ve ever regretted in the backcountry making a conservative decision, and this is especially the case when solo.

And the mountains will still be there. You will live to fight another day.

You’ll never regret just pulling up short or calling it a day or bailing out entirely versus pushing on alone with no safety system, with no safety backup system in place.

Another thing you can do when you’re having to make solo decisions is to verbally rationalize your thoughts.

And I do this often where, yes, it seems kind of crazy.

Like someone can look crazy for talking to themselves, but there’s something about saying it out loud and you can kind of call yourself out on your bullshit.

So if I have a thought and I start saying it out loud, and as I’m saying it out loud, it either sounds reasonable or it sounds like I’m trying to convince myself of something.

And maybe that would set me up for the last piece of advice, is that as a solo backpacker, you need to be really self-aware and realistic about your abilities, about your fitness, about your skill level, about the conditions.

Don’t get too optimistic or blinded by a goal.

At the end of the day, you need to come home.

In addition to being the lone decision-maker as a solo backpacker, the other thing that happens is that you have no backup when you’re out there.

This puts a much bigger onus on you to know your route, to select gear that is truly appropriate for the conditions.

And on that note, to really be familiar with the likely conditions that you’ll encounter.

You also need to be familiar with bailout points in case something goes wrong.

When you’re selecting your equipment, you want to keep in mind that you don’t have any redundancy in the equipment of a second person.

As a solo backpacker, it’s really important to carry two lighters.

I carry one in my little ditty kit. I carry the other somewhere else.

Because a lighter is one of those life-saving tools.

It’s also more convenient on a daily basis that it lights my stove.

And if you’re with a group, it’s not that big of a deal if your lighter goes out, right?

If you run out of fuel or if you lose it or if it gets wet, not that big of a deal.

But as a soloist, if your lighter goes out on you or you lose it, you don’t have another lighter and that makes life difficult.

As a solo backpacker, you also want some kind of backup water purification system.

So for example, if your Sawyer Squeeze gets blocked up, you have some kind of backup.

Whereas in a group setting, if everyone had their own water purification system, if my system goes out, I can borrow my buddy’s.

You can also extend this idea too of having some redundancy in your system.

Or not necessarily redundancy, but you just need a more robust kit because you can’t rely on the cumulative possessions of everyone in your group.

That tends to be why on a solo backpacking trip, I’m going to carry a fuller first aid kit that includes a more complete array of over-the-counter medications, maybe some extra Leukotape than what I would probably likely need on one trip because it’s possible that I might need a lot of it.

I’m also going to carry more storm-worthy clothing and a more robust shelter because if something were to happen to me, so for example, I get cold and wet, I get stuck in a big storm, I can’t really rely on someone else to be taking care of me.

So having a little bit of extra margin in there goes a long way.

And then the last thing that falls into this is that you need to have a backup plan.

If you’re in a group, it’s pretty rare where entire groups get themselves into problems, especially a larger group.

It definitely happens, but typically the injuries that we see are not to an entire group.

The issue is more like one particular person rolled an ankle or did something or you got physically hurt. One person isn’t feeling well.

It’s usually not like the whole group that comes down with stuff like that.

That backup plan would start with a satellite messenger like a ZOLEO or a Garmin inReach.

And then the other part of your backup plan would be leaving a really good itinerary with some emergency contacts.

And that emergency plan would look like, it would say, here’s my route, here are my dates, here’s the date and time on which I will be exiting or that you need to give me until you can start to get worried.

It would include in that emergency document a way of contacting your satellite messenger.

It would include all of your logistics info like airfare, your ground transportation, what permit you have for a particular trailhead or for a particular trail.

And that way, if something were to happen to you, people know where to start looking.

So if you’re ready to start backpacking solo, there are two ways to go about it.

One way is that you could just jump in, jump in the deep end.

I think it could be really rough. It could be pretty uncomfortable either emotionally or physically.

And overall, it could just kind of be a scarring experience that never makes you want to backpack again or at least backpack alone again.

The other way to start hiking solo is just to build up to it.

So you could start, for example, with frontcountry car camping trips.

And then you could do one or two overnight trips in places that you know well, maybe places that you’ve gone before with groups.

And then build that out or expand that out into trips of longer duration and less familiar locations.

Hopefully at this point, you feel a little bit more prepared to go backpacking alone.
