Lock-in effect likely to keep HELOCs, second lien activity up through 2027

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

With many borrowers holding low-rate first mortgages, lenders report steady demand for HELOCs and closed-end seconds to fund renovations and consolidate higher-rate debt. Benutech recorded more than 6M second mortgages since 2022, and lenders expect activity to remain durable into 2027.  AI Summary

Homeowners have spent the past several years holding onto low-rate first mortgages while looking for ways to access their accumulated home equity. As rates have stayed above pandemic-era lows, the lock-in effect has helped sustain demand for home equity lines of credit (HELOCs) and second mortgages and is expected to continue into 2027.

According to a recent analysis from Benutech that examined county-level mortgage recording data, more than 6 million second mortgages, including home equity lines of credit (HELOCs) and closed-end second liens, were recorded between Jan. 1, 2022, and Oct. 10, 2026.

Annual second-mortgage volume peaked in 2025 at 1.54 million loans, up 21.3% from 2024 and 32.4% from 2022. Through Oct. 10, 2026, 953,185 loans had been recorded, putting the market on pace for approximately 1.23 million loans for the year, the analysis found.

In October, a report from First American Data & Analytics found that U.S. homeowners held $13.2 trillion in accessible home equity in Q2 2026. Roughly 88% of that equity was tied to mortgages with rates below the market average.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s Center for Microeconomic Data reported similar findings in its quarterly report on household debt and credit, released at the start of November. HELOC balances increased by $23 billion to $481 billion, marking the largest quarterly increase since 2005. HELOC limits also rose by $31 billion, extending an expansion that began in 2022.

As a result, the news cycle is abuzz with tales of borrowers turning to home equity products. But Anthony Stratis, vice president of lending partnerships at Figure, pointed out that demand has been building for several years, with homeowners using second liens to fund renovations and consolidate debt.

A significant share of Figure borrowers use home equity financing for those purposes, he said.

Stratis said the demand could outlast the current interest-rate environment as lenders improve their products and borrowers become more accustomed to using home equity to access cash.

“Home equity has really evolved to the point where it’s a pretty quick way to access cash when you need it for a project or whatever it might be,” Stratis said. “I think that part is going to stay in place, and home equity is likely to have a place in the mortgage ecosystem for a while, independent of rates.”

Matt Kroon, a home loan specialist at Churchill Mortgage, pointed out the “emotional connection” that borrowers have with their low, COVID-era mortgage rates. But persistently high rates and an aging home stock have led borrowers to inquire about other solutions.

“Since affordability is a major concern right now, people are accessing their equity to make those improvements, or to pay off any higher-interest debt,” Kroon said. “Renovations and debt consolidation HELOCs are the main uses right now. However, some other uses include major expenses like a new roof, siding, furnace, etc.”

Tom Hutchens, president of Angel Oak Mortgage Solutions, agreed with Stratis that his company has seen steady HELOC demand over the past several years. Angel Oak, a non-QM lender, sees most of its demand among self-employed borrowers and those who use bank statement documentation to qualify for both first mortgages and HELOCs.

Hutchens said borrowers are also using home equity to pay down high-interest credit card balances. Angel Oak’s average combined loan-to-value ratio on HELOCs is in the 60% range, he said.

“Private capital is getting into the HELOC business. You’re going to start to see more and more HELOC securitizations,” he added.

Kevin Leibowitz, founder of Grayton Mortgage, said that while HELOC inquiries have increased, not all conversations result in second mortgages.

“[There is] lots of interest in HELOCs, a lot of production in HELOCs,” Leibowitz said. “But a lot of my conversations start with HELOCs, and we kind of pivot into other types of lending because the other types of lending might make sense.”

The decision often comes down to comparing the cost of a borrower’s existing debt with the rate available on a new loan, he said. While borrowers may be reluctant to refinance a low-rate first mortgage, high-interest credit card balances and other obligations can make a new first mortgage more advantageous.

Benjamin Cohen, managing director and SVP of mortgage lending at Rate, said borrowers who might have done a cash-out refinance in the past are now looking at a HELOC or a fixed-rate second mortgage like our HELoan.

“The math is pretty straightforward. If you owe $500,000 at 3% and need another $75,000, a cash-out refinance puts the entire $575,000 at today’s rate,” Cohen said. “With a second mortgage, you keep the $500,000 at 3% and pay the new rate only on the $75,000. Even if the second mortgage carries a higher rate than the cash-out refinance, your blended rate across both loans can still be meaningfully lower. That can be a good compromise if the payments, fees and repayment terms make sense for you.”

Leibowitz cited one borrower who had accumulated significant credit card debt after being laid off. By replacing the borrower’s first and second mortgages along with the credit card debt, he said, he improved the borrower’s monthly cash flow by about $3,500.

For borrowers with relatively low first-mortgage rates, keeping that loan and adding a HELOC may still be the better option. But HELOCs typically carry variable rates tied to the Wall Street Journal prime rate, exposing borrowers to higher payments if rates rise, Leibowitz said.

Adam Hopson, chief operating officer of Flyhomes, said home equity can also help homeowners purchase another property before selling their current one. However, the additional borrowing can affect their ability to qualify for a new mortgage.

“A HELOC can unlock a down payment without unlocking a move,” Hopson said. “Having access to equity and qualifying for the next mortgage are two different things, though.”
