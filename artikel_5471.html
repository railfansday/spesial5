What the U.S. government spends money on, from Social Security to interest

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Free daily briefing on global business news.

See where federal tax dollars go, from Social Security and Medicare to defense and interest on the debt, in 10 major areas of the U.S. budget

Many federal payments are never voted on line by line in a given year. Social Security checks, Medicare claims and interest on Treasury debt go out under permanent law or binding obligations. They keep flowing whether or not lawmakers act. The difference between that spending and the money Congress approves annually is the starting point for understanding where federal tax dollars go.

Federal spending falls into two broad groups. Mandatory spending is set by standing law. The rules that decide who qualifies for Social Security or Medicare, and what they receive, stay in force until Congress changes them. Spending on these programs depends on how many people qualify and what the law entitles them to, not on a yearly vote.

Discretionary spending works differently. Congress sets it each year by passing appropriations bills, which fund agencies and programs for a single fiscal year. Defense is the largest part of this category. The rest covers a wide range of federal activity, from scientific research to much of veterans' health care. The federal fiscal year runs from Oct. 1 to Sept. 30. That means the budget calendar does not line up with the calendar year.

Borrowing adds a third layer. When spending exceeds revenue in a given year, the government borrows to cover the difference. That one-year gap is the deficit. The accumulated total of past borrowing is the national debt. The Treasury owes interest on the securities it sells to finance that debt. Those payments are a legal obligation rather than a program Congress votes on.

Knowing which group a program falls into helps explain the federal budget breakdown. It shows which costs lawmakers can adjust in one appropriations cycle and which require rewriting the underlying law. Financing is a separate question. Some programs draw on dedicated payroll or fuel taxes, while others rely on general revenue.

What follows is a selection of 10 major areas of federal spending and finance. It is not a ranking by size or a complete accounting of what the U.S. government spends money on. Each spending slide explains what a program or obligation does, who runs it, how it is paid for and whether it is mandatory or discretionary. The final slide turns to the revenue side, covering where the money comes from and how borrowing fills the rest.

Social Security sends monthly payments to retired workers, disabled workers and the families of workers who have died. Most of the money comes from payroll taxes collected under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, better known as FICA.

The combined tax rate is 12.4% of wages up to an annual earnings cap. Workers pay 6.2% through paycheck withholding. Employers pay a matching 6.2%. The cap is adjusted each year to track average wage growth. Earnings above it are not subject to the Social Security portion of the payroll tax.

Self-employed people pay both halves themselves. Their contribution falls under the Self-Employment Contributions Act rather than FICA. The combined rate is the same 12.4%.

Payroll tax receipts are credited to two trust funds held at the Treasury. The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund pays retirement and survivor benefits. The Disability Insurance Trust Fund pays benefits to workers who can no longer work because of a qualifying medical condition. Any surplus in the funds is invested in special-issue Treasury securities.

The system largely operates on a pay-as-you-go basis. Taxes paid by today's workers fund the checks going to today's beneficiaries. The money is not saved in individual accounts.

Benefits go straight to individuals. The Social Security Administration calculates each payment from a person's lifetime earnings record. Higher lifetime earnings produce larger checks, up to a maximum. Retirement benefits can start as early as age 62 at a reduced amount. Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later. Waiting past full retirement age earns delayed retirement credits until age 70.

Survivor benefits can go to widows, widowers, dependent children and, in some cases, dependent parents. Disability benefits require enough recent work history to qualify. They also require a medical finding that the person cannot sustain substantial work.

Payments rise in most years through an automatic cost-of-living adjustment. The adjustment is tied to the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers. Congress does not vote on each year's increase.

Social Security is mandatory spending. Its benefit formulas and eligibility rules are set in the Social Security Act, which President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed in 1935.

Some higher-income beneficiaries also pay federal income tax on part of their benefits. Much of that revenue is credited back to the Social Security trust funds. A portion goes to Medicare's hospital insurance trust fund instead.

Medicare is the federal health insurance program for Americans 65 and older and for some younger people with disabilities. Its coverage is split into parts: Part A pays for hospital care, Part B for doctor visits and outpatient services, and Part D for prescription drugs.

President Lyndon B. Johnson signed Medicare into law in 1965 as an amendment to the Social Security Act. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, an agency within the Department of Health and Human Services, runs the program. Medicare is classified as mandatory spending. Anyone who meets the eligibility rules set in law can enroll.

Age is the main route in, but it is not the only one. People who have received Social Security disability benefits for 24 months become eligible before 65. So do people with end-stage renal disease, which is permanent kidney failure, and people with ALS.

Part A is financed mainly by a payroll tax. The combined rate is 2.9% of wages, with employees and employers each paying 1.45%. Self-employed workers pay the full 2.9% themselves. Unlike the Social Security payroll tax, the Medicare tax has no cap on the wages it applies to. The Affordable Care Act added a 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above set income thresholds. That tax took effect in 2013.

Part A revenue flows into the Hospital Insurance Trust Fund. Most enrollees pay no monthly premium for Part A. They qualify for premium-free coverage because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes for at least 40 quarters, or about 10 years of work.

Parts B and D work differently. They draw on general federal revenue and on premiums paid by beneficiaries, through the Supplementary Medical Insurance Trust Fund. Enrollees with higher incomes pay larger premiums for both parts.

Part D is the newest major piece. Congress created it in the Medicare Prescription Drug, Improvement, and Modernization Act of 2003, and coverage began in 2006. Private insurers that contract with Medicare deliver the drug plans. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 gave Medicare authority to negotiate prices for certain high-cost drugs.

Many beneficiaries get their coverage through Medicare Advantage, also called Part C. These private plans bundle Part A and Part B benefits, and most include drug coverage. Medicare pays the plans to provide those benefits.

Medicaid is a health coverage program paid for jointly by the federal government and the states. The federal share differs from state to state and is set by a formula called the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage, or FMAP.

That shared structure separates Medicaid from Medicare, which the federal government runs on its own. Each state administers its own Medicaid program within federal rules. States decide many details, including some optional benefits, payment rates for doctors and hospitals, and how people enroll. The federal government then reimburses its share of qualifying costs.

The FMAP formula compares each state's per capita income with the national average. States with lower incomes receive a larger federal match. Wealthier states receive a smaller one. Federal law sets a floor of 50%. No state covers more than half of its standard Medicaid costs on its own.

Federal Medicaid funding is mandatory spending, and it is open-ended. The federal government matches whatever qualifying amount a state spends rather than sending a fixed sum. Federal costs therefore rise and fall with enrollment and with medical prices in each state.

Medicaid covers several groups with limited incomes: children, parents, pregnant women, older adults and people with disabilities. It is also a major payer for long-term care in nursing homes. Medicare covers only limited skilled nursing stays. Many older Americans who need extended nursing home care qualify for Medicaid after spending down their savings to meet its eligibility limits.

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 let states extend Medicaid to most adults with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level. The federal government pays 90% of the cost for that expansion group. That is a higher match than the regular formula provides. Not every state has adopted the expansion.

The Children's Health Insurance Program, known as CHIP, fills a different gap. It covers children in families whose incomes are too high to qualify for Medicaid. Congress created the program in 1997. Unlike Medicaid, CHIP is funded through capped federal allotments to each state. Its federal match rate is higher than the regular Medicaid rate. States can run CHIP as part of Medicaid, as a separate program or as a combination of the two.

Medicaid itself dates to 1965. It was signed into law alongside Medicare as part of amendments to the Social Security Act.

Defense is the largest part of discretionary federal spending, and most of that money flows through the Department of Defense. Congress sets the level each year through the National Defense Authorization Act and a separate defense appropriations bill.

The two bills do different jobs. The National Defense Authorization Act sets policy and authorizes programs. It covers matters such as troop levels, military pay raises and which weapons programs may go forward. The appropriations bill provides the money the Pentagon can actually spend. A program can be authorized and still go unfunded if appropriators do not supply the dollars.

The Pentagon's budget is organized into a handful of broad accounts. Military personnel funding covers pay and allowances for active-duty and reserve troops. Operations and maintenance is the account that keeps forces ready. It pays for training, fuel, equipment repairs, base operations and the salaries of many civilian employees. A separate account, the Defense Health Program, runs military hospitals and clinics and the Tricare health plan.

Procurement covers the purchase of ships, aircraft, combat vehicles, missiles and munitions. Research, development, test and evaluation pays for designing and proving new systems before they are bought in quantity. Military construction pays for barracks, runways and other facilities. It moves through a separate appropriations bill that also funds the Department of Veterans Affairs.

Some national security spending sits outside the Pentagon. Nuclear warheads are designed, built and maintained by the Department of Energy through its National Nuclear Security Administration. That work counts toward the federal government's broader national defense budget category. It is not part of the Department of Defense's own budget. The Coast Guard also sits outside the Pentagon. In peacetime, it falls under the Department of Homeland Security.

Timing shapes how the money is spent. The defense appropriations bill is one of 12 regular spending bills Congress is meant to pass before each new fiscal year begins. When lawmakers miss that deadline, the Pentagon typically operates under a continuing resolution. That stopgap generally holds funding at the prior year's levels. It also generally bars new programs from starting until a full-year bill passes.

Net interest is the federal government's cost of carrying the Treasury securities it has sold, minus the interest income it collects. Federal law pays interest on the public debt through a permanent, indefinite appropriation, with no yearly vote required.

The calculation starts with interest on debt held by the public. That debt consists of securities owned by individuals, banks, pension funds, mutual funds, state and local governments, foreign governments and the Federal Reserve. Interest the government receives from other sources is then subtracted to reach the net figure.

Interest the Treasury owes to its own trust funds, such as those for Social Security, sits outside the net figure. Those payments move money from one government account to another. In the overall budget, they cancel out.

Budget presentations from the Congressional Budget Office list net interest as its own category, separate from mandatory and discretionary spending.

Two forces set the cost: how much debt is outstanding and what interest rates the Treasury pays. A larger debt raises the bill even when rates hold steady. Higher rates raise it even when the debt stops growing.

The mix of securities shapes how quickly rate changes reach the budget. Treasury bills mature in one year or less. Treasury notes run from two to 10 years, and Treasury bonds run 20 or 30 years. When a security matures, the Treasury usually sells a new one to repay the holder. The new security carries whatever rate the market sets at auction.

Debt weighted toward short-term bills reprices fast when rates move. A 30-year bond keeps the rate set at its original auction until it matures.

Some securities adjust on their own. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities raise their principal in line with inflation. The interest paid on them rises as that principal grows. Floating rate notes mature in two years. Their interest payments reset with the rate on 13-week Treasury bills.

Missing an interest payment would count as a default on U.S. government debt. That risk surfaces during standoffs over the statutory debt limit, the cap Congress places on total federal borrowing. Hitting the limit does not erase interest already owed. It restricts the Treasury's ability to borrow the cash needed to meet all of its obligations, interest included.

The Department of Veterans Affairs spends federal money in two main ways. It runs a nationwide health system through the Veterans Health Administration, and it pays benefits to veterans, including monthly disability compensation for conditions connected to military service.

The Veterans Health Administration owns and operates its own hospitals and clinics. It also employs its own doctors, nurses and support staff. That sets it apart from Medicare and Medicaid, which mostly pay outside hospitals and physicians for care. The VA also buys care from community providers. The VA MISSION Act of 2018 set the rules for when veterans can see community doctors at VA expense.

Much of the VA health care budget is discretionary. Congress sets that money each year through appropriations bills.

Disability compensation works on different terms. It is a monthly, tax-free payment to veterans whose illnesses or injuries were caused or made worse by their service. The VA assigns each eligible veteran a disability rating from 0% to 100%, in 10% steps. The payment rises with the rating. Veterans rated at 30% or higher can receive more if they have a spouse, children or dependent parents.

Disability compensation is mandatory spending. A veteran who meets the eligibility rules is entitled to the payment.

Education benefits trace back to the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, the original GI Bill. Its current main version, the Post-9/11 GI Bill, was enacted in 2008. It can cover tuition and fees and pay a monthly housing allowance to eligible veterans and service members. Some service members can transfer unused benefits to a spouse or children.

The VA home loan program also dates to the 1944 law. The VA usually does not lend the money itself. Instead, it guarantees part of a mortgage made by a private lender. That guarantee lowers the lender's risk if the borrower defaults. It also allows many eligible borrowers to buy a home with no down payment.

Compensation, education benefits and home loans are run by the Veterans Benefits Administration, a separate arm of the department from the health system. A third arm, the National Cemetery Administration, maintains national cemeteries and provides burial benefits for eligible veterans.

SNAP, Supplemental Security Income and the Earned Income Tax Credit are three major federal income-support programs, and each runs through a different agency. One provides food aid, one pays monthly cash and one delivers money through the tax system.

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program is run by the Department of Agriculture's Food and Nutrition Service. It helps low-income households buy groceries. For decades it was known as food stamps. Congress renamed it SNAP in the 2008 farm bill. Benefits now arrive on an Electronic Benefit Transfer card, which works like a debit card at the checkout counter.

The federal government pays for the SNAP benefits themselves. States take applications and determine eligibility, and they share the cost of administering the program. Benefit levels are tied to the Thrifty Food Plan, a USDA estimate of what a low-cost, nutritious diet costs. Congress reauthorizes and revises SNAP through the farm bill, the same legislation that covers crop subsidies and other agriculture programs.

Supplemental Security Income is run by the Social Security Administration. It pays monthly cash to people with low incomes and limited assets who are 65 or older, blind or disabled. Congress created SSI in 1972, and it began paying benefits in 1974. It replaced earlier aid programs for older, blind and disabled people that the states had administered.

SSI is separate from Social Security disability benefits, even though the same agency runs both. SSI does not depend on a recipient's work history. It is paid from general tax revenue rather than from payroll-tax trust funds. Many states add their own supplement on top of the federal payment.

The Earned Income Tax Credit works through the tax code instead of a benefit office. Workers claim it on their federal income tax returns, and the IRS administers it. Congress enacted the credit in 1975. Only people with earnings from work can receive it. The credit grows as earnings rise, levels off, then phases out at higher incomes. It is larger for families with qualifying children.

The credit is refundable. When it exceeds a household's income tax liability, the IRS pays out the difference. That refunded amount counts as federal outlays. The part of the credit that only reduces a tax bill shows up as lower revenue instead.

All three programs are classified as mandatory spending.

Most federal money for roads, bridges and public transit moves through the Highway Trust Fund. Fuel taxes are its main source of revenue, including the federal gasoline tax of 18.3 cents per gallon.

The fund works differently from much of the federal budget. Drivers pay the gas tax at the pump as part of the price of each gallon. That money is credited to the trust fund instead of being pooled with general Treasury revenue. Spending from the fund is reserved for surface transportation.

Congress created the fund in 1956, the same year it authorized the Interstate Highway System. Lawmakers set its spending programs through surface transportation laws that typically cover several years at a time.

The fund has two parts. The Highway Account pays for building, repairing and replacing roads and bridges. The Mass Transit Account, added in 1982, supports bus and rail systems in cities and regions.

The federal government builds very little of this itself. The money reaches the ground mainly as grants to state and local governments. Those governments build and maintain most of the country's roads. They choose and plan projects, hire contractors and handle routine upkeep.

Two agencies inside the Department of Transportation handle the grants. The Federal Highway Administration oversees road and bridge funding. The Federal Transit Administration oversees money for transit systems.

The gas tax is a fixed charge per gallon, not a percentage of the pump price. Revenue depends on how many gallons are sold, not on what each gallon costs. A jump in fuel prices does not increase the tax collected on a gallon.

The rate is also not indexed to inflation, so each cent buys less construction over time. Cars that burn less fuel pay less tax per mile driven. Fully electric vehicles pay no federal gasoline tax at all.

Fuel taxes alone have not covered what the fund pays out. In recent years, Congress has transferred money from general revenue to keep the fund able to meet its obligations. As a result, part of the cost of highways and transit falls on taxpayers generally, not only on drivers at the pump.

The National Institutes of Health, part of the Department of Health and Human Services, funds biomedical research at universities, medical schools and laboratories across the U.S. NASA and the National Science Foundation pay for much of the government's other scientific work, from space missions to basic research in physics, biology and engineering.

All three agencies are funded with discretionary money. Their budgets can rise, fall or stay flat from one fiscal year to the next, depending on what Congress appropriates.

The agencies sit in different spending bills. NIH funding moves through the bill covering the departments of Labor, Health and Human Services, and Education. NASA and the National Science Foundation are both funded through the Commerce, Justice, Science bill.

Most NIH money leaves the agency. It goes out as competitive grants to researchers at outside institutions, a category known as extramural research. Applications go through peer review. Panels of outside scientists score proposals before funding decisions are made. NIH also runs its own intramural research program, with government scientists working largely at its main campus in Bethesda, Maryland.

The agency is organized into 27 institutes and centers. Each one focuses on a disease, an organ system or a field of study. The National Cancer Institute and the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases are two examples.

The National Science Foundation is an independent agency created by the National Science Foundation Act of 1950. It supports basic research and science education across most nonmedical fields, including mathematics, computer science, geosciences and the social sciences. It awards its funding largely through grants chosen by merit review. It also supports large research facilities, including telescopes and the research stations of the U.S. Antarctic Program.

NASA was created under the National Aeronautics and Space Act of 1958. Its budget covers human spaceflight as well as science. The Science Mission Directorate oversees work that includes planetary science, Earth science, astrophysics and heliophysics, the study of the sun and its effects across the solar system. Its missions are built and run by NASA centers working with contractors and university partners.

Grants to universities generally cover two kinds of cost. Direct costs pay for salaries, equipment and supplies tied to a specific project. Indirect costs, also called facilities and administrative costs, cover shared expenses such as buildings, utilities and regulatory compliance. Each institution negotiates its indirect cost rate with the government.

Science funding is not limited to these three agencies. The Department of Energy's Office of Science supports a network of national laboratories. The Department of Agriculture funds research on crops, food safety and farming.

The federal government pays for its spending mainly with three taxes: individual income taxes, payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare, and corporate income taxes. Smaller levies add to those receipts, and the Treasury borrows whatever they do not cover.

The individual income tax has been a permanent part of the federal system since the 16th Amendment was ratified in 1913. It applies graduated rates to wages, salaries, business profits and investment income. Most workers pay it through withholding from each paycheck. The Internal Revenue Service collects it along with payroll taxes.

Payroll taxes follow a different logic. They are tied to wages and earmarked for specific programs. The money is credited to dedicated trust funds instead of being pooled with general revenue.

Corporate income taxes fall on the profits of companies organized as C corporations. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 replaced graduated corporate rates with a single flat rate, effective in 2018. Many businesses, including partnerships and S corporations, pass their income through to their owners. Those owners report it on individual returns and pay individual income tax on it instead.

Smaller streams round out federal receipts. Excise taxes apply to specific goods and activities, such as gasoline, tobacco, alcohol and airline tickets. Customs duties are tariffs on imported goods, collected by U.S. Customs and Border Protection. When the Federal Reserve earns more than it spends on its own operations, it sends the excess to the Treasury.

Revenue has fallen short of spending in nearly every recent year. To make up each year's gap, the Treasury sells securities to investors, including individuals, pension funds, banks and foreign governments. Treasury bills mature in a year or less, notes in two to 10 years, and bonds in 20 or 30 years.

That borrowing accumulates in the national debt until it is repaid. A year in which revenue exceeded spending would produce a surplus instead, which could be used to pay down debt held by the public.

Congress also sets a statutory debt limit, a cap on the total amount the Treasury may borrow. The limit does not authorize new spending. It determines whether the Treasury can borrow to pay for obligations Congress has already approved. Raising or suspending the limit requires separate legislation.
