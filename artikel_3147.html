I’m Begging You, Don’t Let AI Bots Manage Your Money

Your bank probably won't cover the mistakes you give AI permission to make.

If you’re using AI agents to manage your money, you should probably stop right now.

Agents such as Meta’s Muse, OpenClaw and OpenAI’s dots are all the rage in the AI world these days. They promise to do more than chatbots that give you information. Instead they’re tools that can take action if you ask them to.

When you first sign up for an AI agent, you may be asked to grant permission for it to access your apps, browsers and data so it can work with them. That might put your money at risk in unexpected ways.

“The intention might be something simple, like managing a calendar. But that access can give the agent the ability to do much more than the person intended,” said John Strand, the owner of the cybersecurity firm Black Hills Information Security. While you may be giving the agent access to simple smart home routines or getting help planning a trip, your banking information may also be accessible.

It might be tempting to think you can automate your financial chores and have more time for other things, but handing over your account credentials to an agent opens you up to many different things that can go wrong.

For one, AI agents might not be as secure as you think. This technology is very new — these kinds of always-on, persistent agents have really only been available for less than a year, and big companies like Meta and OpenAI have just put out their own versions in the last couple of months. These aren’t polished products that have had all the kinks worked out.

One of those challenges is that they don’t always behave in the way you expect or do what you want them to. Recent news stories about AI agents hacking other websites — including the AI platform Hugging Face and Australian government sites — have involved agents acting in unexpected ways to achieve an assigned goal. You don’t want AI to act like that with your checking account.

When agents use your login info, “a bank cannot tell an authorized agent from an attacker using the exact same login,” said Ted Miracco, CEO of the mobile security firm Approov. “That is more of an identity failure than an AI failure. Until agents carry verifiable identities, scoped permissions and an audit trail, blocking them is the best defensible control a bank has.”

Many banks are aware of the risks and are already cracking down on unauthorized use of agentic agents. Charles Schwab’s CEO Rick Wurster told Bloomberg that bots are taking the liberty to move clients’ money.

“I get a report on how many times we’ve blocked clients from using one of these agents in a week, and it’s in the tens and twenties — per week — that the big agents are being blocked,” Wurster said in a Bloomberg TV interview. “What that tells me is that while people love AI, they’re not willing to turn their financial accounts over to them just yet.”

But that number is likely growing, and with bots going mainstream, it could grow rapidly. More banks are taking action against bots to protect you.

More news of banks standing up against AI caught my attention when I received an email from Ally warning me that when customers use an AI service, they’re responsible for any actions that the AI takes.” Ally gave a warning that if you share your login information with a loved one, they may use that information in a way you didn’t intend — the same is true for AI.

The email goes on to warn that AI can make mistakes and misunderstand your instructions — which could be a big loss for you.

A representative for Ally said in an email to CNET that AI can create new ways to manage your finances, but Ally’s priority is to protect your accounts and personal data. Ally’s email also said that you should understand the risks that come with letting AI access your financial information.

“Not sharing Ally usernames, passwords or security codes is good practice. As the technology evolves, important questions around security, authorization, accountability and customer protection remain areas of focus across the industry,” the statement said.

A spokesperson from Wells Fargo also said in an email to CNET that Wells Fargo has already taken steps to block unauthorized agents from accessing customer accounts, too.

Some banks aren’t completely against AI, though. — they just want you to use their features and tools instead. Wurster added in his interview that he recommends for Schwab customers to use Charley, Charles Schwab’s proprietary AI tool, to gather market insights, ask about personal financial information like balances and manage everyday financial tasks like transfers and payments. These measures make it much safer, but in the meantime, it may be best to keep your banking business to yourself.

Personal finance expert Alaina Fingal broke down when you should and shouldn’t use AI for your money matters.

“AI can be a helpful tool for managing finances, but I think there’s a difference between using AI to assist with understanding money concepts and allowing AI to make financial decisions for you, Fingal said. She recommends using AI as a tool, but not as your financial advisor. You should make any final decisions yourself. Sharing your personal financial data puts you at risk for identity theft and even fraud.

As for letting AI actually access your accounts and do things on your behalf, Fingal said she’s concerned.

“I believe AI is moving incredibly quickly, and the safeguards, regulations, and accountability measures are struggling to keep up with the technology. And we’re prioritizing convenience and innovation without giving enough attention to consumer protection,” Fingal said.

If you want to check to see if bots have been meddling in your banking, look at your login history and account activity. Strand suggests looking for activity that doesn’t match your pattern — like multiple logins in a short period or access happening at the same time every day. That may not mean it’s your AI agent, but it’s worth keeping track of. If you notice anything suspicious, contact your bank immediately for assistance.

Strand also recommends enabling two-factor authentication now as a preventative step to safeguard your financial data, but it’s also a good time to reevaluate the access you’re giving AI. Strand recommends restricting which applications and sites AI agents can access.

“Check the permissions. Make sure they’re limited to what it needs to do the job you gave it, and remove anything beyond that,” he added.

If you’ve already given AI bots access to your financial data, Fingal recommends revoking access and changing the passwords that you’ve shared with any AI platform. Next, set up multifactor authentication and transaction alerts to stay alert.

Most importantly, keep an eye on your accounts. “I tell my clients to take 10 minutes each morning to review their bank transactions; that one habit can give them so much more control over their finances,” said Fingal.

Shang Saavedra, founder of personal finance platform Save My Cents, suggested you regularly revisit your AI agent’s access to apps and data and require it to ask for permission before accessing new data, apps and features.

If you decide to use AI for personal finance purposes, set limitations. “I’ve tested AI with some basic questions and basic calculations, and the information it provides is good for a beginner plan that gives you some pointers as to where to go,” said Saavedra. “I also like using AI to search for book and resource suggestions.”

But the actual handling of money? Maybe do that yourself.
