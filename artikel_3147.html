‘Hexed’ Review: Disney’s Not-So-Wicked Witch Tale Is Cheery Enough, but Not Terribly Crafty

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

A rebellious Ohio teen discovers her helicopter mom is not all she seems in this bright, perky animated feature, which feels a bit secondhand in the wake of films like 'Wicked' and 'Wildwood.'

Thank goodness for Aunt Gladys. If it weren’t for last year’s fright-wigged “Weapons” villainess — a bracing figure of pure, malicious evil amid cinema’s recent run of goody-two-shoes witches, sparkling up the likes of “Wicked” and “Practical Magic 2” — you’d begin to worry that screen sorcery has altogether lost its edge. Not that there’s anything wrong with humanizing witches per se, but surely we can all agree that the bad ones have more fun. The new Disney animated feature “Hexed” splits the difference. Its suburban teenage heroine Billie is about as wholesome a witch as witchery has ever seen, but at least she enjoys her powers, instead of moping about them: After the “Wicked” films spent nearly five hours telling us it’s not easy being green, the chief takeaway of the bright, chipper “Hexed” is that witchin’ is bitchin’. There’s not a whole lot more to it than that, however, even as Fawn Veerasunthorn and Jason Hand‘s film attempts to squeeze a mother-daughter bonding tale in amidst all the magical hijinks. “Hexed” is ostensibly the first original animated film from the Disney stable since 2023’s swiftly forgotten “Wish,” but it certainly doesn’t feel that way: Quilting together story elements from any number of previous, occult-tinged coming-of-age tales, it’s about as familiar as, well, a witch’s right-hand cat: Even its lurid violet-and-jade color palette looks secondhand so soon after “Wicked’s” ubiquitous green-and-pink branding.

A tone of chatty irreverence is struck by the opening prologue, which lays out a fairytale backstory in jagged, collage-style animation: a witch-ruled queendom, a power struggle, the loss or misplacement of magical powers that our blasé narrator describes as “a real bummer.” She doesn’t sound that invested, so neither are we. But as we begin the story proper in modern-day Salem, our interest is sparked in adorable, saucer-eyed newborn Billie — seemingly a typical baby girl, doted on by her single mom Alice (Rashida Jones), until one night in her crib, she demonstrates literal, lightning-like magic in her fingertips. Sadly the intriguing prospect of a telekinetic infant is quashed when, following a near escape from an otherworldly portal accidentally opened up by the tot, Alice neutralizes Billie’s powers with an unprepossessing bracelet, and leaves Salem for Blahville, Ohio.

Sixteen-odd years later, Billie (now peppily voiced by Hailee Steinfeld) is a strong-willed high-schooler wholly unaware of her powers, and chafing against her mom’s constant, chiding attempts to control her. When the latest of Billie’s rogue escapades gets her expelled from school, Alice decides again to leave town — at which point Billie, who has somehow never removed her safety bracelet until now, finally does so, and is immediately whooshed away into the magical realm of Hexe, and is immediately shown the strength of her witchy capabilities.

Those include flying — rare even among her newfound kind, and an immediate threat to Queen Celeste (Jodie Foster), the silver-coiffed sorceress who rules over Hexe with chic cruelty. Soon exasperatedly following her through the portal is Alice, who it turns out knows Hexe — and Celeste — all too well: No surprise that Billie’s powers are hereditary, and that it falls to mother and daughter to save the embattled queendom, with some crotchety assistance from Beef (Walton Goggins), a three-eyed, Southern-drawled feline familiar who looks a little like Disney’s Cheshire Cat as misremembered by ChatGPT. Goggins’ ripe vernacular delivery is the most pleasingly unexpected element here: Everything else runs pretty much entirely according to studio-blueprinted plan, down to the breakneck, back-and-forth chase of a finale in which the real duel for dominance plays out on the soundtrack: Demi Lovato’s “Confident” battles composer Kris Bowers’ busy orchestrations to a cacophonous draw.
