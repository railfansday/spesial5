Webb looked at a giant black hole from the dawn of the universe and saw something unexpected beside it

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Webb spotted a faint light beside a giant black hole just 900 million years after the Big Bang, and astronomers think it may come from a second black hole.

Giant black holes from the young universe still puzzle astronomers. Now a team has found what looks like two of them, close together and both feeding.

The pair, nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, dates to just 900 million years after the Big Bang.

If confirmed, it would be the closest-spaced quasar pair known from cosmic dawn.

A quasar is the brilliant glow produced as gas falls toward a supermassive black hole. That glow can outshine all the stars in its galaxy combined.

Silvia Onorato, a postdoctoral research associate at NSF NOIRLab, led the study.

Her team thinks pairs like this one may mark places where young galaxies were crowding together, feeding new stars and fast-growing black holes at the same time.

The researchers lean toward two real quasars. They haven’t ruled out, though, that the fainter one is a copy of the brighter one, made when light bends around a galaxy in between.

“There is no definitive theoretical explanation for how a single one of these giant quasars exists so early in the age of our Universe, much less two,” Onorato said.

“We observed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua at a time when the Universe was only a thirteenth of its age and astonishingly, its supermassive black holes are just as massive as those we see today.”

The universe’s first billion years, a period astronomers call cosmic dawn, were busy.

Clumps of dark matter collapsed, galaxies made their first stars, and the first supermassive black holes took shape at the centers of the biggest galaxies.

Galaxies back then often ran into each other, and some merged completely.

When galaxies collide, gas can funnel toward their central black holes. The gas heats up as it spirals inward, producing the light we see from a quasar.

Since galaxies collided so often, astronomers expect plenty of quasar pairs closer together than the width of a typical galaxy.

So why have so few turned up? Part of the problem is timing, since a black hole shines as a quasar for only a short part of its life.

Distance is the other problem. Quasars from cosmic dawn are so far away that they look faint and are hard to detect from the ground. And when two of them are very close together, it’s hard to tell one from the other.

Those hurdles make Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua a rare find. If confirmed, it would be the third known quasar pair from cosmic dawn.

The pair’s Hawaiian name was created through the A Hua He Inoa program at the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center of Hawaiʻi. The University of Hawaiʻi’s Hawaiian Language College, Ka Haka ʻUla O Keʻelikōlani, also took part.

In English, the name means “Two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the Universe.”

Astronomers found the pair in the Aether survey, a James Webb Space Telescope program that’s taking a census of objects in the early universe.

Emanuele Paolo Farina, an associate scientist at NSF NOIRLab/Gemini, leads the survey.

Farina’s team used Webb to split the light of 200 quasars into its colors, point by point. That’s 43% of all the quasars known from this stage of cosmic history.

Onorato’s team checked every one by eye. Next to J1602+4228A, a quasar first reported in 2004, they spotted a second, fainter source. No other quasar in the survey had a companion like it.

The faint one, J1602+4228B, is so dim and so close to its partner that seeing it from the ground should have been nearly impossible.

Earlier Gemini observations from a 2017 program had pointed only at the brighter quasar.

The team went back with Gemini North, a telescope on Maunakea, a dormant volcano in Hawaiʻi, and recorded each quasar’s light separately.

“The sky over Maunakea was so clean and sharp that we managed to collect the data in only two nights,” Onorato said.

The brighter quasar’s black hole weighs about 1.5 billion times as much as the Sun. Its partner’s comes in at about 400 million, roughly a quarter as heavy.

As seen from Earth, the two are about 18,100 light-years apart, or about one-fifth the width of the Milky Way.

The two quasars’ light was stretched by almost exactly the same amount on its way here, which means they’re at nearly the same distance from us.

Both black holes are pulling in matter near or slightly above the Eddington limit. At that point, light from the falling gas pushes outward about as hard as gravity pulls inward.

Feeding that fast is typical for quasars this early, the researchers wrote.

The fainter quasar also looks redder, a sign that more dust is blocking its light.

Could the second quasar be a copy of the first? A massive galaxy between Earth and a quasar can bend the quasar’s light and make two or more copies of it. Astronomers call this gravitational lensing.

To test the idea, the team looked at light from gas spread across hundreds of light-years around each black hole.

Stars inside a lensing galaxy can distort light from a small area near a black hole, but not from gas spread that widely.

The light from the gas showed different patterns in the two spectra. The brighter source showed a stronger signature of gas flowing outward.

The researchers wrote that this difference favors two separate quasars.

Older Hubble images do show a galaxy near the line of sight to the fainter quasar, which could hold the extra dust or act as a lens. Its spread-out look suggests it’s fairly close to us, making strong lensing unlikely.

Even so, the team wrote, spectra alone can’t definitively rule out lensing.

Based on how quasars cluster over larger distances, a pair this close should be extremely rare.

Yet one turned up among the 199 quasars the team searched around. That’s roughly 100 to 1,000 times more clustering than the predictions suggest.

Predictions at such small distances are very uncertain, though, and one pair can’t show whether close pairs are really this common.

“Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua is a really unique environment,” Onorato said.

“It’s a laboratory for astronomers to study how the very first massive galaxies and their central black holes assembled together through direct galaxy interactions during these early times.”

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
