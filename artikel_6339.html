What makes a singing voice stand out? Scientists identify three key qualities

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

A singing study linked higher scores to expressive performances, rich voices, and the gentle pitch wobble called vibrato.

Singing teachers often describe a beautiful voice with words like “warm,” “ringing,” or “focused.” Two listeners can use the same word, though, and mean different things by it.

Researchers in Japan wanted a clearer answer, so they staged a small classical singing contest. Higher scores went with three qualities: vibrato, resonance, and expressiveness.

Clean pronunciation and accurate pitch showed no clear link to the final scores. And some of what the judges rewarded also showed up in measurements of the recorded voices.

Haruka Kondo, a doctoral student in vocal science at Keio University, led the study.

She worked with research fellow Sotaro Kondoh and with Shinya Fujii, who directs Keio’s Neurosciences and Music Laboratory.

All three belong to the university’s Research Center for Music Science.

“Our study bridges the gap between artistic intuition and science and does not aim to replace the expert ear. It helps translate what experienced listeners hear into a shared, measurable language,” said Kondo.

The team recruited 22 Japanese classical singers, each of whom was studying at a music university or had graduated from one. Ten were men and 12 were women. Their average age was about 25.

Each one sang the same fairly easy Italian art song, “Caro mio ben,” once and without accompaniment. A microphone about 8 inches (20 centimeters) from the singer’s mouth recorded every performance.

The five judges were professional singers with 11 to 34 years of teaching experience.

They sat in one row near the middle of a 720-seat hall on Keio’s Shonan Fujisawa Campus, three empty seats apart, with no audience around them.

To keep the scratch of pens off the recordings, the judges waited until each singer had finished before writing anything.

Scientists have measured singing voices since at least 1925, but studies comparing those measurements with expert opinion mostly used recordings.

Here the judges heard each singer live, in a room that shapes the sound on its way to the listener.

Each judge gave every singer an overall score out of 100 and rated six qualities on a scale from 1 to 7. Those were vibrato, resonance, expressiveness, timbre, diction, and intonation.

Vibrato is the regular rise and fall in pitch heard in classical singing.

Resonance covered how well a voice carried and how rich it sounded, while expressiveness took in emotion, changes in loudness, and the shaping of musical phrases.

A one-point rise in a singer’s vibrato, resonance, or expressiveness rating went with about 3 more points overall, even after accounting for the other five qualities.

Together, the six ratings accounted for 85% of the differences in scores.

The judges’ overall verdicts, in other words, closely followed the qualities they’d been asked to rate.

The top scorer, a 34-year-old baritone, got 100 from every judge, while a 19-year-old soprano with two years of vocal experience came last with an average of 54 points.

The team’s 2025 study turned out a little differently. There, judges heard recordings of 10 women singing the same song in a soundproof booth, and only vibrato stood out.

The authors suggest a live hall may make differences in projection easier to hear.

Diction means clear pronunciation, and intonation means accurate pitch.

Timbre describes a voice’s color, such as brightness or warmth, and none of the three showed a clear link to overall scores.

The authors point out that pronunciation and pitch have fixed targets, the written word and the written note. Once a singer hits them, there isn’t much room left to do better.

Everyone in the contest had university training, and the song asked relatively little of either skill.

In this setting, diction and intonation may have distinguished singers mainly when someone fell short.

Timbre is different, because it describes the kind of voice someone has. A darker voice isn’t a worse voice than a brighter one.

Vibrato, resonance, and expression are another matter. Singers can keep refining them long after the words and notes are right.

Trained classical voices often have extra energy between roughly 2,000 and 4,000 vibrations per second.

A peak in that range, called the singer’s formant, helps a voice carry across a room without a microphone.

The team compared the energy in that band with the energy at lower frequencies. Singers with more energy in the band scored higher, and that held for both men and women.

The microphone was close to each singer, while the judges listened from much farther away.

Because the link showed up anyway, the authors suggest that voices with a strong band near the mouth kept it all the way to the judges’ seats.

Loudness was a different story. How loud each singer was in the recordings showed no clear link to scores.

A 2008 study of live university singing auditions had tied louder singing to success among women, but not men.

The 2008 study measured sound about 33 feet (10 meters) away, and it let singers choose their own pieces.

The authors suggest that distance, and how much room a song leaves for loud singing, could explain the gap.

Vibrato has two parts that can be measured: how fast the pitch rises and falls, and how far it swings. Researchers call these the rate and the extent.

Professional classical singers usually produce about six of these pitch wobbles a second.

Among the men here, each extra wobble per second went with roughly 9 fewer points, while the women showed no clear link.

Extent showed the reverse pattern. The swing is measured in cents, and neighboring piano keys are 100 cents apart.

Among the women, each extra 10 cents went with about 1.7 more points, while the men showed no clear link.

Across all 22 singers, the average swing was 138 cents, a little more than the interval between neighboring piano keys.

The two singers with the fastest vibrato also had the narrowest swings, and the authors suggest a fast rate may be a sign of vibrato that hasn’t fully developed.

The vibrato figures for those two singers rest on very little data, though.

The analysis found vibrato in only about 1 in 10 of each singer’s vowels, leaving just 9 and 11 usable vowels, so the pair may have swayed the result.

The findings are associations, not proof that changing one quality would raise a score. The judges could also see the singers, so posture and facial expression may have influenced the expressiveness ratings.

Why wider vibrato mattered more among women is still unclear, especially since men and women had similar average extents.

Expectations may differ by voice type, and this study couldn’t separate voice type from sex.

Kondo sees possible uses in teaching, although the study didn’t test any of them.

“Our results show that something as personal and artistic as ‘a beautiful voice’ can be partly understood through science,” Kondo said.

“This could help students understand what their instructors mean when they say, ‘let it ring more’ or ‘your vibrato is too shallow.'”

The contest involved just 22 singers, five judges, and one easy song. Real competitions feature harder arias and songs that give performers more room for expression.

The authors want future studies to use several pieces from actual competition repertoire. Those studies would show whether the same three qualities still track the scores when the music gets harder.

The full study was published in the Journal of Voice.
