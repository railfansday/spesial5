Ice Age humans were far less isolated than scientists assumed, according to a 30,000-year-old woman’s DNA

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Ice Age Europeans mixed across the continent earlier than genetics had shown, according to a 30,000-year-old genome and DNA from cave dirt.

Most people imagine Ice Age Europe as a patchwork of tiny hunting bands living out their lives in isolated valleys, far from any other group.

Archaeologists have doubted that version of history for a long time. This is mainly due to the fact that the same styles of tools and carvings show up across the entire continent. Even so, DNA from Ice Age samples has mostly supported the isolated view.

A woman who died around 30,000 years ago in what is now southwestern France has changed that.

Most of her ancestry came from the western European people of her time. However, part of it also came from populations related to central and eastern Europe. The mixing happened roughly 32,500 to 31,500 years ago.

The analysis of her DNA now gives the oldest set of genome-wide data from a modern human in southwestern Europe.

Pere Gelabert and Ron Pinhasi of the University of Vienna‘s Department of Evolutionary Anthropology led the work. Their co-authors include researchers at Harvard Medical School, the University of Cantabria and the University of New Mexico.

The study rests on DNA from three new Ice Age individuals and one sample of cave sediment. Thus, the picture it draws is built from very few people.

The bone fragment of the woman was recovered from Isturitz Cave in the Basque Country, western Pyrenees. Excavators dug it up in the 1950s, and it stayed in a Paris institute for over 60 years. Then, in 2013, Pinhasi took a sample from it for DNA analysis.

The team recovered more than 454,000 of the genetic markers it was looking for. This represents one of the richest Gravettian datasets yet.

Gravettian is the name for the culture that spread across Europe during the onset of the last Ice Age, between about 34,000 and 25,000 years ago. This culture is known for its shared tool styles, burial practices and portable art.

Until now, DNA data from this Ice Age period in Europe sorted into two stable groups, a western one and a central-eastern one, with little sign of contact between them.

The Isturitz woman belongs mostly to the western group. But several tests also found a share of her ancestry related to the ancestors of people buried at Dolní Věstonice in what is now the Czech Republic.

In an interview with Earth.com, Gelabert said the find overturned a long-held assumption.

“Until this new genomic data, we assumed that the people in Southwestern Europe were quite the same for more than 20,000 years,” he said.

The new data, he said, point to population movements that were probably continental in scale, and that the team doesn’t yet clearly understand.

Isturitz is one of the richest Ice Age sites in southern Europe, with an unusual number of carved objects and signs of repeated, intensive occupation.

Archaeologists have long argued that it was a gathering place where different groups met to hunt, trade, socialize and find partners. The cave lies at the natural passage between the rest of Europe and the Iberian Peninsula.

The woman isn’t alone. When the team reanalyzed a previously published genome from Ormesson, near Paris, dated to about 31,800 to 30,000 years ago, it found a similar eastern signal.

In addition, Belgian individuals from Goyet Cave, about 28,000 to 26,000 years old, were already known to carry mixed ancestry.

Two explanations are possible, the authors wrote. People from the east may have moved west during the Gravettian itself. Or the eastern-looking ancestry may be an older component, already in western Europe before 35,000 years ago.

The team favors the first. The DNA signal is absent from earlier Ice Age genomes in the same region, and it vanishes from southwestern Europe after 30,000 years ago.

At El Mirón Cave in Cantabria, northern Spain, the team took two pinches of sediment, 0.002 ounces (50 milligrams) each.

They came from a layer dated to about 22,100 to 21,700 years ago, the coldest stretch of the last Ice Age, when human remains are extremely rare. No bone or tooth was needed, only the dirt that people had walked on.

The researchers built nine DNA libraries from those two extracts and sequenced them 101 times. Out of roughly a billion DNA fragments, more than 294,000 were human.

After the team kept only fragments showing the chemical damage of real ancient DNA, 14,737 genetic markers remained, enough to read the ancestry of the people who used the cave.

The sediment’s ancestry matched the western group, the Isturitz woman’s lineage, so that ancestry persisted in the region through the coldest millennia.

“Sedimentary DNA allows us to recover genetic information from periods where human remains are missing,” Pinhasi said. “This gives us a way to connect archaeological layers with the genetic ancestry of the people who occupied a site.”

Gelabert told Earth.com the team had recovered DNA from this cave’s sediment before. “What surprised us this time is that the quality is unmatched,” he said.

He described the sample as one of the richest sources of human DNA ever reported. For the first time in the region’s Upper Paleolithic, he said, it let the team tie a culture to a group of people.

The third new genome came from Ostuni Cave in southern Italy and belongs to a male fetus about 27,800 to 27,400 years old.

The team identified him as a first-degree relative of a woman from the same cave whose genome was already published. He was most likely her son.

Comparing the two showed that his father came from the same population as his mother, as far as the data can tell. Yet the parents were not close relatives.

The boy’s genome carried no long stretches of identical DNA from both parents, and the population he came from was genetically larger than any other Gravettian group measured so far.

Gelabert said this is the first mother-and-fetus pair of genomes from the European Paleolithic, and that comparing them is what showed how genetically diverse her population was.

All three individuals, and the people in the El Mirón sediment, are predicted to have had dark skin, dark hair and brown eyes. That was the usual pattern for Europeans of that era.

The whole Gravettian, Solutrean and Epigravettian record, three Ice Age cultures, rests on genome-wide data from fewer than 50 people. This study adds an extra three.

The third is a tooth from Chufín Cave in Cantabria, dated to about 26,000 years ago. It was so degraded that it yielded fewer than 20,000 markers, enough to place it in the western group and little more.

Sediment has its own limits. The DNA in it comes from several people mixed together, and the team’s reading suggests more of them were male than female. The date for the east-west mixing also assumes it happened in a single pulse.

The study does not settle where the eastern ancestry came from. The authors wrote that no particular central European group stands out as the source, and that no genome from the earlier Aurignacian culture exists for southwestern Europe to compare against.

The team did find one thing the data argue against. Earlier work on tooth shapes had suggested a population turnover in western Europe around 28,000 years ago.

The new genomes, the sediment and the reanalyzed published data instead show the same western ancestry in place from 30,000 to 20,000 years ago.

Asked by Earth.com what comes next, Gelabert returned to the sediment.

“Next step is clearly to find out when the population movements that we see in Isturitz really happened. Sediment will be key for this goal,” he said.

The authors named a target: the Aurignacian layers of Isturitz itself, deposits older than the woman, which could show whether the eastern signal was already there before she was born.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
