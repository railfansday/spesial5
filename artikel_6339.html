Scientists finally link human fossils found in China fifty years ago to the Denisovans, early human ancestors that we still know very little about

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Two bones from Xujiayao, dug up in China in 1976-79 and dated to 199,000-138,000 years ago, yielded 18 proteins that point to Denisovans.

Between 1976 and 1979, excavators in the Nihewan Basin of northern China recovered 20 human fossils at a site called Xujiayao. From there, it took nearly five decades to tie them to the Denisovan lineage, an extinct human group first identified from DNA in a Siberian cave.

The finds included pieces of skull and jaw and some teeth, buried with animal bones and artifacts that were mostly scrapers and spheroids. Researchers later filed them under five different names.

A team that includes Song Xing and Qiaomei Fu of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) in Beijing has now recovered 18 ancient proteins from two of those bones.

One small change in a collagen protein points both to the Denisovan lineage, although the proteins cannot show which Denisovan population the people belonged to.

Uranium-series and electron-spin-resonance dating place the Xujiayao layer at around 199,000 to 138,000 years ago. The first studies noted a thick skull vault and teeth that resembled those of Neanderthals.

Those early descriptions led scientists to call the people Homo sapiens neanderthalensis, early Homo sapiens or archaic Homo sapiens. When the arguments over sapiens stalled, some papers fell back on the neutral term archaic Homo.

A later study of the partial skull called Xujiayao 6 measured a braincase of about 1.8 quarts (1,700 milliliters). That unusually large figure made the skull the type specimen of a proposed species, Homo juluensis, one of several new names listed in a 2024 article in Nature Communications.

The name placed Xujiayao in a group apart from the Homo longi skull from Harbin and from fossils at Dali and Jinniushan. The new paper argues against that divide.

Animal bones from the same layer came first, and they gave a positive result for ancient protein. Next came the human fossils, a right parietal bone from Xujiayao 6 and a piece of the Xujiayao 14 mandible, labeled PA1490 and PA1497 in the lab. Each went through a 3D scan before anyone touched it with a drill.

Researchers in protective gear drilled one small hole in each bone in a clean room at the institute’s Molecular Paleontology Laboratory. They wrapped the surrounding surface in aluminum foil and kept the holes as small as they could.

The powder went into hydrochloric acid and then a 149-degree Fahrenheit (65-degree Celsius) bath for three hours, which split each sample into three fractions.

Empty blanks ran alongside to catch contamination, and the bone of a modern Bactrian camel went through the same steps.

Two labs read the fragments on mass spectrometers, one at Capital Medical University and one at Fudan University in Shanghai.

One software search returned 15,194 unique peptides, the short chains of amino acids that make up proteins, across the two samples.

Old proteins carry a chemical wear called deamidation, and the team used it to separate ancient material from modern traces. Keratins and the trypsin used in the lab showed far less of it.

After further filtering and a check in a second software program, 18 proteins remained, 13 in the skull bone and 15 in the jaw.

The decisive signal sits in COL1A2, one of the chains of type I collagen, the main protein in bone. At position 996, Neanderthals and modern humans carry the amino acid arginine.

Every Denisovan tested so far carries lysine there instead, according to the paper. That reference group consists of two individuals from Xiahe, Penghu 1, the Harbin skull and Denisova 3 from Siberia.

Both Xujiayao bones showed lysine. The skull bone gave 57 peptides with the variant and the jaw gave 61, while each sample gave only one low-quality peptide with arginine.

The researchers then built a family tree from 2,074 amino acid positions in eight proteins, adding Neanderthals, 50 present-day people and a chimpanzee for comparison. Xujiayao grouped with Denisova 3 and other Denisovan-related individuals with a probability above 99 percent.

Xujiayao ranks “among the earliest molecularly confirmed Denisovans,” the authors wrote in Nature. Similar protein work identified a jaw from a Tibetan Plateau cave at Baishiya Karst in Gansu, dated to at least 160,000 years ago.

With the fossils assigned, the team compared their anatomy against other human relatives, using CT scans at a resolution of 37.56 microns.

They took 26 linear measurements, three angles and two indices from the Xujiayao 6 skull. C.-H. Chang shared a 3D model of the Penghu mandible, and X. Wu allowed the scanning of casts from Xuchang and Hualongdong.

Parts of the skull look like Homo erectus. The vault measures 0.30 inches (7.7 millimeters) thick at the top of the head and 0.47 inches (12 millimeters) at the parietal bulge, and the braincase is low and wide.

Other parts look more recent. A strongly arched temporal bone also appears in later species, including modern humans, while the inner-ear layout and, in the Xuchang fossils, a small pit at the back of the skull recall late Neanderthals.

The mix also has its own signature. The authors list a low skull with nearly parallel side walls, a massive brow ridge, a flat cheek region and a lower face that barely juts forward.

The paper reaches beyond Xujiayao. Its authors compared the anatomy with the Harbin skull known as Dragon Man and a jawbone from Penghu, off Taiwan, and they assigned most of East Asia’s late Middle Pleistocene fossils to the Denisovan lineage, including Jinniushan, Dali and Hualongdong.

The team also rejected the brain-size argument for Homo juluensis. Braincases in that group hold 1.8 to 1.9 quarts (1,700 to 1,800 milliliters) and the other East Asian fossils 1.2 to 1.5 quarts (1,120 to 1,420 milliliters), but modern humans alone span 1.15 to 2 quarts (1,090 to 1,880 milliliters).

If shape and molecules count together, the authors argued, Denisovans lived in East Asia from at least 300,000 to 45,000 years ago. The authors linked their disappearance from the region to a major migration of modern humans around 40,000 years ago.

The authors wrote that adding other skulls gives “a nearly complete picture of the cranial morphology of Denisovans.” That portrait is a composite.

Its account of a sloping forehead, square eye sockets and a broad nasal opening relies on facial bones from Harbin, Jinniushan, Hualongdong and Dali, and it also matches features predicted from Denisovan DNA.

Only Harbin among those four has molecular confirmation in the paper. Jinniushan, Dali and Hualongdong join the lineage on shape alone, and the authors note that the full set of traits does not appear together in any single specimen.

The protein evidence also has limits. It rests on one reliable variant, known from five reference individuals, and it comes from two of the 20 Xujiayao fossils. Within the Denisovan branch, individuals formed an unstable arrangement with probabilities below 50 percent.

A test for a variant specific to Penghu produced no reliable result, so the team placed Xujiayao only in a late Middle Pleistocene East Asian Denisovan population. The 1970s excavators left the bones with a site name, and a drill hole in each of two pieces has now added a lineage.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
