A “sponsored” label marks an influencer’s post as an ad, but it doesn’t stop the ad from working, so cut how many ads reach you. Unfollow or mute shopping accounts, switch off the ad settings that use your browsing elsewhere, and put anything you first saw in a feed on a wish list for a day.

One home-decor pin leads to a lamp, the lamp to a matching rug and throw pillows, and soon $200 of pieces you didn’t want a week ago is sitting in your cart.

Ads use the same few tactics again and again. A crossed-out “was” price makes the real price look like a deal. A “limited drop” or a sale that ends Sunday pushes you to buy now. Star ratings and a creator using the product at home suggest everyone loves it. And the fifth time the same lamp shows up in your feed, it feels familiar, which is easy to mistake for wanting it.

On social apps, those tactics come from creators you follow and from ads that look like your friends’ posts. The FTC revised its Endorsement Guides in 2023. Its guide for influencers tells creators who are paid or given free products to say so in plain words like “ad” or “sponsored,” placed where they’re hard to miss. That label tells you it’s an ad, but it doesn’t stop the tactics from working. The lamp still looks good in a living room you admire, and the fifth sighting still feels familiar.

So the cheapest defense is seeing fewer ads and sponsored posts. Unfollow or mute the shopping accounts and the creators whose posts mostly sell something. On Instagram, muting hides someone’s posts without unfollowing them, and they aren’t told. Then switch off the ad settings that use your browsing on other sites:

You’ll still see ads, but they won’t be based on what you browsed on store sites.

Last, put anything you first saw in a feed on a wish list, and give it the full day the 24-hour rule calls for.

After the wait, buy only what you’d have gone looking for without a post showing it to you.
