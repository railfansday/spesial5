Giant anteaters traipsing across Brazil’s pristine wetlands and savannahs have ingested traces of plastic pollutants. Credit: Sylvain Cordier/Gamma-Rapho via Getty

Giant anteaters (Myrmecophaga tridactyla) and lowland tapirs (Tapirus terrestris) across Brazil have traces of microplastics circulating in their blood — even if they live more than 60 kilometres away from human infrastructure. Researchers found that these animals carry roughly 74 particles per millilitre of blood on average — similar to the levels of plastics found in humans — with the highest levels found in those that lived closest to urban areas and waste-management facilities. The findings are an important step to better understand how these particles impact wildlife, says geochemist Sofia Ya-Hsuan Liou.

Artificial-intelligence giant Google DeepMind has released AlphaGenome 2, an upgrade to their AI tool that predicts the molecular effects of ‘non-coding’ DNA sequences. The tool, announced on 11 November, has already impressed researchers. Its underlying AI model was trained on gene activity and other measurements made in more than 100 million individual cells, across 68 tissues. But the tool can also make predictions when fed data from cell types that it wasn’t trained on, which could help researchers more quickly identify variants underlying disease, says DeepMind scientist Adam Gayoso.

An approach that measures waist circumference and focuses on excess body fat could be a more accurate way to diagnose obesity — and identify people with related health risks — than body mass index (BMI). In an analysis of data from more than 800,000 people, researchers found that using BMI to diagnose obesity failed to identify a considerable proportion of people with elevated health risks caused by excess body fat. The new framework classified an extra 5–9% of people as having obesity, who would have been labelled only as overweight with the BMI-based approach.

Bioengineered bacteria that ‘fix’ nitrogen into a useful form for plants could replace up to half of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, slashing the greenhouse-gas emissions associated with these chemicals, researchers suggest. Bacteria can deliver nitrogen to roots more efficiently than synthetic fertilizers, and scientists are engineering them to feed nitrogen to crop plants that don’t have natural microbial partners, such as maize and wheat. Gene-edited biofertilizers have already been used in the United States, but a slew of advances are needed to demonstrate higher levels of nitrogen fixation.

In July, artificial-intelligence titan Anthropic acquired a biotechnology company that developed two promising drug leads with just one full-time human employee and an AI assistant. The mini-firm benefited from the help of several part-time consultants and external research organizations to craft its products — two early-stage antibodies designed to treat a rare form of muscular dystrophy — and many more researchers will be required to take the drugs forward. But the acquisition has sparked discussion about whether one-person, AI-enabled biotech is the drug-discovery business model of the future.

With the rise of multidisciplinary science and artificial intelligence, biologists are “poised to at last understand the complexity of life”, writes epigeneticist Edith Heard, chief executive of the Francis Crick Institute, London. But people are what will unlock these discoveries, not technology alone. “Funders, institutions and scientific leaders must embrace new technologies while protecting the conditions in which discovery thrives: curiosity, collaboration and, above all, the freedom and support to follow unexpected observations to see where they lead,” Heard writes.

“Environmental change is altering the conditions in which brains develop and minds are sustained”, says a new report from a commission convened by Nature Mental Health. For example, heat, air and noise pollution and loss of green space have been linked with depression, anxiety and cognitive decline — with poorer people, children and older people among those most exposed. The Earth–Brain–Health Commission offers a new framework for uniting neuroscience, psychiatry, public health and environmental science around shared challenges and solutions.

Today I’m adding M. John Harrison’s The End of Everything to my reading list. The post-apocalyptic novel was announced as the winner of this year’s Booker prize on 9 November — the first “bona fide” science-fiction novel to win the award, says Gaby Wood, chief executive of the Booker Prize Foundation.
