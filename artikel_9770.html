AI Is Everywhere, but Where Are the Productivity Gains?

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

On 13 November 2026, Federal Reserve Vice Chair Philip Jefferson quoted a 39-year-old book review at the UC San Diego Economic Roundtable. “Productivity growth has in fact moved up in recent years, but it’s too early to say what role AI played in that development,” he said. In the same speech, he said of Robert Solow, “He may have been expecting too much from the computers he saw.”

He wasn’t the first to reach for Solow. Apollo Chief Economist Torsten Slok wrote in February 2026 that “AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data.” In May, St. Louis Fed president Alberto Musalem borrowed the line in Reykjavik: “today we see AI everywhere but in the aggregate productivity statistics.”

That sits awkwardly next to what I wrote on 7 November in the Jevons essay. “On the output side, software already looks like coal in 1865,” I said. I was counting tokens, new GitHub repositories, and App Store submissions, and those are real numbers. I stand by them. They’re also counts of activity, and I hadn’t checked whether they turn into shipped work.

So I went to check. I read Solow’s 1987 page, how it broke, and the 2026 numbers for engineering teams and US output. My reading is that software is running Solow’s paradox in miniature. This time, you can see the bottleneck.

On 12 July 1987, Robert Solow reviewed Manufacturing Matters, Cohen and Zysman’s book on programmable automation, on page 36 of the New York Times Book Review. He faulted them for ducking an awkward fact: everyone felt a technological revolution, yet productivity growth had slowed everywhere, Japan included. He closed with an aside:

I checked Yoram Bauman’s scan of that page. Almost nobody quotes it right. Paul David, Erik Brynjolfsson, and Robert Gordon all misquote it, and even McKinsey Global Institute mangled it in a report whose advisory committee Solow chaired.

The numbers behind his joke were grim. On the Bureau of Labor Statistics long-term table, US nonfarm business labor productivity grew 2.7% a year from 1947 Q1 to 1973 Q4. Computed from the BLS index, it averaged 1.4% a year from 1973 to 1995.

Then in 1996, the line broke upward. Computed from the same index, growth averaged 3.1% a year from 1995 to 2004, fourteen to fifteen years after IBM’s August 1981 PC. In March 2000, as the Cleveland Fed and Gordon note, New York Times reporter Louis Uchitelle reported Solow saying you could now see computers in the productivity statistics.

Part of that wait was arithmetic: in 1994, Stephen Oliner and Daniel Sichel showed from BEA data that computer hardware was 2.0% of the nominal net capital stock in 1993. Between 1970 and 1992, it added 0.16 percentage points a year to output growth. The slice was too small.

In 1990, Paul David looked for the deeper holdup in how factories change, and compared computers to the electric dynamo. Central power stations opened in New York and London in the early 1880s, yet in 1899 electric motors drove under 5% of American factory machinery. Factories didn’t pass halfway until the early 1920s, four decades later. That’s when manufacturing productivity jumped.

Replacing serviceable steam plants didn’t pay right away. Until about 1920, factories ran on group drive: they kept their overhead shafts and belts, and hooked electric motors to sections of shafting. Swapping the power source doesn’t give you a new factory. Productivity jumped only after unit drive put a motor on each machine. That allowed lighter single-storey buildings, better materials flow, and repairs without halting the plant.

Reorganization shows up as cost before output. In 2002, Brynjolfsson, Lorin Hitt, and Yang found each computer dollar at large firms went with over $10 of market value, against just over $1 for other tangible assets. They read that gap as intangible organizational capital, though it’s a market correlation rather than an asset count. Later, Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson called the dip-then-rise the productivity J-curve.

Most people stop the Solow story there. I think the two sequels matter more.

In 2001, McKinsey Global Institute broke the late-1990s acceleration down by industry and found the boom was narrow. Six sectors held 31% of nonfarm private GDP and 99% of the net jump: retail, wholesale, securities, telecom, semiconductors, and computer manufacturing. The other 53 sectors managed 0.3% a year. That’s despite 62% of the rise in IT intensity. Even in retail, much of what put Wal-Mart ahead, the big-box store format, didn’t depend on IT.

It didn’t last either. Between 2004 and 2019, US labor productivity fell back to 1.5% a year, computed from the BLS index. Gordon argued in 2012 that the computer revival lasted just eight years.

I find that a cooler parable than the slogan. The gains took a decade and a half, needed new workflows, landed in six industries, and lasted under ten years.

If AI breaks Solow’s paradox faster than PCs did, software is the first place to look. Developers actually use the tools. In Stack Overflow’s 2026 survey of over 30,000 developers, 73% of AI users were on them every day.

In April 2026, Google CEO Sundar Pichai wrote, “Today, 75% of all new code at Google is now AI-generated and approved by engineers, up from 50% last fall.” Anthropic’s institute reported the same year that “more than 80% of the code we merge into Anthropic’s codebase was authored by Claude.” Its typical engineer was merging eight times as much code per day as in 2024.

Those are company counts of code written or merged. They don’t tell you how much software shipped. Even Anthropic’s institute warns that its figure “is almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain.”

When independent researchers time developers, results split by design. Sida Peng and colleagues at Microsoft Research, GitHub, and MIT ran a randomized trial in 2023 with 95 Upwork freelancers building one JavaScript HTTP server. The Copilot finishers “completed the task 55.8% faster,” and less experienced coders gained more. One greenfield task can’t tell you much about maintaining a real codebase.

Field trials inside big firms find smaller wins. In 2025, Cui and colleagues, two at Microsoft, pooled three trials covering 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 firm. Copilot access lifted weekly completed pull requests by 26.08%. Short-tenure developers gained more than long-tenure ones, though that gap wasn’t statistically significant.

Then came the July 2025 trial from METR, a nonprofit, which ran a randomized trial with 16 experienced open-source developers working on 246 real issues in their own repositories, averaging over 22,000 stars and a million lines of code. Using mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet, the developers expected AI to speed them up by 24%. Afterward, they still believed it had sped them up by 20%. On the clock, they took 19% longer, with a confidence interval from +2% to +39%.

I wouldn’t wave that result around as proof AI slows everyone down, and neither does METR. Their paper warns that veterans on familiar repos with early tools don’t represent most coding. The post now carries a banner reading “These results are out of date.”

METR’s late-2025 follow-up with 57 developers and over 800 tasks broke down by February 2026. Early numbers pointed to an 18% speedup for returning developers and 4% for new recruits. Both ranges crossed zero. Worse, developers kept withholding tasks and declining to join because “they do not wish to work without AI.” METR hasn’t published its redesigned trial yet, and I couldn’t find another 2026 randomized trial on professional developers.

I don’t think those results conflict once you put the designs side by side. Greenfield tasks and junior tickets speed up fast. Million-line repos you already know don’t. Sebastian Maier and four co-authors pooled 23 studies in May 2026 and found a moderate positive effect overall, bigger in controlled experiments and smaller in open-source and enterprise codebases.

Developers see that limit too, though Stack Overflow rewrote its trust question in 2026, so the answers can’t be compared with earlier years. Only 6.6% trust AI output for important work decisions. Nearly half, 48.0%, picked “I trust it when I can easily verify the output.”

Verification is where the queue forms. In the compiler essay, I wrote that when you speed up one step, work piles up in front of the next slowest step. Two large 2026 studies measured that pileup.

The first is “Writing Code vs. Shipping Code” by Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff, and Liyuan Yang, released through NBER in May 2026 and revised in September. They matched over 500,000 GitHub developers with AI telemetry in an event study. At the top of the funnel, weekly commits rose about 30% with autocomplete. Adding interactive agents brought the cumulative effect to about 180%, and adding autonomous agents to about 240%.

Downstream, those gains shrink at every handoff. That jump in commits falls to 80% for projects, and to 30% for actual releases. The release figure stops at interactive agents, because the paper doesn’t report releases for autonomous ones. Across four app marketplaces, they find “a sharp increase in the number of new apps but no increase in total usage.”

The second is “Artificial Intelligence in the Firm: Bottlenecks in Software Production,” a Harvard job market paper by Fiona Chen and James Stratton updated in November 2026. Using Jellyfish data on 720,542 workers at 655 firms from 2021 through August 2026, they compared companies adopting AI tools at different times. Twelve months after a firm adopted coding assistants, pull requests opened per worker were up 20.9%. Completed epics moved 3.4% and completed issues fell 1.5%. Neither change was statistically significant.

Adding AI agents pushed the top harder: another 35.7% on pull requests opened, on top of the assistants’ effect. Completed epics rose 18.0% and issues 13.7%, both just short of statistical significance. In the middle, with agents, “the average time from the code’s review submission to its merge increases by 56.1%.” Their conclusion is blunt. “Code review becomes the bottleneck,” they write.

I read both papers as careful estimates rather than lab proof. Neither is a randomized trial, and issues and epics are proxies for real value.

Automated tests don’t clear the queue either: in March 2026, METR had four maintainers of three repositories blind-review 296 AI pull requests. They found “roughly half of test-passing SWE-bench Verified PRs” wouldn’t be merged into main. Passing tests isn’t the same as passing a maintainer.

Code quality adds drag on top of volume, according to Hao He and four colleagues at Carnegie Mellon, who tracked Cursor-adopting open-source repositories against matched controls. They found a large, temporary jump in velocity alongside a lasting rise in static-analysis warnings and code complexity. A January 2026 follow-up from the same group measured roughly 18% more warnings and 39% higher cognitive complexity with agents.

I’d treat vendor datasets cautiously, since they’re correlational and have no control groups. In its 2025 telemetry report, Faros AI wrote that “we observed no significant correlation between AI adoption and improvements at the company level.” By Faros’s 2026 report across about 22,000 developers, median review time had grown fivefold and weekly deployments were down 11.7%. Google Cloud’s 2025 DORA survey agrees. Throughput turned positive, while “AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability.”

This is Paul David’s group drive happening in real time. Software teams hooked coding agents onto a factory floor built around human typing speed: line-by-line human reviews, manual QA passes, tribal context, and test suites that don’t catch half of what maintainers care about. Typing got fast. The plates stacked up at the pass.

Outside software, Solow’s paradox has a twist. Nobody can say investment is too small. Amazon projected in October that its 2026 cash capital expenditures would be “approximately $220 billion,” up from $131.8 billion in 2025. Meta put its outlook at $135 billion to $145 billion, roughly double 2025. Net of imports, ING estimated in August that tech investment drove 36% of year-over-year US GDP growth in the second quarter.

Headline US labor productivity really is up: on 5 November 2026, the BLS reported preliminary third-quarter nonfarm business productivity up 3.6% at an annual rate. It’s averaged 2.2% a year since Q4 2019, against 1.5% from Q4 2007 to Q4 2019. Q3 isn’t final yet, and single quarters swing hard. Still, the multi-year lift is clear.

That puts Erik Brynjolfsson ahead of Robert Gordon in Long Bet #868, their $400 wager that 2020 to 2029 private nonfarm business productivity beats 1.8% a year. Computed from the BLS index, it needs 0.7% to 0.9% a year across the remaining thirteen quarters. In a February 2026 Financial Times op-ed summarized by Fortune, Brynjolfsson argued we’re moving into “a harvest phase.”

Federal Reserve officials still quote Solow anyway. If you look one layer down, the numbers are quieter. On the BLS annual release, private nonfarm business total factor productivity averaged 1.0% a year over 2019 to 2025, against 0.6% over 2007 to 2019.

The gap between those two series matters, because when you pile capital behind each worker, labor productivity climbs. TFP doesn’t move until you get more from the same combined inputs. Slok wrote in October that “strong output per hour alongside flat TFP is the signature of capital deepening, not of a technology shock.” In November, Federal Reserve Board staff concluded “the contribution of generative AI thus far is likely small,” and the pickup “appears mostly to predate the current AI innovations.”

Firm surveys depend on who you ask. In the US Census Bureau’s Business Trends and Outlook Survey, 19.8% of US firms used AI in any business function in May 2026. Among firms with 250 or more employees, it was 37%. In “Firm Data on AI,” revised in October 2026, Ivan Yotzov, Nicholas Bloom, and colleagues surveyed nearly 9,000 CFOs, CEOs, and senior executives across seven countries. In that summer sample, 78% of firms used AI.

Those are two surveys of two populations. Even those executives put AI’s annual productivity lift at just 0.2% over the past three years. They expect 0.6% a year over the next three.

A surge does show up in digital volume: GitHub’s Octoverse 2026 counted 34.3 billion commits against 12.9 billion a year earlier, on recalculated figures that aren’t comparable with the prior edition. Over 40% of September pull requests were started by an agent. On Apple’s App Store, Sensor Tower counted about 560,000 new apps in the first half of 2026, as 9to5Mac cited from the New York Times. Downloads “grew 2 percent to 17.6 billion.”

One official industry series jumped too. In 2025, BLS Software Publishers (NAICS 5112) labor productivity rose 12.9%, well above its 4.4% average for 2019 to 2025. I’d read it with two caveats: BLS derives output from deflated revenue, so price indexes matter, and it doesn’t publish an index for computer systems design. Still, I find that jump hard to ignore.

At the cautious end, Daron Acemoglu estimated in May 2024 that ten-year TFP gains should be “no more than 0.66% in total,” roughly 0.064 percentage points a year. His model doesn’t count new products or new tasks. The Penn Wharton Budget Model projected in September 2025 that productivity would be 1.5% higher by 2035, with the boost peaking at 0.2 percentage points a year in 2032.

At the high end, Aghion and Bunel’s June 2024 paper put the task-model median at 0.68 percentage points a year, and 0.8 to 1.3 points by historical analogy. Goldman Sachs projected in March 2023 just under 1.5 percentage points a year over ten years, with the clock starting “following widespread adoption.”

I think they’re arguing over the same two questions from 1995. One is how fast firms move from group drive to unit drive, rebuilding review and verification instead of only speeding up drafts. The other is whether gains stay inside a few digital industries, like the 1990s six, or reach the other 53.

Here’s what I’d bet, with dates and the public numbers that settle each one.

On US headline numbers, I bet Erik Brynjolfsson wins Long Bet #868. Nonfarm business labor productivity already sits at 2.2% through Q3 2026. Computed from the BLS index, it needs under 1% a year over the remaining thirteen quarters to clear 1.8%. Total factor productivity is a different story. I’m setting my own threshold there at 1.5% a year, and betting annual private nonfarm business TFP won’t average 1.5% or more across 2026 to 2028. Capital deepening lifts output per hour years before workflow redesign lifts TFP. The early-2030 BLS Productivity and Costs release and the 2029 annual BLS TFP release settle it. I’m wrong if a slump hands Gordon the bet, or if TFP clears my bar.

On how wide the boom spreads, I bet the gains stay concentrated in software-heavy sectors through 2028. BLS Software Publishers (NAICS 5112) keeps beating its 4.4% average from 2019 to 2025. Elsewhere, adoption stays patchy. Census BTOS stays below 50% of US firms using AI, and multi-industry executive surveys keep AI’s annual lift under 1 percentage point. The 2028 BLS industry release, late-2028 BTOS data, and follow-up waves of Yotzov and Bloom’s Firm Data on AI settle it. I’m wrong if Software Publishers goes flat or negative in 2026 and 2027, or if BTOS passes half of US firms and non-tech sectors match software’s jump by then.

Inside engineering teams, I bet code volume keeps outrunning shipped releases through 2027. User adoption lags behind too. Commits, pull requests, and new app submissions stay multiples ahead of actual releases until review pipelines stop merge latency from climbing. GitHub Octoverse 2027, Appfigures and Sensor Tower app-store counts, and follow-up firm telemetry or METR’s redesigned trial settle it by early 2028. I’m wrong if a large study by then shows completed work matching pull requests without longer queues, or if app usage jumps alongside new releases.

If you run an engineering team, stop counting lines of code and AI-authored share. You can fool yourself fast with activity numbers. Watch how long a pull request waits for a verified merge, track production incidents, and check whether shipped features change what users actually do. Until those move together, you’ve bolted an electric motor onto the old shafting.
