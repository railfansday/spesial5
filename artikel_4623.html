Three Ways Families Can Make Mental Health Problems Worse

In 1959, a sociologist named George Brown led a study of 156 men diagnosed with schizophrenia who had recently been discharged. Most went to live with family members: parents, spouses, siblings. A smaller group lived alone in rented rooms. Brown followed them for a year. He found that among those who lived with family, about one-third ended up in the hospital. Of those who lived alone, none did.

More studies followed. Data from a 1976 study suggested that cutting back on time with a critical relative was roughly as protective as antipsychotic medication against relapse in schizophrenia.

A 1989 study showed that the single best predictor of relapse among patients who’d been hospitalized for depression was their response to the question, “How critical is your spouse of you?”

A 1998 meta-analysis found that a critical or overinvolved family was linked to poor outcomes for conditions as diverse as depression, bipolar disorder, and eating disorders.

What’s interesting is that these aren’t families you’d ordinarily deem “abusive.” The standard framework for assessing family dysfunction today, the adverse childhood experiences framework, doesn’t capture them. These studies examine lesser forms of dysfunction, what many of us would call “normal.”

I’m going to wager that you’ve never heard of this body of research. As a historian, I find that this raises an equally fascinating question: Why not?

In the 1960s, psychiatrists were quick to blame parents for mental illness, especially mothers. Schizophrenia was explained by a “schizophrenogenic mother,” autism by a “refrigerator mother.” But the more careful psychiatrists emphasized that the problem was never solely the mother, or solely the father, but the family dynamic that engulfed mother, father, and child.

By the late 1970s, however, parents of patients were tired of being told that their kids’ mental illness was their fault. This era led to the emergence of groups like the National Alliance for the Mentally Ill, founded in 1979 by two Wisconsin mothers whose sons had been diagnosed with schizophrenia.

Such groups promoted the emerging biological perspective that viewed mental illnesses as brain disorders. Historian Anne Harrington, in Mind Fixers (2019), documents the role such advocacy groups played in popularizing the biomedical view.

By the 1990s, the biomedical model was firmly established in the American psyche. Everything was now a chemical imbalance. Family dynamics seemed to be off the table for good.

Today, psychiatrists claim that they’ve moved away from narrow biological reductionism to accept a “biopsychosocial” model, one that explores the whole range of factors that create and sustain mental illness. You’d think the family would have been put back on the map. It wasn’t, with one partial exception.

Advocates of the biopsychosocial framework often use the adverse childhood experiences framework to assess family precursors to mental illness. But its entries center on extreme forms of trauma, abuse, and neglect. The lesser forms of family dysfunction that psychiatrists once sought to understand remain largely off the table.

To my knowledge, only one research tradition has kept the family in view from the 1950s to today: “expressed emotion” research. It has built a robust body of findings.

Its core finding, put at the most general level, is that high “expressed emotion” in families is a strong predictor of relapse in numerous illnesses, including schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, and eating disorders. To break it down a bit more:

1. Criticism: When the family member expresses constant disapproval, or when they insinuate that the person is lazy or selfish.

2. Hostility: When the family member disparages the person for who they are, or when they believe that the person could “snap out of it.”

3. Emotional overinvolvement: When the family shows excessive concern for the person’s well-being, and when they convey the message that the person is “unable to cope” with the world.

A 1994 analysis of 25 studies, covering more than 1,300 patients around the world, found that the link between expressed emotion and relapse was “overwhelming.” More recent work, by researchers such as Preethi Premkumar, has extended these findings to people who have never been diagnosed with anything, showing that young adults with mild psychosis-like traits (“schizotypy”) see their relatives as more critical, and the feeling of being criticized goes along with depression and anger.

As I know from my own experience, having a loved one with schizophrenia or other serious mental disorder can be overwhelming, taxing, and uncertain. It can create a mix of intense feelings like pity, guilt, and anger.

Proponents of expressed emotion research emphasize that the solution isn’t to lay more shame and blame on families, but counseling and education. Clinical practice guidelines universally recommend family counseling.

Can families cause mental illness? The jury’s still out. But they can sustain mental health crises and make them more likely to recur. That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t go home for the holidays. But it may help to be aware of how family dynamics can affect your mental health.
