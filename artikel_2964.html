AI Agents vs. Banks: Who Will Win? I'm Betting on Banks.

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Doom-and-gloom predictions about AI agents destroying banks’ business model seem premature at best.

With the September launch of the Muse personal artificial intelligence (AI) agent from Meta Platforms (META -1.20%), and other recent advances in agentic AI, some AI industry observers believe AI agents could disrupt the banking industry.

Agentic AI assistants like Meta's Muse could help automate people's personal finances. With user permission to access personal banking accounts and credit card data, Muse can manage advanced personal financial tasks such as canceling subscriptions or negotiating bills.

If AI agents' capabilities improve to the point that people can easily move money between bank accounts, this could hurt banks' business models. What if an autonomous agent could quickly find better deals to move your extra cash from a zero-interest checking account to another bank's high-yield savings account? What if AI agents get into an online arms race to game the system for credit card rewards?

If AI agents become more active managers of people's money, it could drive up banks' costs of doing business, cut their profit margins, and deprive them of checking account funds, which are a key source of cheap deposits. Could banks lose market share or even lose market cap because of AI agents?I'm skeptical. I think there are a few reasons banks won't let their business get hijacked by AI agents.

Just because AI agents have the potential to get into bank accounts and upend the world of banking doesn't mean banks have to let them. Banks are not rolling out the red carpet for AI agents. Many major U.S. banks, including Citigroup (C -1.14%), Bank of America (BAC -1.28%), and JPMorgan Chase (JPM -2.44%), have already announced that they will not allow third-party AI agents or assistants to access customer accounts. JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon recently said he doesn't want "rogue agents" in customer accounts.

Rick Wurster, CEO of Charles Schwab (SCHW -1.18%), recently struck a similar chord, telling Bloomberg in an interview last month:

"We don't want someone just giving their password to Muse, and letting Muse come in and take action on that client's behalf. We can't control it, the client can't control it, something could go wrong and who's liable?"

Banks are required by law to know their customers. Banks must be able to verify that any requests to transfer funds or execute transactions are valid and authorized by a real person who controls the account. Unless regulations change to clarify new rules, limits, and liabilities for agentic AI banking, banks are likely to keep restricting third-party agents.

For example, on Sept. 30, Charles Schwab announced the launch of Charley, the company's new AI-powered assistant. Charley doesn't offer the same capabilities as a full-service automated AI agent, but it can help Schwab clients find answers, access account information, and enhance the customer service experience.

According to CNBC, JPMorgan Chase recently started a pilot for a new AI tool called "Smart Cash." This tool would analyze a customer's cash flow and help move money to maximize the customer's yield.

Banks aren't going to sit around and wait for AI companies to disrupt their business and take their deposits. It seems more likely that banks will use the competitive threat posed by AI agents as inspiration to develop their own "killer banking apps" powered by AI technology.

Do people really want to hand over their financial lives to AI? There's been a lot of hype in recent years about open banking, fintech apps, neobanks, and challenger banks. But despite all the digital banking hype, traditional brick-and-mortar banks seem to have a strong foothold with customers. Reporting from The Financial Brand in July found that major banks have opened more than 1,000 new bank branches in each of the past three years.
