The price of success nobody warns founders about: Who are you after you sell your company?

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

We entrepreneurs spend decades learning how to build, scale, and exit successful businesses, and there are countless books, podcasts, courses, and articles dedicated to the pursuit of this goal.

Yet almost nobody teaches us how to live after the exit.

In 2008, I did what most entrepreneurs dream of doing. I exited the business I’d spent nearly two decades building, just before the global financial crisis. By almost any measure, it was a remarkable success.

What I discovered, and what I want to share with my fellow entrepreneurs, is that no one tells you about the psychological consequences of success. We celebrate the exit as the ultimate achievement: the culmination of years of sacrifice, sleepless nights, and relentless determination. A liquidity event takes place and the founder collects the check and disappears into a life of freedom and fulfilment. That’s the narrative, anyway.

But my experience was very different, because my identity was fully linked to my business and everything it represented. I didn’t recognize it then, but I’d confused what I did with who I was. Without the business, I wasn’t entirely sure who I was anymore, and almost overnight my identity disappeared.

What I’ve come to find out is that this is much more common than you may think it is.

The hidden cost of no exit plan

I built my property advisory business, Ober-Haus, after arriving in Estonia in the early 1990s, and developed it into a company that operated across seven countries. For nearly 20 years, my life revolved around building, negotiating, financing, and solving problems. I loved it and became good at it.

Then I sold the company. Suddenly, I had the financial freedom I’d only dreamed about and spent most of my adult life pursuing—but that was when things began to unravel. Years before building Ober-Haus, I’d confronted alcoholism, entered recovery, and rebuilt my life. Yet I hadn’t fully recognized how easily the compulsive drive that once fueled my drinking could find a more socially celebrated outlet: ambition and success.

After my exit, I moved to Argentina and immersed myself in a world of horses, polo, travel, and an extraordinary lifestyle. From the outside, it looked like the perfect reward for decades of hard work. Parts of it were wonderful, but something fundamental was missing.

I’d spent so long pursuing financial independence that I hadn’t seriously considered what I would do with it once I had it. The adrenaline of building a business had been replaced by the pleasures of spending the proceeds. What I mistook for freedom was, in some ways, another form of escape. And the purpose of life disappeared slowly until finally it was gone.

Over time, my wealth diminished. My marriage collapsed. I made difficult financial decisions and watched much of what I’d accumulated disappear. The exit that was supposed to secure my future became the beginning of one of the most difficult periods of my life, and came with a hidden cost that I’d never considered.

A darker side to success

Looking back, I can see that the problem began long before I sold my company.

Entrepreneurs develop qualities that help us succeed: drive, competitiveness, resilience, an appetite for risk, and an almost obsessive determination to achieve our goals. These characteristics can build extraordinary businesses, but they don’t necessarily build extraordinary lives.

We become accustomed to controlling outcomes, solving problems and measuring progress. We approach relationships like negotiations and personal fulfilment like another business objective. When something isn’t working, our instinct is to push harder or cut our losses. Success demands sacrifice; we accept that almost without thinking, whether it’s another missed family dinner, another canceled holiday, or another year promising ourselves that life will slow down after the next transaction.

But there comes a moment when success starts asking for something we’re no longer willing to give. Our health. Our integrity. Our relationships. Perhaps even our sense of self. And the question becomes whether we can keep going without betraying ourselves further.

This is the dark side of success that is rarely discussed—the problem with getting everything we ever wanted. Not because entrepreneurs don’t experience it, but because admitting it feels dangerously close to admitting failure.

The answer isn’t another achievement

When my life began falling apart, my instinct was familiar: start another business. I’ve done just that with a property credit business in the U.K., which restored direction, confidence, and financial purpose.

What’s different this time around, however, is that I’ve come to understand that rebuilding a business and rebuilding a life are two different things. One can be measured in revenues, assets and returns. The other requires questions that don’t fit neatly into a spreadsheet: Who am I without the title? What do I actually want? Which relationships have I neglected? What am I still trying to prove, and to whom?

These are uncomfortable questions for people accustomed to having answers, which is how many entrepreneurs operate. Yet avoiding them simply postpones the reckoning.

It’s been hard to face this, especially when you consider that we live in a culture obsessed with optimization. We’re encouraged to extend our peak, maximize performance, preserve our youth, and constantly reinvent ourselves. While there’s nothing inherently wrong with that, sometimes this optimization becomes a sophisticated way of avoiding harder questions about identity, relationships, integrity and purpose.

A changed relationship with success

I want to share my story because I suspect there are thousands of successful founders wrestling with the question of who they are after the exit. From the outside, they appear to have everything. Inside, they’re wondering what happened.

Today, in my sixties, I’m still an entrepreneur, still ambitious, and still interested in creating things. But the fundamental difference this time around is that my relationship with success has changed. I’m beginning to understand that my next chapter isn’t necessarily about becoming the version of the person I used to be—it’s about letting go of the person I no longer need to be.

Contrary to what all the books and courses and stories tell you, the hardest part of success isn’t reaching the top. It’s actually when you discover that the person you became to get there is no longer the person you want to be.

So if you’re an entrepreneur in a similar position, I invite you to consider the idea that your identity is more than your business achievements, and to then find the courage to build a life that doesn’t require you to keep climbing.
