Who is Vitalik Buterin, and why is Ethereum’s co-founder now questioning the security of the very devices crypto investors use to protect their wealth? The 32-year-old programmer has turned his attention to a problem that even open-source software cannot necessarily solve: compromised computer chips.

In an essay published on November 26, Buterin argued that cryptocurrency security needs to extend beyond software and into the physical hardware on which it runs. His message builds on one of crypto’s oldest principles, but with a new twist:

For someone who helped build an alternative to centralized financial infrastructure, questioning the manufacturers behind that infrastructure seems like a natural next step.

Vitalik Buterin is a Russian-Canadian programmer, researcher and writer who conceived Ethereum in 2013, at the age of 19.

Born in Russia in 1994 and raised in Canada, Buterin discovered Bitcoin in 2011. He went on to co-found Bitcoin Magazine, where he explored the technology and economics behind cryptocurrencies long before digital assets attracted mainstream financial institutions.

His involvement in Bitcoin exposed him to a problem that would eventually lead to Ethereum. Blockchain technology had potential beyond transferring money, but building more sophisticated applications on Bitcoin presented technical limitations.

Rather than trying to accommodate every possible application within Bitcoin’s existing architecture, Buterin began working on a blockchain designed specifically for programmability.

At the time, he was studying computer science at the University of Waterloo, where he also worked as a research assistant to cryptographer Ian Goldberg.

According to the University of Waterloo, Buterin received a $100,000 Thiel Fellowship in 2014 and subsequently left university to work on Ethereum full-time.

In November 2013, Buterin wrote the initial draft of what would become the Ethereum whitepaper.

The proposal described a blockchain with a general-purpose programming environment. Developers would no longer need to create an entirely new network every time they wanted to build a decentralized application.

Consider a lending platform. Instead of relying on a bank to manage collateral, calculate interest and authorize withdrawals, developers could encode those rules into smart contracts running on Ethereum.

That does not eliminate every risk. Smart contracts can contain bugs, depend on unreliable external data or operate under centralized administrative controls. What Ethereum introduced was the ability to execute financial and other programmable agreements on a shared blockchain.

Although Buterin originated the concept, Ethereum was not built by him alone. Its founding team included seven other individuals: Gavin Wood, Joseph Lubin, Charles Hoskinson, Anthony Di Iorio, Mihai Alisie, Jeffrey Wilcke and Amir Chetrit.

The project’s first live network, known as Frontier, launched on July 30, 2015.

What began as an experimental computing platform eventually became the foundation for much of the decentralized finance industry. Stablecoins, decentralized exchanges, lending protocols and tokenized financial assets now use the infrastructure that Buterin and Ethereum’s early contributors helped establish.

The network’s own evolution, particularly its transition to proof-of-stake and the development of Layer 2 scaling networks, has shaped Ethereum’s first decade.

A hardware wallet is supposed to keep private keys away from potentially compromised computers and smartphones. But what happens if the device itself cannot be trusted?

Buterin’s latest research examines that possibility. He points to supply chain attacks involving tampered wallets and considers the potential for malicious modifications concealed inside apparently legitimate chips.

Open-source firmware allows researchers to examine the instructions a device runs. But those instructions reveal little about whether the manufactured chip has been altered.

Buterin wants manufacturers to disclose physical chip layouts, known as GDS files, so investigators have a reference against which to inspect actual hardware.

The distinction matters. Knowing what a chip is designed to do is not necessarily the same as knowing what the chip inside your wallet actually does.

One approach highlighted by Buterin comes from hardware security researcher Andrew Huang, better known as bunnie.

Huang has been developing a technique called Infra-Red, In Situ inspection (IRIS), which exploits the fact that silicon becomes transparent at certain infrared wavelengths.

Using infrared illumination and suitable imaging equipment, researchers can examine parts of a chip’s internal structure without necessarily removing it from its circuit board.

The technique could help detect substituted components or unexpected differences between manufactured chips and their intended designs.

In a 2024 update on his research, Huang explained that infrared imaging has resolution limits. It cannot reliably reveal every microscopic feature of modern chips. Combining imaging with additional electrical testing could improve confidence in a component’s integrity.

That is an important limitation for crypto investors. Publishing an open hardware design does not automatically make a wallet secure, just as publishing a smart contract’s code does not establish that it is free of vulnerabilities.

Buterin acknowledges that sophisticated hardware inspections are more likely to be performed by specialist organizations than ordinary wallet owners.

His essay is a proposal for improving security, not an announcement that existing hardware wallets have all been compromised.

Despite his status as Ethereum’s most recognizable co-founder, Buterin is not its CEO.

Ethereum is maintained through the participation of independent developers, researchers, node operators, validators and other ecosystem contributors. Changes to the network involve technical proposals, discussion, software development and adoption by participants.

Buterin has considerable influence over those discussions, but publishing a proposal does not give him the authority to implement it unilaterally.

His role within the Ethereum Foundation is also distinct from control over the blockchain itself. In its April 2025 governance announcement, the Foundation described Buterin’s responsibilities as providing technical and intellectual guidance to the wider ecosystem.

His research can influence the direction Ethereum takes. Whether the network ultimately follows that direction depends on considerably more than his endorsement.

Buterin accumulated considerable wealth through his involvement in Ethereum, with much of his publicly visible cryptocurrency wealth tied to ETH.

However, his exact net worth is not publicly established.

Blockchain analytics can estimate the value of assets held in wallets attributed to him, but those figures do not necessarily account for all his investments, liabilities, private assets or holdings at addresses that have not been publicly identified.

They also fluctuate with cryptocurrency prices. A wallet valuation should therefore not be confused with a complete assessment of his personal wealth.

Buterin’s latest essay is part of a longer-running interest in making digital infrastructure independently verifiable.

In September 2025, he published The Importance of Full-Stack Openness and Verifiability, exploring how transparency could extend across the hardware and software underpinning increasingly important parts of people’s lives.

These interests share a practical concern: whether users can independently establish that the systems they rely on behave as intended.

There is a commercial obstacle, though. Semiconductor manufacturers invest heavily in proprietary processes, and designing chips specifically for inspectability can involve performance compromises.

But a device signing a cryptocurrency transaction does not necessarily need the computing power of a modern AI processor. That makes security-focused hardware a potentially useful starting point.

More than a decade ago, Buterin challenged the idea that blockchains should primarily be used to transfer money. His latest work challenges another assumption the industry has grown comfortable with: that trusting the code is enough.

For crypto investors, the next meaningful development may come from hardware manufacturers willing to make their products independently inspectable, rather than another software update promising greater security.
