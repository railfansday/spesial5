A new report says free childcare in NYC will pay for itself

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Since taking office, New York City mayor Zohran Mamdani has made it his mission to expand access to affordable childcare in the city, and for good reason. On average, NYC families spend $21,000 on childcare annually per child—the equivalent of about $10 billion every year. While about 40% of children in the city receive some free or subsidized care, many families continue to pay for childcare entirely out of pocket, with 60% of children receiving no subsidies.  

A new report reveals how universal childcare would not only benefit families in New York City, but also boost the economy: Just 35 hours of free childcare per week could bring up to $6.5 billion in economic value each year, which would likely exceed the cost of providing that care. 

The report—which was produced by a think tank, the 5BORO Institute at Citizens Union, in partnership with Boston Consulting Group—surveyed over 2,700 parents of children under five, along with about a hundred families who moved away from New York City recently. 

According to the report, nearly 70% of children under the age of six are cared for by parents or adults who work. When those parents are unable to find care and forced to drop out of the workforce or switch jobs, the city loses $1.7 billion in annual income; among the parents surveyed, 15% altered their work situation due to childcare issues. For hourly workers, childcare gaps can force them to miss out on $360 million in earnings each year. The report also found that childcare was a leading concern for families who had left the city, a growing trend that is supported by plenty of other data. 

In other words, employers also feel the effects of inadequate access to care. That can mean high turnover or simply lost productivity if their workers regularly contend with disruptions to their childcare: More than half of the parents surveyed said they had missed at least one full day of work in the past three months; on average during a three month period, they missed out on three to four full days of work. 

These childcare gaps can cost New York City employers about 54 million hours of missed work, according to the report. The act of providing 35 hours of free childcare each week would also enable nearly 30,000 parents to reenter the workforce, which could yield over a billion dollars in annual earnings.

Childcare has been a key focus for Mamdani since his campaign, and as Fast Company reported in a recent cover story, the mayor has even gotten the business community on board with its investments in this area. Mamdani is already building upon the universal 3-K program established by former mayor Bill de Blasio to extend care to younger children; the recently launched 2-K program is now providing free, full-day childcare to 2,000 two-year-olds, with plans to serve an additional 10,000 children by next fall. (The city has provided free pre-K to all four-year-olds since 2014.) 

Even business leaders who are critical of Mamdani have seemingly come around to the idea of free childcare, though they may not agree on how to achieve that. This fall, the 5BORO Institute created the NYC Child Care Business Council, bringing together a number of business leaders and organizations committed to putting their muscle behind this issue and advocating for childcare investment (something the business community has not historically done).

For years, employers have invested in generous parental leave policies and backup childcare benefits for their workforce, to help them court top talent and retain some of their best employees. But it seems companies are finally realizing that their best efforts can’t make up for a lack of robust childcare options or offset the mounting cost of care.

“The market is not able to solve this problem,” Grace Rauh, the executive director of Citizens Union, recently told Fast Company. “Frankly, private businesses aren’t able to provide the kind of fully robust childcare benefits that their employees need.”
