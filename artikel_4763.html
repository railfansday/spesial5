On March 24, 2015, a Germanwings pilot with severe mental issues purposefully crashed a passenger plane with 150 people on board in the French Alps, resulting in the tragic deaths of all passengers. This horrible incident highlighted how important it is that people who work in high-risk fields and have a responsibility for the survival of others are mentally stable.

Moreover, in some jobs, psychological evaluations may be conducted with people who are already employed if they show signs of impaired mental health or thinking abilities. This includes, for example, physicians and other healthcare workers.

Should people in certain high-risk AI development roles undergo psychological evaluations?

One group of people that does not routinely undergo mental health screenings is programmers involved in the creation of AI. Over the course of the last few weeks, there has been a lot of discussion in the media about the extent to which rogue AI agents may potentially cause harm to humans in the future or even kill all of humanity. While experts debate about whether AI agents turning on humanity is a real risk or not, one potentially more likely risk of AI often gets ignored.

Just like a pilot could cause a plane to crash on purpose, an AI developer could purposefully build AI models that lack the necessary checks and balances to prevent them from hurting humans, or even an AI that exists with the purpose to harm humans, e.g., by performing hacking attacks on critical infrastructure. Thus, AI does not need to turn rogue by itself to harm humans; one developer doing so on purpose could lead to massive economic and personal harm. Since a scenario like this seems more likely to occur in the near future than AI turning rogue by itself, measures to prevent such an incident are needed. Because there are mental health conditions like antisocial personality disorder, which has been linked to violating the rights of other people without feelings of guilt, or dark personality traits such as psychopathy, such a scenario is not entirely unlikely.

I believe occupationally mandated psychological evaluations of AI developers should be considered.

Given that the big AI companies themselves warn about the potential risks of AI, more psychological research is needed into whether severe, untreated mental health conditions in AI developers could lead to the risk of real harm for other human beings. While no one knows how likely catastrophic events akin to the Germanwings airplane catastrophe would be in the field of AI development and what impact they would have, occupationally mandated psychological evaluations of AI developers could help ensure that only mentally stable people are involved in the development of frontier models with the potential to cause real harm to humans. Thus, professional organizations for psychologists, companies that make hiring and employment decisions in relation to the development of AI tools, and government agencies involved in the regulation of tech companies should consider occupationally mandated psychological evaluations of AI developers.
