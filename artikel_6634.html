Bobbi Althoff on Tradwives, Reply Guys, and Sharing a Bed With Drake

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Bobbi Althoff has a baby face and a vocal fry. Her tenor––which you’ve likely encountered in your TikTok feed, whether or not you know her name––is vaguely baritone and sharply deadpan, an oddly melodic gravel on the eardrum. Depending on who you ask, it’s either endearing, or lazy, or simply slutty. Despite rising to fame on a skittish gaze, the 29-year-old MomTokker turned podcaster turned non-Mormon cast member of Secret Lives of Mormon Wives is now holding eye contact.

We’ve known each other for 20 minutes. She puts down her fork and palms her phone. She wants to read me something, she says, her gemstone-encrusted ring (reading “MOM”) catching the sunlight streaming into The Dominick hotel, where Althoff is staying during the unseasonably warm week of the Mormon Wives premiere.

It’s an essay entitled “I Hate Myself,” written by Althoff when she was a teenager mired in a bout of depression and anxiety made worse by bullies at school. I’d asked what she would title her memoir, if she were to write one, and how she handles online hate, from being accused of sleeping with Drake after interviewing him for her podcast to rumors that her marriage ended due to infidelity to scrutiny about her appearance. “Can a girl gain some weight in her face without you guys fucking saying she has cheek filler?” she wrote on Instagram. “Can a girl be insecure about her upper lip and get some fucking lip filler without you guys fucking commenting on it?” Althoff has been at the center of myriad social media firestorms, some motored by straightforward, internet-enabled misogyny and others by exasperation with her indirect, vaguely hostile approach to engaging with her podcast guests.

She takes a breath, and begins reading. “I want so badly to be able to show everyone the real me, the person that is hurt and is scared to trust anyone, the little girl that is funny, loud, and doesn't care what other people think. Instead of being myself, I create a different personality to satisfy everyone's needs. I hide the real me behind fake smiles and high-pitched laughs.” She pauses, then chuckles (at a normal pitch).

“I was 14 when I wrote that,” she continues. “And that was before ChatGPT. I wrote that myself.”

A smattering of the many awkward—or awkwardly flirtatious—and reliably fury-baiting encounters that have gone down on Althoff’s The Really Good Podcast since its inception in 2023: She and Jason DeRulo LARP a date that gets interrupted by an attempted larceny. While next to him in bed, she informs Drake that Aubrey is a girl’s name. She accuses Michael Cera of founding Cerave, then argues with him about whether his wife needs to know that their feet touched. Lil Yachty threatens to teach her to dance. An oral mishap cum Freudian slip—did she say magician or musician?—in a conversation with Sukihana appears to spark a feud, but instead jumpstarts a friendship and another podcast, this year’s surprisingly raw and affectionate That’s BS with Bobbi and Suki.

Despite her reputation as an invulnerable, even defensive interlocutor, Althoff is strikingly invested in self-exposure. She has spoken openly about her severe struggles with mental illness, insecurities around beauty, her plans to get breast implants, and her painful experience with botched Botox injections. Her penchant for oversharing, she told me, startles her interview subjects, inspiring their own surprising and frank disclosures in return.

“I’m like, hi, nice to meet you, I tried to kill myself,” she tells me. From a young age, she has used comedy to cope with the indignities of womanhood, and she comes by her wry, bluntly charming humor honestly. “Mom, by the time you read this, I’m going to be chilling with Satan,” she remembers writing in a note she left for her mother as a teenager when, she tells me, she attempted suicide. “Keep in mind, I literally was so religious that I accepted that I was going to go to hell for killing myself.” Althoff grew up in a hyper-religious Christian household in California, where she was taught to believe in a classic set piece of Hell that involved “being burned alive forever.”

Althoff has since left the Church, and with it the “conservative worldview” that characterized her adolescence and early adulthood, leading her to get married at the age of 22. Now, she says, she is effortfully de-centering men after “being told that my purpose in life was to be a wife.” Which brings us to her latest venture: divorced, un-religious, founding cast member on a reality show that purports to follow a cabal of Mormon wives living in Orange County. Althoff says her friendships with fellow cast members have been formative and healing, her first experiences with genuine connection among a group of girls after a childhood defined by bullying and a young adulthood spent slaloming down social media slopes riddled with misogynistic vitriol that came from women just as often as it did men.

With her star turn as one of two non-Mormon, unmarried cast members in Mormon Wives, Althoff says she is she surrendering the control over her image that comes with having her own editing team for her podcast, which entails being “completely myself” to a “terrifying” degree. Where the original iteration of Mormon Wives, set in Salt Lake City, centered a pre-existing friend group, the OC spinoff opens on a cast of highly competitive loose acquaintances largely connected by a rabid desire to build their individual audiences. Entering the arena as the most random cast member makes watching Althoff build actual friendships in real-time bizarrely affecting. Instead of sitting safely in the interviewer’s chair, coquettishly crossing her legs and batting her eyelashes, this time those eyelashes are drenched in tears, and she’s not hiding the streaks of mascara dripping down her cheeks.

Those cheeks were once pumped full of Botox, which caused serious facial paralysis (a relatively rare side effect). But enduring a period in which she “couldn’t smile” inspired a shift in her approach to beauty standards and an appreciation for human foibles over cosmetic perfection. “I have been trying to find beauty in the fact that I’m not perfect,” she tells me. “Do I not like my cellulite? Yes, but, I can show it and it’s fine because I’m human. My boobs are saggy: I literally breastfed both of my kids.” Doing reality television also led to an epiphany: she’s been too hard on herself about her appearance. This occurred to her watching the show back with her new castmates-turned-friends, women who she finds beautiful. “I actually don’t need all this stuff,” she says. “I don’t need to have a perfectly chiseled nose and perfect boobs.”

Some might wonder whether joining the Mormon Wives cast, comprised largely of buxom, blonde, traditionally attractive women who in previous iterations of the show have attended group Botox appointments as a form of socializing, might be a counterintuitive way of deprogramming conservative aesthetic norms. But Mormon Wives runs on the tension inherent in its premise: these are women famous for being religious tradwives, who repeatedly slip and fall into subverting tradition: they are breadwinners, they are in bisexual throuples, they talk about sex on camera (even if they insist they only engage in it in the marriage bed). Perhaps most poignantly, they seem to find more emotional sustenance in their relationships with each other than any of their largely deadbeat, cheating, whining, soda-chugging boyfriends and husbands.

In the show’s premiere, she corrects an offscreen producer who calls her an influencer––she’s an internet personality. Online, she is disembodied, cut into clips, context-free and content-forward. And like many of today’s hyper-contemporary internet personalities, Althoff is paradoxically very famous and utterly unknown. As in: she has almost four million followers on Instagram, and every straight man I mentioned this profile to immediately had multiple, highly specific inquiries about interactions she’s had with male celebrities. (“What was it like to share a bed with the 6 God?”) Meanwhile the majority of girls, gays and professional podcasters I brought Althoff up to started at me blankly, evincing not even a dreg of familiarity with her work.

Her legions of followers cannot, in large part, be called “fans,” but do include avid viewers, reply guys, bewildered women, judgmental tradwives, hate-watchers, and pop culture fanatics. She collects connoisseurs more than disciples. As such, Althoff is a cipher, and a harbinger of a novel vector of celebrity that’s potentially liberating for women who’ve been trained to seek validation, adoration, and eternal “relevance,” as she puts it. This makes her an intriguingly modern economic actor. When she is recognized in public, she doesn’t tell her children that they just met a few of Mommy’s fans. “I say, these are all Mommy’s coworkers,” she tells me, seeming sincere as she emphasizes that she wouldn’t want her children to think she was traditionally “famous.” But she clearly can’t resist a facetious acknowledgment of how successful her campaign for relevance has been: “They think I have coworkers everywhere.”

She “judges relevance by incoming brand deals,” which means she’s willing to maneuver to maintain your attention. “My audience has a lot of men who are just wishing they could see me naked,” she says, a reality she enthusiastically “manipulates a little bit.” She knows her combative approach to flirtation sparks outrage among her male viewers. She’s also aware that many of those men resort to infantile insults, which is where her deadpan self-deprecation comes in: “I want to beat people to the punch,” she claims. “You can't call me ugly and have it hurt me because I called myself ugly first. You can't say I'm not funny: I've already said it.”

When I relay to Althoff some of the questions my straight male friends had for her, she admits the rabid speculation around her sex life is frustrating but also flattering. “They obviously think I had a chance with these guests,” she deadpans. “There’s no interaction I can have with a man where people aren’t like, Oh, you guys fucked.” Her haters, she’s observed, fall into two camps: Trump voting white men “who think they would never have a chance with me” and believe unmarried mothers to be morally corrupt. On the flip side are “women who are jealous.”

Her political leanings––which have veered far to the left since her childhood––periodically foment some turmoil among her new girl squad, but Althoff says she also finds much to admire in her conservative cast members lifestyles. “Do I agree with everything in the Church? No. But I see the beauty in the tradwife life.” The relationship between McCall DaPron, a fellow cast member and frenemy turned pseudo sister of Althoff’s, inspired her to break up with her ex, because she saw a healthy dynamic missing from her own relationship. She is committed to maintaining her conviction around political issues her cast members might rather not address: “Does it bother me when I’m the only one in the cast posting about certain things happening in the world? Yes. Fuck ICE, Free Palestine. Free Congo. Free Sudan.”

She hopes her onscreen proto-sister wives, and viewers like them, might allow her to “burst that bubble a little bit”—to widen their perspectives, open their eyes, and inspire re-evaluations of issues close to Althoff’s heart. Admittedly, as the show illuminates, sometimes that means earnestly defending a political position, and other times it means (antifascistly?) refusing to follow the dress code one of the other women attempts to enforce at a party. Observing that male guests on her podcast “always rank themselves a ten out of ten, she notes that “men could be a little more realistic.” She’s not attacking all men, she clarifies, but she would like to alert them to the following: “I don't need to fuck every man that hits on me.”

She adds, with the sprightly sarcasm that’s become her trademark, one final message to readers of this magazine: “Men who read GQ: be nicer to me. I’m not mean, I don’t hate you, don’t hate me.”
