Mary Miss’s Long Fight to Keep Public Art Alive

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

In the early decades of postwar America, public art tended to be seen as the pallid stepchild of architecture and design. Steel, bronze, or stone abstractions sited in front of modernist buildings were denigrated as “plop art,” or even more damningly as “the turd in the plaza.” And, accordingly, public artists were relegated to the province of decoration or historical commemoration while public responses to their art ranged from indifference to outrage over the expenditure of public money.

Then, in the 1980s, public art was jolted by a revolution. Rebelling against the tradition of the static monument, a group of young artists aspired instead to explore how art in public spaces could encourage connection, reflection, and interaction among viewers who didn’t necessarily have an art education. They created mazes, placed mirrors in the landscape, and drew lines in the snow. An extension of the boundary-breaking experiments of avant-garde artists of the 1970s, this new public art slowly began to gain acceptance from funders, developers, government officials, and general audiences. Among the most adventurous of these pioneers was Mary Miss. Raised in the American West, she was interested in the tensions between rural and urban landscapes, the built and unbuilt environment, and the ways in which people navigate their path through the spaces of the modern world. By 1988, she had created an impressive roster of temporary and permanent projects that challenged the conventional definitions of public art. That year saw the creation of one of her most celebrated works, a waterside park titled South Cove (1984–87) in the newly created Battery Park City development at the bottommost tip of Manhattan. Created on a former landfill site, this work incorporates natural plantings, rocks, walkways, trellises, wooden pilings, a circular jetty, and an overlook that cantilevers over the edge of the Hudson River. Brilliantly weaving together architecture, landscape, and references to Manhattan’s maritime history, it remains a much-loved intervention in the urban landscape.

The year she made South Cove, Miss sat down with curator Patterson Sims at the 92nd Street Y for a wide-ranging discussion of her work and her views on public art. When he prodded her to speculate on the future of this new way of creating art for the public, Miss replied, “I think we’re just beginning to see what the possibilities are.” Then, reflecting on the support provided for such projects by the National Endowment for the Arts, she added, “I think they’ve started something that can’t stop.”

The irony of this faith in the NEA is not lost on Miss today. This spring, sitting in the Tribeca studio she has occupied since 1970, she recalled the enthusiasm of her younger self with what seemed like envy. “I was still feeling so optimistic,” she says. “I hadn’t been beaten down yet.”

Now a seasoned practitioner, her dedication to public art and dialogue remains intact, but has been tempered by the realities of politics, economics, and societal change. She has endured bureaucratic meddling; watched lofty rhetoric butt up against budgets, architects, and physical and technical limitations; experienced the shrinking and politicization of public money; and seen one of her signature pieces dismantled. In the course of her long career, she’s worked on numerous proposals that haven’t come to fruition, and at times put up with nonpayment and the theft of her ideas.

Yet, while these conditions might have deterred a less determined artist, Miss has responded by adapting: She shifts gears, rethinks her basic premises, and finds a new way. Her current project, “City as Living Laboratory” (CaLL), takes the idea of public art away from its dependence on gatekeepers and experts and returns it to the communities in which it’s created—a testament to her resilience and her remarkable capacity for reinvention.

IT TAKES A SPECIAL kind of person to operate successfully—and continually—as a public artist. Miss was born in New York City in 1944, but spent a peripatetic childhood moving around the country, with extended stays in Colorado and Florida as the family followed her father, who was in the military. She recalls a formative childhood memory of sitting in the back of an old Chevy and watching the scenery spin by. Fences laid across the fields whizzed by in a fine filigree and then gradually disappeared as the urban landscape took over. Such experiences sparked an enduring interest in vernacular forms and quotidian materials. They also provided a metaphor she has frequently invoked of public art as a wide net that stretches across barriers and connects people.

After getting a BA from the University of California, Santa Barbara, Miss came East and enrolled in graduate school at the Maryland Institute College of Art in Baltimore. However, she did not find the faculty very receptive to her interests, so she spent a year out of residence in Colorado. There, she did outdoor projects that contained the germ of her subsequent public work before returning East to New York City in 1968. It was an exciting time to be an artist: While the city itself was struggling economically, cheap rents made it a paradise for the era’s avant-garde. Miss fell in with a group of young women, among them Hannah Wilke, Elizabeth Murray, Nancy Holt, and Alice Aycock, who were challenging the orthodoxies of a male-dominated art world that was still fanning the flames of formalism. Miss recalls, “We figured, OK, you guys aren’t going to play with us. We’ll figure out another way.”

Miss’s early works, extending ideas of the ordinary yet defamiliarized spaces she had begun to explore in Colorado, were constructions using common materials that challenged viewers’ perceptions and sense of space. Some were indoor sculptures comprising bits of rope, ladders, stairs, or barbed wire. Others, using similar materials, were set outside in open fields and on hillsides. She generally eschewed urban contexts, and her modest approach was a marked contrast to the massive works of earth-movers like Michael Heizer and Robert Smithson, for whom an unpeopled landscape provided an empty canvas to be reshaped into a giant sculpture. Instead, Miss’s works were designed to alter viewers’ perspectives as they moved through and around the site, discovering unexpected vistas and pathways.

One work from this period, Perimeters/Pavilions/Decoys (1977–78), temporarily installed across four acres at the Nassau County Museum in Roslyn, New York, embodied the kind of paradoxes Miss was pursuing. Three towers and two semicircular mounds drew viewers forward, but the bulk of the work was not visible on the surface of the land. Instead, a ladder between the mounds invited viewers underground, where they encountered a series of corridors, doors, and slotlike windows.

The work caught the eye of the formidable critic Rosalind Krauss, whose now-classic 1979 essay “Sculpture in the Expanded Field” helped launch Miss into the limelight. In it, Krauss laid out a framework for understanding the movement of sculpture beyond the realm of the object and into an entanglement with architecture and landscape. She opened the essay with a description of Perimeters/Pavilions/Decoys, using it to illustrate the deconstruction of the idea of sculpture and its replacement, the idea of the field. In her typically abstruse fashion, Krauss noted, “Thus the field provides both for an expanded but finite set of related positions for a given artist to occupy and explore, and for an organization of work that is not dictated by the conditions of a particular medium.”

Miss was grateful for the attention, but remarks wryly, “I don’t know what she was interested in, and I didn’t know then what she was talking about. And she obviously didn’t really mean the ‘expanded’ field, because as soon as I started doing that, I went off her radar.” In fact, while Krauss’s essay mapped a set of formal relations, Miss was expanding the field of art to address human concerns.

The artist’s attention shifted from rural to urban settings, particularly to New York City, where she lived and worked. Her projects involved deep research into a site’s history, context, and present use, and these issues helped shape her concepts. For instance, when she was invited to work with the architects who were renovating the Union Square subway station in 1992, she delved into the archeology of the station and discovered how greatly original features like stairs, lighting fixtures, mosaic tiles, and turnstiles had fallen into disrepair or been altered or replaced over the years. She created 125 red framing elements that marked these changes, highlighting historical details that passersby might otherwise miss. At Battery Park City’s South Cove, pilings, blue ships’ lanterns hung on wooden lampposts, and native rocks and plants bring New York City’s residents back in touch with the area’s previous life as an important port. In a playful touch, the shape of the overlook platform deliberately mimics the crown of the Statue of Liberty, which is visible across the water.

In 1989, Miss embarked on a pivotal project—one that recently made headlines. She was invited by the Des Moines Art Center to create something in the nearby Greenwood Pond. Once an important community gathering place, the pond had deteriorated and become overrun with green algae. She started by researching the site, speaking with local ecologists, historians, and Meskwaki Nation elders. Miss discovered that the site had once contained a wetland, and that it, like most of Iowa’s wetlands, had been drained and tilled for agriculture. She also investigated the history and lives of the Meskwaki tribe, which had once occupied this land.

From that research, Miss decided to restore the pond while transforming it into a kind of demonstration wetland, using forms that echoed Native American lodges and earthen mounds. This work, titled Greenwood Pond: Double Site (1989–96), comprised a network of paths, canopies, and mounds, including a wooden pavilion following the curve of the shore as well as an elevated lookout and a walkway that plunged below the surface, allowing visitors to sit beneath the water level. As a newly restored wetland, it welcomed birds, insects, wildlife, and native plants in an educational display of biodiversity.

For this project, Miss collaborated with a wide range of community members and groups. She reflects that Greenwood Pond: Double Site “was kind of a fulcrum for me, because I was able to have a scientist, the Science Center, the Founders Garden Club, the Parks Department, the museum, all in conversation. It was a different way of working.”

For Jenny Dixon, director emerita of the Noguchi Museum and the inaugural director of the Public Art Fund, this “different way of working” is what makes Miss “a major figure in this field.” Dixon, who has known Miss since the 1970s, adds that “you just can’t make a piece of public art in your studio. There has to be another piece to it, whether it’s community, architecture, the space, the site, the environment. Mary’s not one-dimensional.”

With Greenwood Pond: Double Site, Miss began to shift from her earlier focus on land and history to the kinds of ecological issues that subsequently have become her primary focus. “In the wake of climate change,” she remarks, “artists need to help redefine nature. I’ve long claimed that the city is nature and we are living in it.”

But the project was also pivotal for a less happy reason: While the work was enjoyed by the public and maintained by the Art Center through various administrations, by 2024 it had fallen into disrepair. Kelly Baum, the Art Center’s director, informed Miss that the work would need to be dismantled because the Art Center did not have the $8 million necessary for restoration and future maintenance. A public uproar and lawsuit ensued as Miss attempted to force the museum to raise the funds to save the work. In the end, a settlement was reached: The work was taken down, and Miss was awarded $900,000, a portion of which she earmarked for a newly established fund to support threatened site-specific art works.

AT LEAST GREENWOODPOND: DOUBLE SITE was realized and lasted for 29 years. For Miss, the 1990s were marked by increasing frustration as many projects that had consumed considerable time and effort never came to fruition. These included projects for New York City; Los Angeles; St. Paul; St. Louis; and Long Beach, California. Many other projects that did see the light of day were then altered or diminished by the architects and planners with whom she was working.

Then came September 11, 2001. Miss’s studio was in the shadow of the Twin Towers. She recalls that her place “was covered with dust because the windows had been open. We had to come in and clean everything.” Deeply aware of the need for collective mourning, she soon came up with the idea of creating a temporary project to establish places where people could gather and lay flowers. Working with architects Elliott Maltby and Victoria Marshall, Miss named the work Moving Perimeter: A Wreath for Ground Zero (2002). She was struck by the motley nature of the temporary barriers and fences—composed of wood, chain link, concrete, and pipe—that kept out people making a pilgrimage to the site. Her idea was to paint some of these blue and replace others with new sections of fencing, seating, planting, and blue lights in order to create a movable perimeter that would grow denser and smaller as the exclusion zone decreased. It would operate as a place for people to gather and connect and share the profound sense of loss that was enveloping the city.

After years of struggling with bureaucracies, distant deadlines, and ultimately rejections, this project was an eye-opener for Miss. Although it was never realized, it still allowed her to rethink what public art could be: If it was temporary, it could be executed quickly and flexibly. And it would help if it relied on willing participants rather than reluctant bureaucrats to give the work meaning. “Wait a second,” Miss says she realized at one point. “There’s a way I can function instead of just waiting to get something built. You just need to take charge.”

While she was still working on some large-scale permanent projects during this period, she began to think in terms of interactive, collaborative, open-ended works that left a light footprint. This culminated in 2010 with the inauguration of CaLL, based on a concept that went all the way back to her idea of art as a net stretched across communities or even whole cities. She describes CaLL as “the idea that artists, working in collaboration with scientists, engineers, planners, and communities, can have an essential role to play in addressing the pressing environmental issues of our times.”

Over the past 15 years, CaLL has evolved into a series of different practices, including workshops, urban walks, programs, and community-initiated projects. The most ambitious of these is an initiative with the city of Milwaukee called WaterMarks (2015–). Working with ecologist Eric Sanderson, she has focused on the city’s complex relationship with water. A source of life, water also poses dangers from flooding, contamination, and aging infrastructures. WaterMarks involves going into neighborhoods to explore their specific relationship to water, engaging local residents, artists, and public and private institutions along the way. The project coalesces around a set of markers, 25-foot poles wrapped with solar panels and topped by illuminated blue letters chosen by the community.

These poles map out the nexus for educational and artistic projects that center on local stewardship of water. For instance, at Milwaukee’s Acosta Middle School, students chose the letter A for their marker, standing both for Acosta and, in this Latino area, agua, or water. In a series of walks with experts through the neighborhood, students learned about the area’s water history, its green infrastructure, and the need for storm water management in an urban environment. A green space created on a remediated slice of abandoned industrial wasteland became the Green Tech Station, designated with the letter G. This is a site for development, demonstration, and education about green infrastructure projects relating to the management of invasive species, pollutants, and flooding. The letter H designates the Harbor View neighborhood, where enjoyment of water includes activities like swimming, fishing, and kayaking.

So far, WaterMarks has 14 markers in place. Together they form what Miss describes as an atlas of water for the city. Rising above the network of markers is a 350-foot stack in the center of the city’s water treatment plant that has been repurposed as a beacon, with changing colors that warn residents when they need to restrict their water usage to avoid possible sewage overflows into Lake Michigan. “One really wonderful thing that’s come out of this 10-year project,” Miss says, “is that there’s a collective of people who are going to take it forward.”

WaterMarks is the most fully realized of CaLL’s undertakings, but the ideas it exemplifies appear in projects in other cities as well. In Boulder, Colorado, in 2007, Miss worked with climate scientists to map the potential disaster of the long-anticipated “500-year flood” with metal discs on trees, fences, and buildings that charted predicted water levels. In Indianapolis in 2014, Miss worked with local organizations to install along the White River more than 100 markers and mirrors that employed technologies like apps, webcam projections, and websites to provide information on the river’s history and ecology.

Building on Miss’s earlier projects in Manhattan, CaLL has also undertaken several initiatives in New York City. Broadway: 1000 Steps, initiated in 2009, is an ongoing reenvisioning of the city’s longest street as a “green corridor” equipped with hubs that provide text, images, and technology designed to engage the public in dialogues about environmental issues. In 2016, CaLL started Rescuing Tibbetts Brook in the Bronx. This project focuses on a plan to unearth a long-buried stream that now feeds into the sewer system and diverts it to a corridor adjacent to the Major Deegan Expressway, where it can become a new linear park.

Miss is currently working in Corona, Queens, on Cloudburst. This project charts the path taken by water during recent devastating flooding events in this low-lying immigrant neighborhood and helps residents understand the dangers as well as the mitigation efforts being undertaken by the city’s Department of Environmental Protection (DEP). CaLL has organized an advisory committee comprised of local residents and institutions, which will work with the DEP to pinpoint problems and needs. Central to Cloudburst are walks, either led by experts or self-guided, that explore the area’s ecology, specifically its water problems and possible solutions. In a move reminiscent of the markers set up in Milwaukee, beneath trees along the path, CaLL has placed benches with signs that invite passersby to connect to informative recordings via the internet.

But it’s the templates these projects create that are the most meaningful—at least for Olivia Georgia, who has been executive director of CaLL since early in its inception, in 2010. “It’s a model that can be refreshed, enhanced, and amplified by other artists,” she says. “We can’t imagine what the other artists’ projects will be. We set the table for them, but they have to figure out how to use the instruments in the tool kit we have assembled.”

Miss came up as part of a generation benefiting from federal support for the arts; but since that time, the tides have very much turned. She recently had grants canceled by both the National Science Foundation and the National Endowment for the Arts. “They’re saying, ‘No science. No climate change,’” she explains, adding that this backlash makes it all the more important to find self-sustaining ways to help people connect to environmental issues. “I’ve had to really change my practice. I loved doing the Des Moines project. I love doing these projects that cost a lot of money. But now I’m trying to find a different way…. I’ve gone from this young woman who wanted to be acknowledged as an artist and who wanted to be able to make a mark with the work that I did, and I’ve ended up wanting this CaLL work to continue. That’s what I would really love to be able to leave behind.

“I really got beaten down in the ’90s,” she reflects, “and yet I ended up in this place. I did come out from under it. I’m glad I reached my optimism again.”
