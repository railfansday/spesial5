Scientists Develop New Nanomaterial That Could Help the Body Regrow Missing Bone

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

A biodegradable material developed by Australian researchers could eventually help children born with cleft lip and palate regrow missing jawbone without undergoing invasive bone graft surgery. In preclinical testing, the experimental nanomaterial produced approximately 80 percent more new bone than a control material after eight weeks.

Developed at the University of Sydney in collaboration with the University of Queensland, the material works by activating the body’s existing repair signals rather than supplying external growth factors. Researchers hope the approach could eventually allow children to receive treatment years earlier than is typically possible today.

Cleft lip and palate affects approximately one in 700 children and develops when parts of the upper lip or roof of the mouth fail to fuse completely during pregnancy. Correcting the resulting jawbone defects is among the most challenging aspects of treatment, with potential consequences for eating, breathing, speech, and social development.

“One of the biggest challenges for children born with cleft lip and palate is repairing the bone defect in the jaw,” said lead researcher Associate Professor Chun Xu, a Sydney Horizon Fellow in the University of Sydney’s Faculty of Medicine and Health.

“While some children with minor defects are treated as infants, many patients need to wait until they are around 10 to 12 years old before surgeons can take bone from another part of the body and graft it into the defect.

“That means many kids live with difficulties for years. It can affect their breathing, eating and speech. Most heartbreaking of all, it can have a huge impact on their confidence and social development.”

More than four million bone repair procedures are performed worldwide annually. Many current treatments rely on bone harvested from the patient or animal-derived materials that primarily provide structural support. The new approach instead aims to stimulate the formation of living bone using a biodegradable calcium-aluminosilicate nanomaterial.

The material activates a naturally occurring growth factor called latent Transforming Growth Factor β1 (TGF-β1). This protein normally exists in an inactive form until biological signals trigger its activity. Once activated, it helps recruit stem cells to the damaged area and encourages their development into bone-producing cells. As new tissue forms, the biodegradable material is gradually replaced by the patient’s own bone.

“The material activates dormant repair signals in the body, triggering a cascade of healing processes that attract bone-forming stem cells and stimulate new bone growth,” Xu explained.

“Our body already contains many of the signals needed for tissue repair,” Associate Professor Xu said.

“We’ve developed a material that can help activate those signals at the right place and time. Instead of supplying external growth factors, we’re encouraging the body to use its own healing potential.”

The study, published in ACS Nano, found that the material activated the body’s latent bone repair growth factor at approximately 10 times the level achieved using conventional methods. It also promoted blood clotting within about 30 seconds, which could help stabilize an injury during the earliest stages of healing.

According to the researchers, this is the first demonstration of a single nanomaterial platform capable of combining rapid blood clotting, activation of naturally occurring growth factors, recruitment of bone-forming stem cells, and enhanced bone regeneration. The findings come from a preclinical bone model, however, and further testing is necessary to determine whether the material can be used safely and effectively in humans.

Xu and his colleagues envision a future in which bone defects can be repaired earlier in childhood, potentially reducing the need to remove healthy bone from elsewhere in the body.

“Our long-term goal is to develop materials that help the body regenerate bone naturally and reduce the need for these invasive and painful procedures. We hope this could allow treatment much earlier than is possible today.”

The researchers are also investigating whether the nanomaterial could be incorporated into personalized 3D-printed scaffolds designed to fit an individual’s bone defect. Beyond cleft lip and palate, potential applications include repairing bone damaged by traumatic injuries, tooth loss, and other conditions involving difficult-to-heal defects.

“Every patient is different, and every bone defect is different,” Xu said.

“In the future, we hope to combine these materials with advanced 3D-printing technologies so treatments can be tailored to the specific needs of each patient.”

The technology remains at the preclinical stage, with additional research required before human trials can begin.

Reference: “Multifunctional Mesoporous Calcium-Aluminosilicate Nanoparticles Enable Heterogeneous Activation of Endogenous Transforming Growth Factor β1 and Rapid Hemostasis for Bone Repair” by Chun Xu, Qingxuan Chen, Hang Chen, Aoni Xu, Juan Wen, Yiwei Li, Huan Dai, Siyuan Liu, Dan Cheng, Yaping Song, Bing Sun, Jie Tang, Jie Fan, Chang Lei and Chengzhong Yu, 15 September 2026, ACS Nano. DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.6c05126
