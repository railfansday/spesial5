CTV Has Trust Issues

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Around four months ago, a top ad sales executive at a CTV ad platform had a meeting with a large QSR advertiser, and he offered the CMO a guarantee.

“We said, ‘You only pay us based on incremental visits to your store as defined and measured by your independent third-party measurement provider,’” the exec shared during a session at LUMA’s Digital Media Summit earlier this week, in what felt a little like group therapy for CTV ad tech platforms.

Not so much. The QSR advertiser wasn’t convinced. “Well, how do I know I can trust them?” he apparently asked.

“They’re your measurement partner!” the CTV ad exec recalled thinking, still incredulous in his retelling.

This tale highlights a frustration that plenty of CTV sellers share. They’ve spent years positioning connected TV as a vehicle for driving outcomes, not just reach and frequency – but getting advertisers to believe it is another matter.

“We’re not there yet,” the CTV exec said. “The promise is there, we’re pitching it, we have it, but we need advertisers to come on board.”

An ad industry vet – and self-described “recovering marketer” – in the audience piped up to venture a theory. It’s partially a perception thing.

Although there’s been an “inexorable pull down the funnel” toward performance, he said, marketers still tend to see TV as upper-funnel “hero media,” he said.

Advertisers also often don’t have the right creative for CTV, and making the creative is too expensive. “There are legacy barriers to taking full advantage,” the vet said, “and [CTV is] just not seen as being as performative as programmatic.”

But whatever it is that’s holding buyers back, it’s not a lack of measurement options.

The CEO and co-founder of a well-known CTV ad platform said that around 10 years ago when he started his company, people would look at him cross-eyed when he tried to talk about incrementality.

But today, there are lots of different independent third-party measurement companies that offer incrementality through a variety of models. It’s a good thing, the CEO said.

Yet, be that as it may, even the offer of a guarantee backed by independent measurement couldn’t close the deal for that aforementioned CTV ad exec. And sometimes the skepticism is warranted. There’s no denying that attribution is kind of a mess.

Every channel, from display to social, tries to take credit for the same conversion, said the co-founder and chief product officer of a streaming ad insertion platform.

To be fair, CTV is no different in that regard, although it does bring something to the table that other channels don’t.

“You’re getting a higher rate of attention,” the co-founder said. “Yes, you might be paying more for it, but you need exposure and attention in order to actually convert.”

But it’s also worth pointing out that this whole debate can sound rather academic to the people who control the budgets, an executive on the buy side reminded the room.

“Not many brands think about all the things that we talk about on a daily basis,” said the exec, who knows of what he speaks. Before joining a buy-side platform, he spent years as a marketer in media and strategy roles, including for a large candy brand.

Marketers care about things like timing, seasonality and sales, not the ad tech plumbing underneath. And that, the exec argued, is why it makes sense for the DSP, not the publisher, to have the final say on when and where ads run across CTV and everywhere else.

For example, October is like the Super Bowl for a large candy brand, but most candy sales don’t happen until the last three or four days of the month in the countdown to Halloween. When the exec would buy direct from publishers, however, most of the impressions would run in the first week of October, well before shoppers were thinking about Halloween candy, because the publishers were focused on monetizing their own inventory, not his business problems.

There would only be a trickle of impressions remaining by the end of the month.

If pressed, the exec said, “[the publishers] would be like, ‘Well, we delivered in full, what do you mean?”

“But I wanted 95% of my spend to be the last week of October,” he said, “because it would have driven a lot more revenue for my company, not somebody else’s.”
