One Bitcoin miner paid an $11.9M tariff bill four years after delivery

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Bitcoin mining companies are still resolving customs disputes over machines that entered the US years ago.

Bitcoin miner Bitfarms thought the machines it brought into Washington state in 2021 had been made in Malaysia. Its supplier said so, and the company provided US Customs officials with documents supporting that claim.

Four years later, the company paid the government $11.9 million. US Customs and Border Protection had challenged the machines' declared origin, arguing that they were made in China and subject to a 25% import duty.

Bitfarms disputed the assessment, but its financial statements show it paid in October 2025, after Customs told it the duties were required.

The payment shows a cost that can follow a mining company long after its equipment was delivered and started working. Other miners have faced similar disputes over where their machines were made, with one company recently avoiding a potential $100 million assessment and another still contesting an estimate that reached $130 million.

Those numbers don't add up to a single bill for the industry. Each dispute involves different shipments, documents, and decisions, and the outcomes can look very different even when the companies believe they have evidence supporting their declared origins.

Bitfarms' dispute began in early 2022, when Customs challenged the origin of the machines imported the previous year. The company said its Chinese vendor had represented that the equipment came from Malaysia, while Customs asserted that it was manufactured in China.

The miner submitted supporting documents in the third quarter of 2023 and again in the first quarter of 2025. It also paid $180,000 of the duties in 2024, according to its later filing. As of June 2025, management said it didn't consider a future cash outflow probable and hadn't recorded a provision for the dispute.

That was an accounting assessment based on what the company knew at the time. In August 2025, Customs responded that duties were required, and Bitfarms reported the payment two months later.

Bitfarms disclosed $180,000 of customs duties paid in 2024 and a separate $11.9 million payment to Customs in October 2025, including interest. The filing’s stated duties and interest components do not reconcile to its reported October payment.

Source: Bitfarms September 2025 financial statements, customs duties note

The company hasn't disclosed enough to reconstruct every step of the customs process or establish whether it paid under protest. Nonetheless, its filings do show how a dispute over a shipment can remain relevant to a company's finances long after the initial purchase.

Bitfarms has since reorganized under Keel Infrastructure and shifted toward AI and high-performance computing. It paid the customs bill before that transition reached a major milestone: Keel stopped its US Bitcoin mining operations in June 2026 as it prepared sites for other uses.

Other miners have also been shifting power access and data-center sites toward AI, while older import disputes still appear in their financial disclosures.

The money involved can affect how much they have available for equipment and projects, although a disclosed potential charge isn't necessarily money they will have to pay.

Import duties can depend on where a product was made, which sounds simple until a machine contains components from several countries and is assembled elsewhere.

US customs forms ask importers to identify the product's country of origin. When production takes place in more than one country, the answer can depend on where the product underwent its last substantial transformation, according to Customs' entry instructions.

The manufacturing process and supporting records can affect the duty owed. The supplier's certificate can support the declared origin, but Customs can still challenge it.

Equipment can also be released and put to work before Customs resolves every issue. Customs uses a process called liquidation to make its final duty calculation for an entry.

Importers can challenge certain decisions through an administrative protest, but deadlines and exceptions depend on the decision being challenged. The public filings don't establish which procedural rules explain every stage of the miners' disputes.

Selling the equipment doesn't automatically settle an importer's customs obligations either. Federal regulations describe duties as a debt owed by the importer, which can be discharged through payment or applicable legal relief.

That doesn't mean the buyer of a secondhand machine automatically inherits the seller's customs bill, since the importer and the terms of any transaction need to be identified separately.

IREN received a notice in April 2025 concerning hardware it imported between April 2024 and February 2025. Customs asserted that the equipment was of Chinese origin and assessed a 25% tariff, while IREN said the seller and shipping documents supported a different origin.

The company contested an exposure of approximately $100 million. In October 2026, Customs approved its protests and accepted the declared origin of the covered hardware, IREN reported in its September-quarter filing. No additional tariff, administrative fee, or interest is payable for that matter.

The $100 million was an amount IREN had disputed and no longer has to pay for the covered imports. The company hasn't disclosed enough about the decision to establish whether the same reasoning applies to other shipments or other miners.

CleanSpark has had a mixed outcome so far. In its June-quarter filing, submitted in August, the miner said Customs had approved some of its protests over machines imported in 2024, while other entries remained contested.

If Customs prevailed on the remaining entries, CleanSpark estimated that its total tariff liability for the previously purchased machines could reach approximately $130 million, excluding interest. Management considered a loss improbable and recorded no provision as of June 30.

DarkHorse Technologies, previously known as Sphere 3D, faces a smaller claim that shows how an older equipment purchase can appear in the accounts of a miner with a different name and business plan.

In August, Customs notified DarkHorse that it was asserting Chinese-origin duties on machines purchased in 2022 by a subsidiary acquired in the Cathedra Bitcoin combination.

The company says its subsidiary received certificates from the seller supporting a non-Chinese origin, and it filed a protest in September. Its latest quarterly report puts potential liability at up to $2.3 million, including interest, if Customs prevails. DarkHorse has not recorded a liability.

Company estimates if Customs prevails. CleanSpark's was approximately $130 million excluding interest as of June 30. DarkHorse's was up to $2.3 million including interest in its Sept. 30 report. Reporting dates and interest treatment differ.

DarkHorse is separately selling its legacy mining fleet as it shifts toward AI infrastructure. Its quarterly report estimated $2.9 million in proceeds, with $1.3 million from machines sold by Sept. 30.

Bitfarms reported a payment, IREN's covered assessment has been resolved, CleanSpark's estimate excludes interest, and DarkHorse's includes it. Adding them together would treat a past payment and an avoided charge as outstanding bills.

CleanSpark's remaining protests and DarkHorse's challenge will determine whether either miner faces a further payment on the disputed imports.

IREN's result shows that a challenge can succeed, while Bitfarms' earlier payment shows why investors can't assume that a company's initial accounting assessment will be the final word.
