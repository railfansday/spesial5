From warships to subway cars: 10 U.S. artificial reefs for fishing and diving

Artificial reef fishing and diving in the U.S. spans sunken warships, retired oil platforms and subway cars — here are 10 places to know about

An aircraft carrier, a missile tracking ship and the steel frames of old oil platforms now sit on the U.S. seafloor. So do retired New York City subway cars. Each one was put there on purpose to give fish something to live on and divers something to explore.

These structures are artificial reefs. The term covers anything deliberately placed on the seabed to create habitat, from sunk ships and retired oil and gas platforms to concrete and quarry rock. Bare sand and mud offer marine life little to hold onto. A hull or a pile of rock adds hard surfaces, crevices and vertical relief where growth can take hold and fish can gather.

The practice has a federal framework. The National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984 set up a national approach to artificial reef development, and coastal states run their own reef programs within it. Several of the sites below are managed or permitted through state wildlife agencies and reef programs.

For anyone planning a trip around artificial reef fishing and diving in the U.S., the range is wide. The sites in this list stretch from the Florida Panhandle and the Florida Keys to Texas, Louisiana and Alabama in the Gulf of Mexico. They also reach Hawaii, Southern California and the mid-Atlantic coast off Delaware and New Jersey.

The structures vary as much as the locations. Some are former military vessels, including Navy ships and Coast Guard cutters. One is a ship that carried troops and cruise passengers before it became a training vessel. Others are retired oil and gas platforms converted to habitat through programs known as rigs-to-reefs. One set of reefs is made from retired subway cars. One California reef was built from quarry rock and concrete to offset kelp losses rather than mainly for recreation.

Access differs just as much. One of these shipwreck dives sits at a depth reachable by recreational divers. Several others lie in deep water suited to divers with advanced or technical training. Many also draw anglers, who reach them on charter boats from ports such as Pensacola.

What follows is a selection of 10 places, not a ranking and not a complete list. Each slide covers what the structure is, where it sits and who it serves.

The USS Oriskany is a former Navy aircraft carrier that rests on the floor of the Gulf of Mexico roughly 24 miles off Pensacola, Florida. Its deep-water setting makes it a dive for advanced technical divers, while anglers fish above it from charter boats.

The Navy sank the carrier on purpose on May 17, 2006, to turn it into an artificial reef. The ship measures 888 feet long. That length is behind the nickname it often goes by, the "Great Carrier Reef."

Before it became habitat, the Oriskany spent decades in Navy service. It was an Essex-class carrier commissioned in 1950. Its name comes from the Battle of Oriskany, fought in New York in 1777 during the Revolutionary War. The ship served during both the Korean War and the Vietnam War.

The carrier's history includes a deadly accident. In October 1966, a fire broke out aboard the ship off Vietnam and killed 44 crew members. John McCain was flying from the Oriskany when he was shot down over North Vietnam in 1967. The Navy decommissioned the carrier in 1976, three decades before it went to the bottom of the Gulf.

Diving the wreck calls for more than a standard recreational certification. The carrier lies deep enough that divers need technical training and equipment suited to that depth. That puts it out of reach for newer divers who hold only an entry-level certification.

The trip out is part of the commitment. The wreck sits in open Gulf water, well away from any sheltered bay. Every visit starts with a boat run from Pensacola.

Pensacola charter boats run reef fishing trips out to the wreck. Anglers on those trips work the water around the carrier from the deck and never need to get wet.

The site serves two groups with different needs. Technical divers come to see a sunken carrier up close. Anglers come for the fish that gather around its structure.

The USS Vandenberg is a former Air Force missile tracking ship, 523 feet long, that was sunk as an artificial reef on May 27, 2009. It lies about six miles south of Key West in roughly 140 feet of water, and it is a dive for advanced divers.

The sinking ended the ship's working life. Its hull now sits as a fixed structure on the seabed near the southern end of the Florida Keys.

Distance shapes how people reach the wreck. At about six miles offshore, it is a boat dive, not a site anyone can swim to from the beach. Key West is the closest land.

Depth shapes who can dive it. The seabed around the ship sits at roughly 140 feet. That is why the Vandenberg is treated as an advanced dive and not as a site for newly certified divers.

The ship does not lie flat against the bottom. Its structure rises well above the seabed. Divers can explore parts of the wreck that stand far higher in the water column than the sand around its base.

Size places the Vandenberg among other large ships sunk as reefs in Florida. At 523 feet, it is 365 feet shorter than the 888-foot USS Oriskany, the aircraft carrier sunk off Pensacola in 2006. It is 13 feet longer than the 510-foot Spiegel Grove, a Navy landing ship dock sunk off Key Largo in 2002.

The Vandenberg also lies much closer to shore than the Oriskany. It sits about six miles from Key West, while the carrier rests roughly 24 miles off Pensacola.

The Vandenberg was the most recent of those three ships to go down. The Spiegel Grove was sunk seven years earlier, and the Oriskany three years earlier.

The water is also deeper than at the Spiegel Grove, which rests at about 130 feet. The Vandenberg site runs roughly 10 feet deeper.

The Spiegel Grove is a 510-foot former Navy landing ship dock sunk as an artificial reef off Key Largo, Florida, on May 17, 2002. It rests about six miles offshore inside the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, in about 130 feet of water, and is a dive for advanced divers.

A landing ship dock is built to carry troops, vehicles and landing craft. Its stern holds a well deck that can be flooded, letting smaller boats float in and out of the ship. That design gave the Spiegel Grove a long hull with large open interior spaces.

The sinking did not go as planned. The ship rolled onto its side as it went down and came to rest on its flank rather than upright on the sand. For about three years, divers explored a wreck lying on its side.

Hurricane Dennis changed that in 2005. The storm helped push the hull upright, giving the wreck the orientation its planners had intended. The episode shows that a sunken ship can keep shifting long after it reaches the seafloor, especially in a region exposed to Atlantic hurricanes.

The location adds a layer of rules. The Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary is a federally protected area managed by NOAA, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Congress designated it in 1990. Its waters surround the island chain and take in natural coral reefs, seagrass beds and other shipwrecks. Activities inside its boundaries fall under federal sanctuary regulations in addition to Florida state rules.

At about 130 feet, the sand around the wreck sits at the commonly accepted depth limit for recreational scuba. Bottom time there is short before decompression becomes a concern, and nitrogen narcosis is a greater risk. Divers usually arrive with deep-diving training and recent experience.

Key Largo is the northernmost of the main Florida Keys islands. It sits at the top of the chain, which makes it the closest Keys dive hub to the Miami area. Dive boats leave from the island to reach the wreck.

The Duane and Bibb are two retired U.S. Coast Guard cutters sunk as artificial reefs off Key Largo, Florida, on November 27, 1987. The Duane rests in about 120 feet of water and the Bibb in about 130 feet. Both wrecks are dives for advanced divers.

The two ships were sisters. Both belonged to the Coast Guard's Treasury class, also known as the Secretary class. Each cutter in the group was named for a former secretary of the Treasury. The Duane honors William J. Duane, who held the post under President Andrew Jackson. The Bibb honors George M. Bibb, who served as Treasury secretary under President John Tyler.

The Duane measures 327 feet from bow to stern. Cutters of this class were built as long-range patrol and rescue ships. During World War II, both the Duane and the Bibb escorted convoys across the Atlantic. German submarines attacked Allied shipping along those routes.

The cutters went down on the same day but came to rest in different positions. The Duane settled upright on the sandy bottom. The Bibb landed on its side. That difference shapes each dive. The Duane keeps the familiar profile of a ship, with its superstructure rising toward the surface. The Bibb presents its deck and hull as a vertical wall.

Both wrecks lie near Molasses Reef, a natural coral reef off Key Largo. The sites sit inside the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. Congress designated that sanctuary in 1990, three years after the cutters were sunk.

Depth is the main planning factor. Both cutters lie well below the 60-foot limit that PADI sets for its entry-level Open Water Diver certification. They also sit deeper than the 100-foot limit tied to PADI's Advanced Open Water Diver course. Divers visiting either wreck need training and gas planning suited to that depth. They also need a charter operator that runs trips to these sites.

The Texas Clipper is a former troop transport, cruise ship and training vessel that now serves as an artificial reef. It rests in roughly 130 feet of Gulf of Mexico water about 17 miles off South Padre Island, Texas.

The ship went down on November 17, 2007. The Texas Parks and Wildlife Department's artificial reef program manages the site.

Every stage of the ship's working life involved carrying people. It entered service with the U.S. Navy as a transport for troops. It later carried vacationers as a cruise ship. Its final role was as a training ship for future merchant mariners at Texas A&M University's maritime academy in Galveston.

South Padre Island sits near the southern tip of the Texas coast, close to the Mexican border. Reaching the reef requires a boat trip across open Gulf water. Offshore weather and sea conditions decide whether a trip can go ahead.

Depth shapes who can dive the site. PADI, one of the largest scuba training agencies, sets 130 feet as the maximum depth for recreational diving. The seabed at the Texas Clipper lies at roughly that depth. At such depths, no-decompression time is short and nitrogen narcosis becomes more likely. A descent to the bottom calls for deep-diving training and careful gas planning.

Upper parts of the hull sit shallower than the seabed. Divers who stay on those sections keep their maximum depth above the sand.

Anglers fish the site too. For them, the wreck is a fixed location that boats can return to with GPS navigation. The Gulf floor off South Texas is largely flat. A ship-sized structure on that bottom stands out as a place where fish concentrate.

The Texas Parks and Wildlife Department is the agency to check for current information on the reef's exact position and any rules that apply to fishing or diving there. The Texas Clipper is one of many sites in the department's artificial reef program along the Texas coast.

Retired oil and gas platforms in the Gulf of Mexico off Texas and Louisiana can stay in the water as artificial reefs instead of being hauled to shore. The approach is called rigs-to-reefs. Both states run formal programs through their wildlife agencies.

The Texas Parks and Wildlife Department oversees the Texas program. The Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries runs the Louisiana program. Once a structure joins a state program, that state takes on responsibility for it as a reef site.

An offshore platform stands on a steel frame known as a jacket. The jacket reaches from the seabed up to the surface. When production ends, federal rules generally require operators to remove the structure. Conversion to a reef offers an alternative to full removal.

Three methods are common. In a tow-and-place conversion, the jacket is cut free and moved to a designated reef site. In a topple-in-place conversion, the jacket is laid on its side where it stood. In a partial removal, the upper section is cut away below the surface. The lower framework stays standing, with enough clearance left for passing vessels.

Operators that choose conversion avoid part of the cost of bringing the steel back to shore. The Texas and Louisiana programs each receive a share of those savings. That money helps pay for managing the reef sites.

The steel legs and cross-braces create vertical habitat through much of the water column. Barnacles, sponges and corals settle on the metal. Small fish gather around that growth, and larger predators follow them.

For anglers, converted platforms are a regular destination for charter and private boats. Red snapper and greater amberjack are among the fish commonly associated with Gulf platforms. Divers visit the structures as well, on boats that run out from coastal ports.

Many converted platforms sit well offshore in open water. Reaching them takes a seaworthy vessel and close attention to Gulf weather. The state programs publish coordinates for their reef sites. Those coordinates help boaters locate the structures.

The Mahi is a former U.S. Navy minesweeper resting in about 85 feet of water off the Waianae coast of Oahu, Hawaii. That depth puts it within reach of recreational divers, a contrast with the deeper ship reefs in the Gulf of Mexico and the Florida Keys.

The ship is about 185 feet long. It was sunk on purpose in 1982 to create an artificial reef. Hawaii's Department of Land and Natural Resources, the state agency responsible for the islands' land and ocean resources, was involved in the sinking.

The Waianae coast lines the leeward, western side of Oahu. That shore faces away from the northeast trade winds that dominate the island's weather. The Waianae Range rises behind it, and the leeward side receives far less rain than Oahu's windward coast.

Depth is the main planning question for a visit. Entry-level open water certification from training agencies such as PADI covers dives to about 60 feet. A dive to the Mahi generally calls for advanced certification or equivalent deep-dive training. That is a much smaller step than the technical training needed for a deep wreck such as the USS Oriskany off Pensacola, Florida.

Bottom time is short at this depth. No-decompression limits at about 85 feet are measured in minutes, not hours. Dives on the wreck are planned around that limit and around a controlled ascent. Divers reach the wreck by boat rather than from shore.

The Mahi predates the federal framework for artificial reefs. The ship went down two years before Congress passed the National Fishing Enhancement Act of 1984.

The Mahi is the only Hawaii site and the only Pacific shipwreck in this group of 10. The other ship reefs covered here lie off Florida, Texas and Alabama, in the Gulf of Mexico or the Florida Keys. For divers on Oahu, the wreck is a ship reef within reach without travel to the mainland.

Retired New York City subway cars rest on the seafloor off Delaware and New Jersey. State reef programs placed them there as fish habitat. Anglers reach the sites by boat.

The cars came from the Metropolitan Transportation Authority, the agency that runs New York City's subway. Between 2001 and 2010, the MTA sent retired cars to several states on the East Coast and the Gulf of Mexico for reef building. The Delaware and New Jersey programs are two well-known examples of where those cars ended up. Cars from the same subway system became habitat along more than one U.S. coastline.

The material sets these reefs apart from other well-known U.S. artificial reefs, many of which are decommissioned warships, Coast Guard cutters or retired oil and gas platforms. These reefs are made from mass-transit equipment. Cars that once carried commuters through New York City now sit on the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean.

A subway car is a long steel box. On the seabed, its walls give marine growth somewhere to attach. Its interior and openings give fish places to shelter.

Black sea bass and tautog are among the species anglers target on these reefs. Tautog are also widely known as blackfish. Both species tend to stay close to hard bottom and structure. The cars add that kind of habitat to an offshore seafloor that is largely sand in this part of the Mid-Atlantic. Structure that holds those species gives anglers a specific place to fish.

The program also shows where reef material can come from. The cars were public equipment that had reached the end of their service lives. The MTA sent them to state programs that could put them to use as habitat.

Wheeler North Reef is a man-made reef of quarry rock and concrete off San Clemente, California. Southern California Edison built it to offset kelp losses linked to the San Onofre nuclear plant. That makes it a mitigation project, not a site built mainly for anglers or divers.

The reef takes its name from Wheeler J. North, a California Institute of Technology marine biologist who studied kelp forests. Its goal is different from that of many reef programs, which sink ships or place structures to attract fish and give recreational boats a destination. Wheeler North Reef was designed to replace natural habitat that had been lost.

The habitat in question is kelp forest. Giant kelp, the species that forms these forests off Southern California, needs a hard surface on the seafloor to attach to. It grips rock with a rootlike structure called a holdfast. It cannot take hold on loose sand or mud.

Rock and concrete on the bottom supply that missing anchor. Once kelp attaches, it can grow toward the surface and form a forest. A mature kelp forest provides shelter and food for fish, snails, sea urchins and other marine life.

The power plant behind the project is the San Onofre Nuclear Generating Station. It sits on the coast just south of San Clemente. Kelp losses tied to the plant created the need for replacement habitat.

The structure also differs from the wreck dives elsewhere on the U.S. coast. There is no hull, deck or superstructure to explore. The reef is a spread of rock and concrete meant to support living kelp. The marine life it holds is the main feature, not the structure itself.

California sets fishing rules through the California Department of Fish and Wildlife. Anyone planning to fish or dive near the reef should check current state regulations and any local restrictions before heading out.

Alabama's offshore waters in the Gulf of Mexico hold many permitted artificial reef sites, and reef fishing is a major draw. The attraction is not a single wreck. It is a network of deliberately placed structures spread across designated reef zones off the state's coast.

The Marine Resources Division of the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources runs the state's artificial reef program. The agency oversees where reef material goes and which sites are permitted. Each placement on the seafloor is authorized rather than left to chance.

Some of the older structures in the zones are Liberty ships sunk in the 1970s. Liberty ships were cargo vessels built in large numbers in U.S. shipyards during World War II. They moved war supplies across the Atlantic and Pacific. Decades after the war ended, some of them went to the bottom off Alabama to begin a second life as habitat.

The rest of the zones hold a mix of other deliberately placed material rather than one type of structure. That spread-out model sets Alabama apart from destinations built around one named ship. A boat leaving the Alabama coast is not heading to a single landmark. Its crew can choose among many permitted sites in the same stretch of the Gulf.

Red snapper is the species most closely tied to reef fishing off Alabama. Snapper and many other reef fish stay close to hard structure on the seafloor. Permitted sites give charter crews known locations to target on a trip. Charter fleets run reef trips out of coastal towns such as Orange Beach and Dauphin Island.

Red snapper harvest is limited to set seasons, and those dates can change from year to year. Other species, including grouper and amberjack, have their own rules. Anglers heading out to the reef zones need to check current Alabama and federal regulations for bag limits and open dates before they leave.
