The Secondhand Gifting Conundrum

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Asking for a friend: would you be happy with a secondhand gift for Christmas?

In the current holiday preamble, shoppers are seeking out pre-loved luxury, and hopeful recipients are adding resale finds to their wishlists. While aspirational consumers seek more bang for their buck, top-end consumers favor one-of-a-kind pieces with a story to tell.

According to Rati Sahi Levesque, The RealReal CEO, gift shoppers are spending big and small this holiday season: designer accessories under $250 and collectible pieces over $10,000. “One of the things I love about resale is that great taste isn’t confined to a particular price point,” she says, explaining that shoppers can do more with less, or invest in something extraordinary for their recipient. “Resale gives you access to things that money alone can’t buy in the primary market.”

This November, Vinted searches for Christmas gifts were up 35% year on year, with interest building steadily since August, according to the platform. Similarly, on Depop, searches for “Christmas gifts” grew 248% year on year, climbing another 321% in the last month. The small luxuries category is building momentum for gifting, with searches for “Coachtopia” and “tall Uggs” up 132% and 96%, respectively, in the last month.

According to Deloitte’s holiday shopping report, 29% of US consumers may buy pre-owned luxury items as Christmas gifts this year. In the UK, 47% of 18 to 45-year-olds are willing to gift pre-loved items, per Ebay’s recent re-commerce report.

The pre-loved gifting surge is in line with broader growth in resale. In 2025, Boston Consulting Group and Vestiaire Collective estimated the global secondhand market’s value at $210-220 billion, forecasting a value of $320-$360 billion by 2030. As mentioned, value for money is a big draw, against a backdrop of inflation and a cost-of-living crisis. Vinted reports that the average adult fashion item sold on the platform is 72% less than RRP, while 69% of members believe Vinted gives them access to higher-quality or designer brands. For some shoppers, it’s also a way to resist the consumerism and waste associated with Christmas.

In this landscape, and as Gen Zs continue to favor messier, more eclectic and unique styling, gifting vintage is no longer niche. Nor is it easy. For gift-givers, pre-loved presents often involve more mental labor — either for the customer or their personal shopper. It requires skill to source a rarefied handbag or archival design — especially one in good condition.

As well as middle-income shoppers, high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs) are shopping secondhand this Christmas, with higher-ticket spending on rarefied goods. Sarah Jordan Buss, founder of The Vintage Project, a Beverly Hills boutique esteemed for high-profile clients, says that the budget for gifting usually sits around $1,000. Paris-founded resale platform Resee is also popular with HNWIs at Christmas. “The holiday gifting season is huge for us,” says co-founder and CEO Sofia Bernardin.

The rub? If shoppers are to nail that choice, platforms and sellers need to demonstrate that the gift has provenance, quality, authenticity and a story behind it. Competitors fighting for market share must sharpen their value proposition through personalization, product curation, clienteling, and strong certification measures.

For wealthy gifters, the process before the transaction is important. Evangeline Li is a high-net-worth curator based in London who often shops vintage accessories and jewelry to gift. She looks to 1990s Chanel, vintage Trifari and Mikimoto designs. Her spend bracket per gift is around £300-£1,000, depending on the person and piece. “I enjoy the hunt almost as much as the discovery itself,” she says, adding that she regularly visits antique markets in Britain, flea markets in Paris, and vintage stores in Japan, while also trawling Ebay, Etsy and Google.

Platforms report a strong interest in secondary market Chanel for Christmas gifting.

More recently, Li has become increasingly reliant on AI in her sourcing process. “It’s particularly useful when I’m searching for something unusual or difficult to describe as it can lead me to objects, designers and specialist dealers I might not otherwise have discovered,” she says.

For Li, it’s not just about designer names, but the historic and sentimental value. “I also enjoy finding gifts that are a little unexpected or playful: antique dice for friends who love games, a mid-century Tiffany & Co. double-deck playing card set in its signature blue box, or nostalgic collectibles such as Pokémon cards and vintage Hello Kitty dolls,” she says.

The RealReal’s Levesque concurs. “It doesn't have to be a big, recognizable logo. A vintage gold pendant for someone who loves layering jewelry or a beautiful Loro Piana belt for someone with a very understated wardrobe can feel much more personal than the obvious choice.” As she sees it, for affluent loved ones, the most desirable fashion items are pieces you can’t buy firsthand — they’re gifts the recipient might never have discovered.

When Buss is advising a client on a vintage gift, she has a few techniques to make it extra special. Often, she pulls a design from the year the intended recipient was born. “People really respond to that detail,” she says. Another technique is to consider small anecdotes the recipient might have made. “It might be a collection they mentioned in passing months ago or a piece from a designer they’ve loved for years — even something they regret getting rid of,” she says.

Resee also records an appetite for highly personalized luxury gifts. On the platform, the famous “Love” poster cards that Yves Saint Laurent would send to his collaborators annually have become a go-to gift during the holidays. Because they are dated — the series ran between 1970 and 2007 — gift givers choose specific designs with the same date as the recipient’s birth year. “It’s all about giving people something that feels as though it was specifically found for them,” says Bernardin. Of course, online, fakes and copies of these posters are rife, but Resee’s authentic poster cards come with the original postage stamps and shipping tubes, which offer a culturally richer gift. “I think the storytelling aspect is so important when it comes to giving a vintage gift,” Bernardin adds.

For Buss, bags are the most common gift clients request, and Chanel, Gucci and Chloé are some of the most popular brands. Often, the founder tells Vogue Business, she receives client requests for the original versions of bags that have been reissued, such as Saint Laurent’s Mombasa design and the Chloé Paddington, signaling the importance of provenance and cultural cachet. Secondhand marketplace 1stDibs is seeing traction on items such as Elsa Peretti’s Tiffany & Co. designs, Cartier watches, Hermès scarves, and iconic handbags and travel bags. Hard-to-find releases, such as Matthieu Blazy’s Chanel designs also perform well for gifting. “Buyers are gravitating toward classics that are easy to gift and easy to style, and that the recipient will cherish for years,” says Anthony Barzilay Freund, 1stDibs’s editorial director and director of fine art.

On The RealReal, consumers continue to spend healthily on household names: Hermès, Prada, Gucci, Cartier, Rolex and Tiffany & Co. “But what I find equally interesting is the appetite for pieces that are a little less expected,” says Levesque, pointing to momentum in emerging designers and distinctive finds — whether that’s a statement sweater from Zankov or collectible ornaments by artist Caroline Zimbalist. The executive also points to strong demand for archival pieces tied to specific designer eras, such as an Yves Saint Laurent Rive Gauche tassel belt from the 1970s or an embellished Chloé bag from the Phoebe Philo era.

Ebay also observes an interest in archival gifting. Kirsty Keoghan, Ebay’s general manager for fashion and luxury in Europe, highlights Prada’s sportier line, Linea Rossa, launched in 1997, as well as Pucci and Blumarine as big hits. She also calls out Karl Lagerfeld-era Chanel, John Galliano’s Dior and Tom Ford-era Gucci.

“Vintage is not a renewable resource and is thus the ultimate luxury,” says Mattie Barringer, co-founder of cult fashion and art label Women’s History Museum, which also runs a vintage boutique within the brand’s Canal Street store. The boutique’s esoteric range specializes in collectible items with fashion-historical value, spanning ’20s pumps through to ’90s Jean Paul Gaultier tops, as well as a selection of items under $100 for smaller budgets. “We feel an item or object with a life before really connects us to our shared humanity and history. What could be more Christmassy!”

While pre-loved gifting is gaining traction, shoppers can be put off by a few obstacles. Many of them are the same hangups associated with vintage in general. Where the thrill of the search remains important for many consumers, some are put off by the hassle and fear of landing a counterfeit. Here, platforms need to offer smoother shopping processes to unlock a larger portion of the market.

“Many shoppers still lack the confidence to complete a purchase because they are unsure whether they can trust the seller, the listing, or the protections available,” says Keoghan, encouraging shoppers to use Ebay’s authenticity and money-back guarantees where necessary. That way, “they can be absolutely certain that the gift they’ve picked out is going to be right,” she says.

This matters. In the gifting economy, platforms are competing with more traditional dealers, many of whom have longstanding relationships with their clients. The key criteria for discerning gift shoppers? “Condition is always my first consideration. I want a piece to be beautifully preserved, especially when I’m buying it as a gift,” says Li. “Beyond that, I really appreciate sellers who understand the history of their objects,” noting the importance of when something was made, how it was used, and what makes it interesting. Li is also hot on transparency, especially regarding authenticity and restorations that might have taken place on a given item. “I value dealers who are knowledgeable, honest, and genuinely passionate about what they sell,” she says.

In this vein, The RealReal offers a filtering function to streamline products that are “pristine” (new with tags), and the platform curates holiday gifting edits online and in-store that are informed by the brands, categories and pieces customers are most interested in by location. Additionally, the platform’s retail experts can offer personalized guidance for clients finding a gift based on the recipient’s taste, lifestyle and wardrobe.

“HNWIs are used to high-touch clienteling, discretion and access, so they expect private sourcing, authentication and care that feels closer to a luxury store than a resale feed,” says Sophy Vanner Critoph, strategy partner at creative agency Amplify. “Aspirational shoppers want speed, sharp cultural curation and zero friction: clear grading, fast delivery and a seamless experience that feels current and reliable.”

Resee’s inventory constantly shifts, but the focus remains on provenance and historical value.

Another issue for platforms to address? Shoppers with less fashion knowledge might be scared to buy something secondhand; the primary market has already been vouched for by a department store or a sales advisor. And if the gift isn’t right, vintage often involves more convoluted return processes, compared to the streamlined approach of online primary retail.

“We kept hearing women who would say, ‘I don’t want my husband to pick anything out for me. I just want him to give me something so I can buy myself something on Reiss,” says Bernardin. “So, we decided to do a vintage gift card.” Women’s History Museum Vintage also offers gift cards if the gift-giver isn’t confident in picking themselves. “There’s always an option to give the gift of a [vintage] shopping gift,” confirms Barringer. “In person, at our shop, via our website, or even Instagram.”
