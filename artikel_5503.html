A Mortgage Rate Buydown Could Make Your Next Home More Affordable

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

You want to find the right home, but you also need the right monthly payment. And that can be hard when it feels like just about everything right now is putting a squeeze on your budget.

But there’s a tool that can help, and many buyers have never heard of it – it’s called a rate buydown. Here are the basics.

When you’re borrowing money to buy a home, it usually works like this. The higher your mortgage rate, the bigger your monthly payment. And with rates where they are now, some buyers are feeling the crunch. That’s where a buydown can really come in handy.

A buydown lets you pay some money upfront to get a lower mortgage rate – and by extension a better monthly payment. Basically, it’s a way to get a lower mortgage payment without waiting for mortgage rates to fall.

There are two main types, and they work a little differently.

A temporary buydown lowers your rate for a specific amount of time – usually the first one to three years. When the buydown period is up, you begin making the full payment amount. The loan’s official rate stays the same – you’re just getting help with part of the payment in the early years.

Think of it like a gift card for your mortgage. Money goes into an escrow account at closing. Each month, part of your payment comes out of that account instead of your pocket.

Here are the most common options when it comes to temporary buydowns:

1-0 buydown: Your rate is 1% lower for the first year.

2-1 buydown: Your rate is 2% lower the first year and 1% lower the second year.

3-2-1 buydown: Your rate is 3% lower the first year, 2% lower the second, and 1% lower the third.

A permanent buydown is a bit different. The big draw here is that they last for the entire loan, which could be 15 or 30 years – not just a couple of years like a temporary buydown. But normally, the monthly savings are smaller.

It’s paid for with points. Generally, one point equals 1% of your loan amount. And each point usually lowers your rate about a quarter of a percentage, but this differs from lender to lender. The important distinction?

A temporary buydown gives you short-term payment relief.

A permanent buydown can lower your payment for as long as you keep that mortgage.

With more homes available for sale, more sellers and builders are offering incentives to attract buyers. Some are even willing to pay for your buydown.

And whether that’s advertised up front or something you ask for in negotiations, it can have a bigger impact on your bottom line than you’d think.

In this example, the temporary buydown provides roughly $520 in monthly payment savings during the first year, compared with about $170 a month from a permanent buydown.

That’s a big difference in the short term. But there’s an important tradeoff.

As mentioned before, the temporary buydown savings don’t last forever. Once the buydown period ends, the full mortgage payment kicks in. That’s why it’s important to compare the numbers over time, not just during the first year.

This is where a lender can really help you explore your options. A buydown isn’t automatically the best choice for every buyer. It depends on your budget, how long you plan to stay in the home, and what you’re trying to accomplish.

A temporary buydown could give you extra room in your budget during the first couple of years, when you’re also managing moving expenses and settling into your new home. And if you expect your income to go up over time, this can be a good option.

On the flipside, a permanent buydown could make more sense if you plan to stay put for years and want the reassurance that the savings are sticking around until you move.

Either way, if you’re considering a buydown, the advice is simple.

Make sure you can comfortably afford the full mortgage payment, even if you’re starting with a temporary reduction.

Factor in what it could look like if the payment goes up after the buydown period ends. You want to be sure your budget can handle it.

Remember, lenders check whether you can afford the full payment, not just the discounted amount.
