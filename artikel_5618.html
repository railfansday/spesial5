Mapped: Where the Most Homeowners Own Their Homes Outright With No Mortgage

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Roughly 3 in 5 American homeowners pay a mortgage every month, yet the share of those who own their property outright is steadily growing—with at least half of all homeowners in three states now completely mortgage-free.

According to a new Realtor.com® data analysis of the recently released 2025 American Community Survey 1-Year Estimates, 59% of homeowners carried a mortgage last year, while 41% owned their homes free and clear.

That marks a major shift from 2010, when fewer than a third of homes (32.8%) were owned outright while 67% carried home loan debt.

Mortgaged homeownership has shrunk across all four regions, though reliance on financing still varies significantly by geography. The West had the highest mortgage debt rate at 63.8%, while the South recorded the lowest at 56.8%. The Northeast and Midwest tied at 58.7%.

At the state level, West Virginia led the nation with 56.6% of its homeowners living mortgage-free, followed by Mississippi (52.7%) and New Mexico (50%).

On the other side of the spectrum, Washington, DC, in 2025 logged the lowest share of outright homeownership, at just 26.5%, trailed by Maryland (30.6%) and Colorado (31.6%).

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu explains that the balance between mortgaged and free-and-clear homes meaningfully shapes housing market dynamics, especially at a time when mortgage rates are high.

"Areas where owners rely more heavily on mortgages are more sensitive to rate changes, because monthly payments and the cost of moving both rise with borrowing costs," says Xu. "Areas with a higher share of outright ownership are less exposed and may feel smaller effects from rate shocks because residents who own their homes outright are likely indifferent to changes in borrowing costs."

Ultimately, those market forces sway inventory levels, home prices, and whether first-time buyers can access homeownership.

With mortgage rates stuck above 7% and the Federal Reserve all but certain to raise interest rates in December—and possibly again in January 2027—to combat inflation, the lock-in effect is poised to chill the market again.

Homeowners sitting on low mortgage rates are reluctant to give them up, keeping much-needed inventory off the market. That supply squeeze, in turn, puts upward pressure on prices and makes it harder for renters to access homeownership.

Free-and-clear owners, however, are not bound by borrowing costs and can list whenever they choose.

"A mortgage-free owner isn’t watching interest rates to decide when to move," Christie Goldman, a real estate agent with Better Homes and Gardens Real Estate Central in Charleston, WV, tells Realtor.com. "They can buy their next home with cash, which makes their offer stronger, and they can close on their own timeline without a lender, an appraisal contingency, or rate risk."

Across the 50 states and the District of Columbia, West Virginia stands out with the lowest share of mortgaged homes, at 43.4%, and conversely the highest share of outright ownership, at 56.6%.

Two main forces drive this dynamic: relatively low median home prices and an older population that tends to hold on to properties longer than residents in many other states.

"In West Virginia, a paid-off home isn’t a someday goal. It's how people here live," says Goldman. "When people put down roots here, they stay."

It is common for a local family to purchase a home, raise their children there, and pay it off, often with the next generation buying property in the same community.

According to Goldman, affordability is what makes that cycle possible.

In October 2026, the median asking price in West Virginia was $259,999, well below the national median of $415,000.

"At those prices, owning your home free and clear is a realistic goal here, not a distant dream," says the agent, pointing to the 2025 United Van Lines’ national study that ranked West Virginia as the second-leading inbound destination for movers.

"Many of those buyers sell a higher-priced home elsewhere and can pay cash here," says Goldman. "Once they discover the mountains, the community, and the pace of life, they don’t want to leave."

Xu also attributes West Virginia's high share of mortgage-free owners to the state's slow population growth, particularly among young adults, which has kept new borrowers from entering the market in large numbers.

Between 2015 and 2025, the population aged 18–34 fell 5% in West Virginia, while the same age group in the U.S. grew 3.8%.

Owning one's home outright offers many perks, sharply reducing monthly expenses and creating a buffer against economic headwinds.

"Mortgage-free homeowners have more financial flexibility, especially with today's higher interest rates and rising living costs," Thomas O’Shaughnessy, vice president and real estate broker at Clever Real Estate, tells Realtor.com. "They don't have monthly mortgage payments, which makes it easier to manage expenses, save money, and handle unexpected repairs or emergencies."

In theory, a high percentage of debt-free homeowners can also help unlock inventory for the next wave of buyers.

"They may be more willing to list, downsize, move elsewhere, or buy again," says O'Shaughnessy. "All of these can improve inventory and help move the market in your community."

In West Virginia, however, Goldman notes that high levels of mortgage-free ownership does not necessarily flood the market with fresh listings because people put down deep roots and tend to stay in their homes for decades.

On the flip side, Washington, DC, and Maryland log the highest shares of mortgaged homes in the nation, at 73.5% and 69%, respectively—a trend Xu links to the region's job market.

"Federal, professional, and contracting employment draws midcareer workers, who buy homes and borrow soon after arriving," she notes.

In 2025, nearly 45% of DC homeowners and 36% of Maryland homeowners were aged 35 to 54. Elevated housing costs also force many buyers to finance: The median listing price in DC was $549,000 and $424,894 in Maryland last month.

Given that local homeowners carry so much mortgage debt, rate hikes hit markets like DC and Maryland particularly hard.

"Many buyers there need loans, so fewer can afford to buy, and homeowners with low-rate mortgages are less likely to sell," says Xu.

The latest ACS data from the U.S. Census Bureau shows that in 2025, homeownership among households aged 75 and up rose to 78.3%, up from 75.7% in 2010.

During the same period, the mortgage-free share among this demographic fell from 79.7% to 71.3%. In other words, most baby boomers still own outright, but the share with a loan rose from 20.3% to 28.7%.

Xu says the two trends—more seniors owning their homes, and more of those owners carrying a mortgage—together shape when senior sellers are likely to list.

"A senior with a low-rate loan faces a lock-in effect in today’s high-rate environment," says Xu. "Selling means giving up that cheap financing for a more expensive loan on the next home, which can keep the house off the market longer and slow the handoff to the next generation."

Meanwhile, seniors who own outright can sell on their own schedule, whether for health, care, or downsizing, so their home is more likely to pass to the next generation when the owner’s circumstances change.
