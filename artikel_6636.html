How To Know If Your Nature Strategy Is Working

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

This story is part of ‘The Business Case for Nature’, a series exploring fashion’s impact on nature and vice versa. Read more here.

Over the past few weeks, Vogue Business has dug into the business case for nature, how to build a winning nature strategy, whether to hire a head of nature, and multiple case studies from brands and non-profits leading the way. But there is still one question we don’t have an easy answer for: how can fashion measure success in nature without reverting to oversimplified vanity metrics?

This problem isn’t unique to nature, but it is particularly difficult in the natural world. “If there’s one thing we know about nature, it’s that it’s very complex and very hard to put into neat boxes. But checking boxes is usually what brands want to do,” says Philippe Birker, co-founder and managing director of regenerative agri-food organization Climate Farmers.

Nature is amorphous, stretching far beyond farm boundaries and specific supply chains to encompass the entire living world. Painting a holistic picture of impact in nature would require near-constant monitoring of thousands of metrics. Whether a brand is transitioning its cotton supply chain to regenerative agriculture and wants to measure how much more carbon is stored in the soil, or it is investing in upstream water resilience to help its suppliers weather flood risks, there is no single, constant metric that captures progress. “Any metric we create will be an oversimplification, so it will always be a challenge,” says ecologist and Nature’s Echo author Tom Crowther.

When you gather data is just as important as what data you choose to focus on, says Jesse Smith, director of land stewardship at US regenerative agriculture hub White Buffalo Land Trust. “If you were trying to measure human health, would you take someone’s temperature in the morning when they wake up, or at night when they go to sleep? Would you do it when they’re sick or when they’re getting better? Before or after they take a probiotic? Ecosystem health is like human health in that regard — there’s so much fluctuation throughout the day, season and year. Hypothetically, it would be great to have a continuous read, but that costs time and money. So the question is: when do we take an assessment of that living entity and call it a representative value? It’s always a snapshot in time.”

The challenge is that brands investing in nature want to see a return on investment, whether they’re supporting biodiversity-boosting efforts or anti-deforestation campaigns. This can be critical to unlocking the funds for future nature-based projects, and proving to executives that these interventions actually help secure the brand’s long-term business resilience. What’s the best way forward?

Which impact metrics come to the fore says as much about who holds the power in supply chains as it does about the impact of nature-based solutions.

Growers like Smith see impact data in three buckets: management, storytelling and research. First, there are metrics they define for themselves, which help them make better land management decisions. Then, there are metrics that come later in the season, which show the impact of certain land management practices over time, which might be necessary to maintain a certification — these are the storytelling metrics. The research bucket is typically facilitated but rarely funded by the grower themselves. More likely, it’s funded by universities and the scientific community, who might need more granular or rigorous sampling to prove out their theories. “If getting a certified product into the marketplace took the same level of detail as publishing a peer-reviewed paper, the price point would be untenable,” he adds.

At White Buffalo Ranch, Smith manages a grass-fed, grass-finished beef operation. He faces similar challenges to a farmer producing leather for fashion. In order to manage his beef cattle successfully, Smith needs to measure how much feed is in each pasture, so he knows how long the cattle can graze there before he needs to move them on. To maintain his certification under the Savory Institute’s Ecological Outcome Verification (EOV), Smith monitors a longer-term set of indicators, including whether the ecological function of his pastures improves by the end of the grazing season. More speculative data monitoring falls under the ranch’s collaborations with the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) — which is monitoring methane fluctuations in California soils — and UC Santa Cruz — which has been looking into how dung beetle lifecycles are affected by the use of antibiotics and dewormers on livestock.

Workers at the trust monitor the health of the local environment through soil, water and biodiversity.

“A customer doesn’t really need to know that, and I don’t need to know it in order to make management decisions,” says Smith, “but it’s going to help inform the next generation of policy initiatives, funding priorities and best practices in agricultural management.”

Farmers with tighter resources cannot measure an infinite amount of impact indicators, says Vikrant Giri, founder of Gallant International, which helps brands source organic and regenerative organic cotton, primarily from India. Brands need to be understanding of this, and avoid compounding the pressure on these smallholder farmers by demanding in-depth data monitoring. What matters most to these farmers — and their peers, who might be watching to see what happens before adopting regenerative organic practices themselves — is business resilience. In the last few years, Gallant International has been able to prove an 18% uptick in farmers’ earning potential (because they spent less on synthetic pesticides and fertilizers) and a 25% increase in cotton yields (thanks to a pilot using carbon-rich biochar, made from farm residues, to improve soil health). “Our goal is that, in four to five years, our farmers should earn at least 50% more than they do currently. If the next generation sees the benefits of farming, they will stay in farming rather than migrating to the city.”

Increasingly, Climate Farmers is expanding its scope to also measure social and economic factors, including stress levels among farmers — who often experience elevated suicide and mental health risks — and profitability on farms. “If those metrics don’t improve, farmers can’t sustain nature regeneration in the long term,” says Birker.

On-site at Brunk Farm, a training site used by Climate Farmers to pilot and demonstrate regenerative agriculture to other farmers.

Nature is not a monolith, and there is no one-size-fits-all approach to measuring the success of nature-based solutions, says Smith. “What you are trying to achieve in leather is probably going to be different to wool or cotton, because they’re grown in different systems, with different issues that need to be addressed. It’s not about finding a one-size-fits-all approach, it’s about leaning into the complexity and working with communities on the ground to achieve the goals you set together.”

Still, there are common best practices and principles that apply across the board. For Smith, these are: keeping soil covered and protected; supporting and increasing biodiversity below and above ground; and reducing disturbance events like over-tillage or the over-application of chemicals. For the Regenerative Fund for Nature, the key principles are slightly different: soil health, biodiversity, climate, water, livelihoods and animal welfare. “We set the principles, and then individual projects get to set the metrics that make sense for their landscapes and communities,” says Conservation International’s senior director of global fashion and nature initiatives, Virginia Keesee, who oversees the fund.

Even within a single plot of land, impact metrics can vary wildly. That’s a major challenge for fashion brands, which tend to operate on a global scale. “Planet Earth is one ecosystem,” explains Smith, “but within that, we have different eco regions and ecological sites.” He operates within the Central California coastal eco-region, but his land spans multiple ecological sites, including coastal sage scrub, annual grasslands, riparian corridor buffers, oak woodlands, oak savannahs, and managed agroforestry like orchards and vineyards. “Each site has its own unique matrix of plants, animals, topography and geology. So in each site, I need to measure different indicators.”

Best practice is for brands to support farmers financially with data monitoring, especially where the metrics in question matter more to the brand than the farmer, says Birker. “Farmers don’t usually have the cash flow to pay for these things. Our training is not expensive — between €1,000 and €2,000 — but farmers often ask to pay in installments, or after they sell their harvest. So we always ask corporate partners to shoulder the cost of new projects, because they’re the ones benefitting from the data.”

Aggregators and bridge organizations like CottonConnect can help brands access farm-level data, says CEO Alison Ward, and they can also fund the process. “We have team members working hand-in-glove with farmers who speak not just the local language, but the local dialect. They’re delivering training, but they’re also monitoring impact at the same time,” she explains. “We shouldn’t be doing anything that loses farmers money.”

Cotton plant under inspection for pest attacks, and overall health.

In India, Gallant International has trained two women in each village where it operates to gather and maintain impact data. The company funds this to reduce the burden on farmers, says Giri. “We provide tools, dedicated staff, training, pay for certifications and guarantee purchases for the farmers,” he explains. “Brands spend so much on advertising. If they spent just a small portion of that on hiring visible, credible people to run these projects and measure the impact, they would be much better off.”

Zooming out, there is a growing movement to turn ground-level impact data into something materially significant for companies and economies.

At New York Climate Week, Himalayan venture capitalist Tenzin Seldon captured the finance world’s attention when she proposed a new concept she is calling Gross Domestic Regeneration (GDR). The way Seldon sees it, Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is a country’s income statement — the summation of what it produces every year. GDR, however, is the balance sheet — the record of what it cost society to produce that much. If GDP looks at the cost of chopped timber, GDR considers the value of a whole, standing forest. If GDP counts the response to a natural disaster as growth, GDR measures the cost of what was lost.

Tenzin Seldon sees Gross Domestic Regeneration (GDR) as an alternative to Gross Domestic Product (GDP), which can capture nature regeneration alongside social and economic metrics.

Seldon is in the process of putting together a consortium of experts who can stress-test her 135-page white paper on GDR and turn it into a full-scale economic system. Many of the impact metrics they will examine are the same ones fashion is using to measure success in nature-based solutions: whether water, soil and forests are getting stronger; whether communities are getting healthier and more resilient; whether investment is compounding value rather than extracting it.

“It pivots from an extractive model that discounts and depletes nature, to one where the end goal is thriving habitats, where our children and grandchildren can breathe clean air and inherit standing forests,” says Seldon. “We have to make regeneration so compelling that every constituent in every country demands leaders look at the whole balance sheet.”
