Keeping the Diwali Flame Burning in New York City

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

The akhand diya, the continuously burning flame, has one purpose: to stay lit. It is a small but enduring presence, tended to around the clock, its care passed from one person to the next so that the light never goes out. I’ve often thought about how much of life is like this, how we are constantly passing the torch from one version of ourselves to another.

As a teenager, I remember feeling that I had crossed an invisible threshold into womanhood. I was becoming someone different from the girl I had been, carrying parts of her with me but beginning to understand the world through a new lens. Now, years later, I find myself at another such transition: I am becoming a different version of a woman, one I am still getting to know.

In many ways, this transition is happening outside of me. Thirteen years ago, I moved away from my family in Mumbai and the home where Diwali had always been celebrated. For all those years, the rituals continued, even as I participated in them from thousands of miles away, mostly over video calls. Back in Mumbai, Diwali meant a house full of family and friends, kitchens bustling with cooking and frying, and the warm glow of diyas illuminating the mandir. I was no longer physically there, but I remained part of it. This year, for the first time, I hosted my parents for Diwali in the home I’ve built for myself in New York City.

It felt cataclysmic, or at least I was making it feel that way in my head. As I welcomed a different version of myself, I was also taking on a new role within my family. I was no longer simply the daughter returning to their house for Diwali; I was the one opening my home, preparing the food, tending to the rituals, and making space for the people who first taught me what this celebration means. There is perhaps no greater joy than watching the people I love receive and experience care. And yet, this was a responsibility I had chosen to take on, and one that felt particularly daunting as the eldest daughter. I wanted to get it right.

For me, that care has always found its way to the table. Food is how my family celebrates, how we mark occasions, and, perhaps most importantly, how we look after one another. So naturally, much of my energy went into planning the meal. Despite my best efforts, my dad promptly assigned himself the task of making the goat kari—apparently, even after 13 years away, some things hadn’t changed. We headed to our local butcher in Brooklyn to buy fresh goat meat, and almost immediately, his attention turned to the spice tolerance of my guests.

“How much spice can your friends tolerate?” he kept asking me. “Should I keep it at a level six? But our level six might be their level ten. What do I do?” he lamented. “Relax, they’ll be able to handle it! Just make it our level five,” I told him.

I gave in and handed over part of the kitchen to my mother, too. She took charge of the paneer, cooking generous chunks in a luscious onion-tomato gravy and making the chapatis. Selfishly, I didn’t mind relinquishing that responsibility: This would be my one chance for the year to eat chapatis fresh off the tava, puffed up and piping hot, and made by my mother.

As the days drew closer, I found myself loosening my grip on the plans I’d made. My parents had their own ideas about what needed to be cooked, and while I was initially resistant to them taking over (any immigrant will tell you how hard it is to get our parents to just relax!), I was learning that hosting didn’t necessarily mean doing everything myself. It also meant making room for everyone to contribute and allowing new rituals to form.

The evening began with freshly fried moong vadas. My mother, of course, insisted on frying them for everyone, right before serving. Made from soaked and ground mung lentils, these little fritters are crisp and golden on the outside, with a soft, almost creamy interior. They have a naturally earthy, nutty flavor from the lentils, sharpened with green chiles, ginger, and spices.

Moong vadas are best eaten immediately, still hot out of the oil, with a tangy green chutney. The scene reminded me of Diwalis back in Mumbai, when the vadas rarely even made it to the serving platter: Everyone would gather around the kitchen, waiting for the next batch to come out of the kadai, reaching for them before they had a chance to cool. I saw the same thing happening in my kitchen in New York.

In the meantime, my friends Leanne and Christina took charge of the tamarind margaritas, my ode to the soft, sweet-tart tamarind candies I grew up savoring as a kid. The tangy cocktails were a welcome counterpoint to the savory fritters, the perfect way to wake up our taste buds (and made exercising some restraint with those moong vadas before dinner particularly difficult). My brother helped set the table and move furniture around, just as we used to do for our parents back in Mumbai. There was something deeply comforting about watching us fall into these old rhythms.

The table eventually came together with a mix of dishes that felt both familiar and celebratory. There was chana masala from my cookbook, East to East, alongside my dad’s goat kari, slow-cooked with spices until the meat was tender. My mother’s saucy tomato-onion paneer was accompanied by a pile of chapatis that disappeared almost as quickly as they came off the stove. We served everything family-style, laying out the dishes on kansa thalis, traditional Indian plates made from bronze or bell metal, an alloy of copper and tin. They reminded me of the way my grandparents served meals, and I wanted to bring a little of that into our Diwali celebration.

At the center of the spread was a vegetable pulao, fragrant basmati rice cooked with carrots, cabbage, cauliflower, and whole spices. Alongside it, I placed bowls of pickled onions and mango achaar, whose sharp, tangy bite cuts through the richness of the karis. There was also cucumber raita, cool and creamy, offering a welcome respite from the heat of the spices. These accompaniments might seem like small additions, but they are essential to the balance of an Indian meal. Every bite can be a little different, depending on what you bring together on your plate.

For dessert, I made homemade gulab jamun, my brother’s favorite. The soft dumplings are made from milk solids and fried until golden, then soaked in a warm saffron-and-cardamom-scented sugar syrup.

As I sat cuddled among both my families, I realized that while it wasn’t the perfectly orchestrated dinner I’d imagined when I first started planning, it was something better. The family I was born into celebrating Diwali alongside the family I’d chosen and built in New York, all gathered around the same table and enjoying every minute of it. Perhaps keeping the flame alive was never about re-creating the past exactly as it was but about allowing it to illuminate whatever comes next.
