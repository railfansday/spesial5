Faisal Islam: What's in the Brexit reset food deal and are there trade-offs?

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Some British sausages have faced barriers to being exported to the EU

In Boston, Lincolnshire, a supplier of ready meals to Marks & Spencer (M&S) manufactures thousands of portions of spaghetti bolognese to be sent to the retailer's shops in the Republic of Ireland.

Every plastic pallet is meticulously individually tagged with a green-grey label âEireâ.

Every truck full of food sent to European Union countries requires hundreds of pages of forms, checks by vets, expense and delays.

It has cost M&S millions of pounds and is part of that reason the British business shut down 11 French stores in 2021.

Many smaller exporters stopped sending their goods to the EU entirely. Some produce, including sausages and certain types of seafood, were entirely stopped.

All this was the predictable consequence of former Prime Minister Boris Johnsonâs 2020 deal prioritising regulatory freedom over freeflowing goods trade.

That will end with a Brexit "reset" that Prime Minister Andy Burnham is set to sign with the EU on Friday.

The government claims it could boost the economy to the tune of Â£5bn a year and cut the cost of food for households.

But will the agreement mean a return to frictionless trade for food with the EU or are there trade-offs and quid pro quos the UK will have to make?

EU affairs minister Hamish Falconer, who is a key negotiator with the bloc, acknowledged a âchoiceâ is being made.

In exchange for being able to export produce such as Lincolnshire sausages to the 27 countries, the flipside is the extent to which the UK will be able to influence EU food policies.

The UK will have a "voice in the room", said Falconer, but no vote. Critics have said a reset will make the UK "a rule taker", not a rule maker.

But Falconer said the UK will be involved in a process to shape the rules.

A Brexit reset would not involve any upfront payments, said Falconer, but made it clear there could well be an "exit clause" in case a future UK government reversed the closer alignment.

He said this was "reasonable" to consider what would happen if a future Conservative or Reform government wanted to undo the new arrangements. Both parties have said they would if they were in power.

"The EU is worried about that and you can see why," he said.

The BBC understands that the clause is a result of EU concerns about the possible cost of having to rebuild facilities for border food safety checks which were put in place following Brexit.

The prime minister has said he believes there is a "win-win" deal to be had with the EU.

Speaking at a Marks & Spencer food court in central London, Falconer said reducing the cost of importing food "will mean less cost for supermarkets, and I hope that less cost then turns into cheaper produce on the shelves".

He said companies have to currently deal with "pages and pages" of form-filling.

Some exports are rejected for having the wrong colour of ink on forms, or an inaccurate Latin name for cooked meats.

The reset could also mean "tearing up" checks that were introduced on goods flowing from Great Britain to Northern Ireland, following Brexit, he said, as well as separate "Not for EU" labelling.

The whole deal to be signed on Friday is calculated to add 0.4% to UK GDP.

Minister for EU relations Hamish Falconer

Tom Bradshaw, president of the National Farmers' Union, welcomed reduced border inspections and paperwork but said that for the NFU it was "a moment of opportunity and risk" with farmers waiting to see what carve-outs and transitional arrangements would be included.

Trade volumes in food exports to the EU had "fallen significantly - by over a third" since 2019, he said.

But the UK should take a "pragmatic" approach to implementing any new rules.

He raised pesticides and precision breeding - or gene edited crops - as areas where he hoped some compromise would be found.

UK firms are, for example, developing tomatoes engineered to contain extra vitamin D.

Historically the EU has had much stricter rules on gene-editing, but this year relaxed regulations.

Falconer said people would have to "wait and see the detail" in this area.

Another contentious area has been crisp flavourings, with concerns raised that stricter EU rules on certain ingredients might limit the sale of some popular snack flavours in the UK.

"I want to take this opportunity to absolutely stamp down on misinformation about smoky bacon crisps," Falconer said.

Post-Brexit the UK had stayed "pretty closely aligned" to European food safety standards, he said.

Two leading snack brands had already adapted their recipes to accommodate EU rules, an example of the kind of alignment that would happen whether or not there was a reset of UK-EU relations.

"The truth is, almost every business in this country that wants to export food or drink will be aligning with EU rules because it's our biggest market.

"Lots of people, like the good people at Walkers and Frazzles have made these changes already, so I would want to reassure people, particularly smoky bacon fans, it's okay," he said.
