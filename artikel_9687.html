Half of what's trending is a base model wearing a new name

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Myth: the trending board on the model hub tells you which models are good.

It doesn't. It tells you which weights people are converting, merging and fine-tuning this week. Those are different claims, and only one of them survives contact with prod.

I keep re-learning this. I'll see something unfamiliar climbing the board, assume it's a fresh release, pull the card, and find a base_model field pointing at something I already evaluated two months ago. Scroll far enough and a big share of the entries resolve to the same root — right now a lot of them resolve to Qwen3.8-27B. Quants in four formats. A couple of merges. Something with "coder" in the name that's the base plus a LoRA. One abliterated variant, because of course. That's not the board malfunctioning. That's the board working exactly as designed. It's a derivative-activity feed with a release-feed layout, and the layout is what fools me.

Here's the part that actually costs people money. A finetune publishes a benchmark table. The number gets screenshotted, credited to the finetune, and repeated in a thread. Three days later you deploy it and get base-model behavior plus a chat template nobody documented.

Most SFT and DPO passes on a strong base don't add capability. They reshape the output distribution. They change tone, refusals, formatting, tool-call syntax, how eagerly the model volunteers a plan. Useful work — genuinely, some of it is excellent craft — but it's a delta on top of a capability that already existed. If the base couldn't do the task, the finetune can't either. It can only make the base more likely to show you the version of itself you asked for.

So when a derivative scores well, what I want to know is: what did the base score, on the same harness, with the same prompt format? When nobody publishes that comparison, the number is a coin flip between "this finetune is good" and "the base was already good and the finetune shipped the chat template that unlocks it."

The second case isn't a scam. It's just a much smaller claim than the one being made.

Concrete example, because I'm still annoyed about it. I spent two days tuning a system prompt against a 27B I'd found on the board. Weird instruction-following failures, stubborn formatting, model ignoring my role definitions. I assumed it was the finetune's fault, then assumed it was mine, then finally opened the tokenizer config.

The uploader had baked a system preamble into the chat template. Every request I sent already had an invisible set of instructions prepended before mine. I'd been fighting a prompt I never wrote.

That's it. That's the whole bug. The capability I was trying to coax out was there; the plumbing was lying to me. Two days of my life for a field I could have read in the first ten minutes. Now base_model, chat_template, and the uploader's other repos are the first three things I check. Eval numbers come after.

I'm not arguing derivatives are worthless. The opposite. The derivative layer is where a model family becomes usable, and the trending board is a decent proxy for how healthy that layer is.

Quantizers are a good tell. People who build GGUF and low-bit conversions do it because they expect the thing to run, and they're usually right about which architectures convert cleanly. When a base shows up in three quantization formats within days of release, that's a signal about convertibility and about how much trust the community has in the weights. Nobody spends GPU hours on a base they suspect is a dead end.

Finetuners matter for a different reason: they find the use cases the lab didn't. Someone ships a tool-calling version because the base is good at structure but bad at emitting valid JSON on the first try. Someone merges two specialists because neither alone handles their workflow. You learn more about a base from the derivatives than from its own model card, which is a bit sad but it's true.

And that's the moat, honestly. Not the weights. The weights leak, the recipes leak, the architecture gets copied in a quarter. What doesn't get copied is a hundred people who've already converted your model to run on a 3090, fixed the tokenizer edge cases, and written the template that actually works. A family with an ecosystem is more valuable than a family with a better benchmark, because the ecosystem is the part you don't have to build yourself.

That last one is the whole myth, really: credit flows to whoever publishes the chart, not to whoever did the work. The base model did most of the work. The quantizer made it runnable. The finetuner made it pleasant. The person who uploaded the weights gets the downloads.

I haven't run Qwen3.8-27B long enough to tell you whether the base is worth your weekend — I've mostly been poking at things built on top of it, which is sort of the point. But I can tell you that if you credit a derivative with a capability it inherited, you'll eventually deploy it for a task the base was never good at, and no amount of prompt engineering will fix a problem that lives in the weights.
