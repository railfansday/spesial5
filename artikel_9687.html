A reel did the rounds this week about Cicada, an open-source app that's supposed to turn any call into a bilingual one. The pitch: a client in Tokyo speaks Japanese and you read English subtitles at the bottom of your screen. You hold one key, answer in English, and they hear Japanese in the call. Nothing extra joins the meeting, so as far as they can tell, you just speak Japanese. When the call ends, the notes are already written.

That's a big promise, and a short reel can't show the parts that matter: what needs a paid key, where your audio goes, and how wrong it gets. So I read the repo, installed the Linux build on my Linux machine and ran a full meeting through it. I didn't use any cloud AI key. Everything ran on local models on the same machine.

Short version: the plumbing is real and well thought out. On Linux, Cicada creates its own virtual microphone, and I recorded my translated Spanish coming out of it. The quality you get depends almost entirely on the AI service you plug in, so I'll be specific about which results came from Cicada and which came from my models.

The repo is github.com/wquguru/cicada, Apache-2.0, by @wquguru. It was created on 1 November 2026 and had 2,781 stars and 439 forks when I checked on 24 November 2026. The latest release is v1.10.1, published on 14 November. It's moving fast: 15 GitHub releases went out between 3 and 14 November, from v1.0.0 to v1.10.1.

The trick that makes "nothing joins the call" true is that Cicada never connects to the meeting. It reads your computer's audio output and writes to a microphone device. To the meeting app it's just a mic.

I put all three behind one OpenAI-compatible endpoint on 127.0.0.1. It's worth seeing what Cicada asks the listening model for. Each stretch of speech arrives as a WAV inside a normal chat message, with a request for exactly three lines back:

One round trip gives you the language, the transcript and the translation. That's why any audio-capable chat model can sit behind it. Cicada's built-in check reported the text model answering in 0.8 seconds and listening in 0.6 seconds.

For the test meeting I wrote a 68-second Spanish product meeting: four turns, 12 sentences, covering a 3 March launch, a $40,000 budget, a load test on Monday and a report due on Wednesday. Writing the script myself meant I knew exactly what was said. I played it through the system audio, the way a call would sound.

On the first run I left Fix misheard words live on, which sends an extra text request for every sentence. With Whisper, the 3B model and live notes all competing for 8 CPU cores, one fix request took 52 seconds. After that, Cicada's listening requests started timing out at 20 seconds.

The app didn't freeze or pile up a backlog. The notes window said:

Subtitles and notes wait for 127.0.0.1 - they resume once it's back

Then it showed "Can't connect â€¦ Will resume automatically; audio while offline is lost." I like that it tells you it's dropping audio instead of pretending. If you run heavy local models on a laptop CPU, expect to see this banner.

For the second run I turned off live fixing, gave Whisper and Ollama 4 threads each, and played the same meeting again.

Cicada cut the 68 seconds into 11 segments and detected Spanish straight away. Each segment's round trip to my models took 2.7 to 14.6 seconds, with a median of 4.2 seconds. The slow ones landed while the notes request had the CPU. Lines that came through clean:

This was the part I cared about most, because it's the claim nobody shows on camera.

In onboarding, the interpreting step creates the microphone and flips to Ready. The sound server then showed what Cicada had built:

The null sink is where Cicada plays the translated voice. The remap source turns that sink's output into a real microphone that any app can pick. With no headphones plugged in, it also puts WebRTC echo cancellation in front of the speakers, so the other side's voice isn't mistaken for yours. The docs say it hands back your original mic and speakers when you turn interpreting off or quit. On Linux this needs a PulseAudio or PipeWire setup that lets apps load modules, which is how a standard Ubuntu desktop ships.

Then I ran the full path. With Cicada still listening to the Spanish meeting, I started interpreting (cicada --speak, the same action as Super+Shift+S). The log read "started en -> es": it chose Spanish because that's what the room had been speaking. I played an 11-second English reply into the mic input:

"Thanks, LucÃ­a. I agree with the March third launch. I will review the load test results on Tuesday and send my feedback the same evening."

At the same time I recorded from Cicada Microphone, which is exactly what Zoom or Meet would hear. Spanish audio started about 1.4 seconds after the English ended and ran for about 11 seconds. Cicada's log put the lag at 4.8 and 9.4 seconds after the end of each sentence. When I ran Whisper over the recording, it heard "Gracias, lucha, estoy de acuerdo con el lanzamiento del 3 de marâ€¦". That's espeak's robotic Spanish saying "Gracias, Lucia", misheard as "lucha" on the way back.

So the core claim holds on Linux: one language goes into your mic, and the meeting app's microphone carries another.

The reel's own caption says machine interpretation can miss nuance, and my run produced a textbook case.

Cicada cuts your speech into segments at pauses. My sentence was split right in the middle of "load test":

The listener hears that I'll review "it", then a fragment about test results, and then "envÃ­e". In Spanish that reads like an instruction to send feedback, not my promise to send it. Every word was translated, but the commitment got muddled. On a real client call, that's exactly how a misunderstanding about who owes what starts.

A stronger model will translate each piece better, but splitting at pauses affects every model. For prices, dates, contracts and who owes what, confirm in writing after the call.

I liked one safety detail. On an earlier take, a 0.6-second blip reached the model, and it replied with an apology in Spanish saying the text was empty. Cicada logged "only a filler, not spoken" and kept it out of the call. When a segment came back with nothing usable, it logged "not interpreted, not spoken". It never spoke a guess.

Also worth knowing from the docs: on Linux the notes window can't hide from screen sharing, so it's labelled "Visible in screen shares". The Mac, Windows and Android builds hide it by default. Don't share your whole screen with the notes open.

If you sit in calls or watch streams in a language you only half-follow, the subtitles and notes alone are worth installing. On an Apple silicon Mac they cost nothing. On Windows or Linux, budget for an AI service, or point Cicada at a GPU machine on your LAN. My run shows a laptop CPU works, and that more compute buys you accuracy.

Interpreting is the headline, and the plumbing works. Whether it's good enough to speak to a client through depends on the service behind it, and on you writing down the numbers afterwards.
