Glucose-powered implant brings devices for treating diabetes a step closer

Junta Imai is in the Department of Diabetes, Metabolism and Endocrinology, Tohoku University Graduate School of Medicine, Aoba-ku, Sendai, Miyagi 980-8575, Japan.

Hundreds of millions of people around the world are affected by diabetes mellitus1. It develops when the activity of insulin, a glucose-lowering hormone secreted by pancreatic β-cells, becomes insufficient to maintain healthy glucose levels in the blood. The condition can be managed with oral medication or by injecting insulin and other metabolic hormones, but the constant need for people with diabetes to manage their lifestyle and blood-glucose levels can impair their quality of life. Therapeutic approaches that monitor and control blood glucose automatically are therefore highly desirable. Writing in Nature, Xiu et al.2 report proof of principle of a bioelectronic device called NeuroAxis that does exactly that in rat models of diabetes.

There are two main types of diabetes mellitus. Type 1 diabetes is caused by severe impairment of insulin secretion owing to the loss of pancreatic β-cells3. By contrast, type 2 diabetes arises from a complex interplay of factors, including insulin resistance (the state in which cells fail to respond to the hormone) and impaired insulin secretion4. The degree to which each factor contributes to the development of type 2 diabetes varies from case to case. Type 2 diabetes is also often accompanied by chronic inflammation, which impairs coordination between the nervous, immune and metabolic systems, disrupting this essential regulatory network5.

Xiu and colleagues’ device was designed to treat diabetes by stimulating the activity of the vagus nerve, which connects various organs in the body to the brain. Afferent vagal signals transmit metabolic information from peripheral organs to the central nervous system, whereas efferent vagal signals travel in the opposite direction to modulate the functions of peripheral internal organs. These afferent and efferent signals collectively regulate systemic metabolism and maintain normal bodily conditions (homeostasis)6.

Vagal signals increase glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and promote the proliferation of pancreatic β-cells7–10. They also reduce the release of glucose from the liver to the bloodstream11. Moreover, vagal signals are involved in suppressing inflammation, and thereby improve insulin sensitivity5. These observations suggest that manipulation of vagal nerves is a potentially promising therapeutic option for managing diabetes mellitus.

When designing NeuroAxis, Xiu et al. addressed one of the most persistent bottlenecks in bioelectronic medicine: the requirement for external power sources, which constrains long-term implantation. The authors bypassed this limitation by building a system that is powered exclusively by glucose in the body. At the heart of the device is a biocompatible fuel cell that converts locally available glucose and oxygen into electricity using robust, enzyme-mimicking nanomaterials.

Crucially, this glucose fuel cell performs a dual role: it acts both as an energy harvester and as a real-time metabolic sensor. Fluctuations in tissue glucose concentrations dictate the output voltage of the fuel cell, creating an intrinsic metabolic feedback loop. When sustained high glucose levels (hyperglycaemia) elevate the voltage above a threshold value, miniature, low-power microelectronics automatically trigger the device to produce a programmed sequence of electrical pulses. These pulses are delivered to the vagus nerve in the oesophagus wall using a soft, self-healing electrode that seamlessly moulds and shapes itself around the oesophagus without causing tissue stress.

Xiu et al. evaluated NeuroAxis in rat models of type 1 and type 2 diabetes. When the type 2 rats were on a high-fat diet, the device reduced excessive food intake, increased energy expenditure and limited body-weight gain compared with animals that lacked the device. NeuroAxis also restored indices of insulin resistance almost to healthy values in these animals, and lowered blood-glucose levels in fasting type 2 rats to levels observed in fasting animals without diabetes.

Profiling of liver tissue showed that the device suppressed the expression of genes involved in the biosynthesis of glucose in the model animals, and restored the activation of insulin signalling cascades (the biological pathways that are normally triggered by insulin). The authors also observed that, in adipose tissue in these rats, vagal stimulation by NeuroAxis drove a shift from a state characterized by the presence of pro-inflammatory immune cells known as M1 macrophages to a state characterized by the presence of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages.

Equally compelling results were observed in the rat model of type 1 diabetes, in which animals were injected with a toxin called streptozotocin that selectively damages pancreatic β-cells. In these rats, vagal stimulation promoted the proliferation of undamaged β-cells and progressively restored the number of functional β-cells in the pancreas. Furthermore, vagal stimulation dampened pancreatic inflammation and reduced an immune response in which cytotoxic T cells infiltrate the pancreas to destroy the remaining undamaged β-cells. Taken together, the animal experiments demonstrated that stimulation of the vagal nerve restores control of blood sugar levels in models of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (Fig. 1).

Figure 1 | An autonomous neuromodulation device. Xiu et al.2 report NeuroAxis as a proof-of-principle design for implants that combat diabetes and their testing of the device in rats. NeuroAxis incorporates a fuel cell that is powered by glucose in the body. When sustained high glucose levels raise the output voltage of the fuel cell above a threshold value, microelectronics in the body of the device trigger electrical pulses. A soft electrode delivers these pulses to the vagus nerve, which runs along the wall of the rat oesophagus. In a model of type 1 diabetes, vagal stimulation improved the animal’s ability to control blood glucose levels by increasing the number of β-cells — the cells that produce insulin, a glucose-lowering hormone — in the pancreas. In a rat model of type 2 diabetes, NeuroAxis increased the sensitivity of the animal’s cells to insulin and lowered body mass, thereby improving control of blood glucose.

Despite these striking findings, several technical and physiological challenges must be resolved before clinical application becomes feasible. First, because the vagal nerve innervates several internal organs, it is paramount to ensure that NeuroAxis does not produce off-target effects. The parameters of the vagal stimulation, such as the frequency, intensity and duty cycle (the fraction of total time that stimulation takes), must therefore be rigorously fine-tuned to avoid unintended side effects or reduction of efficacy over time in broad clinical populations.

Second, in the rat model of type 2 diabetes, the high-fat diet was discontinued during NeuroAxis treatment and in a post-stimulation follow-up period. It therefore remains to be seen whether the observed therapeutic benefits can be sustained, or even occur at all, if vagal stimulation is delivered concurrently with a high-fat diet. This is a crucial translational issue, given that adherence to dietary restriction is a major challenge in clinical management.

Third, the chemical induction of type 1 diabetes by streptozotocin does not directly mimic the autoimmune mechanism by which this form of diabetes develops in humans. Further studies are required using models in which the condition emerges from an animal’s own immune system attacking β-cells, to evaluate whether vagal stimulation still induces protective responses of the immune system and is therapeutically effective.

The authors observed increases in the blood concentration mainly of gut-produced hormones following afferent vagal stimulation, which implies the involvement of a communication loop that travels from the gastrointestinal tract through the vagus nerve to the brain and back again. However, the pathway by which brain signals loop back to act on the gastrointestinal tract has yet to be clarified. Finally, the diversity of the underlying causes and biological features of human diabetes is not replicated in animal models, which means that the voltage thresholds that activate NeuroAxis might have to be customized for each patient.

Notwithstanding these limitations, Xiu and colleagues’ work establishes a transformative template for bioelectronic therapeutics. By unifying bioenergy harvesting, real-time metabolic sensing and therapeutic nerve stimulation into a single autonomous device, NeuroAxis moves us a step closer to self-regulating medical devices that are capable of treating diabetes.
