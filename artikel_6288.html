Humans Will Go Backwards in Time Within 3 Years, a Scientist Says. But How Would We Know?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Medical advances could give us more healthy years. Proving they can keep adding time faster than we age is a much bigger challenge.

Getting older usually means having fewer years ahead of you. But futurist Ray Kurzweil thinks modern medicine might be on the cusp of changing that, possibly as soon as 2029.

The advances are arriving fast enough, he says, that every birthday could come with the gift of replacing the year you just lived. After that, he predicts, it’s possible that we’ll all start actually gaining time.

Kurzweil calls this “longevity escape velocity.” In a March 2024 interview, he put it even more dramatically, as going “backwards in time.” You’d still technically be getting older, at least by conventional standards. But your expected remaining years of life would go in the opposite direction, getting longer with each passing year as medical interventions improved.

Our earlier coverage took a closer look at that prediction and Kurzweil’s argument about how accelerating medical advances could get us there. S. Jay Olshansky, a professor emeritus of epidemiology and public health at the University of Illinois Chicago, has a less enthusiastic name for it.

“I refer to LEV as voodoo demography,” he tells Popular Mechanics.

Yet Olshansky thinks research into slowing aging could produce important benefits, too. His objection concerns the much bigger promise that those benefits will keep arriving fast enough to outrun aging itself.

Life expectancy describes a population, while lifespan is how long an individual actually lives. To determine life expectancy, researchers calculate how many people die at certain ages and come up with statistical predictions. Plenty of people will live longer, and plenty will die sooner.

That distinction matters when somebody promises to add years to your life. The Social Security Administration’s 2023 period life table, for instance, gives a 60-year-old man 21.79 remaining years and a 61-year-old man 21.04. Both calculations assume that 2023 death rates continue throughout the rest of their lives. They illustrate how expected remaining years generally shrink with age under fixed conditions.

Kurzweil’s prediction depends on medicine improving fast enough to beat those numbers, and to keep beating them every year. Imagine a 60-year-old who is expected to live about 22 more years. If treatments got better quickly enough, that person could turn 61 and find they now had 23 years ahead instead of fewer. That would be an impressive gain. But one year of progress wouldn’t be escape velocity. To stay ahead of aging, medicine would have to do the same thing the next year, and every year after that. Without that continuous progress, it’s just wishful thinking.

Olshansky wants to know what evidence would convince Kurzweil’s supporters that humanity had actually reached escape velocity. Beating one disease wouldn’t be enough. A treatment might save someone from one illness and give them more years, but another illness could still cut that life short.

Olshansky does see reason for hope in aging research. He says work underway “likely will yield reductions in the rate of biological aging.” That could mean people develop age-related illnesses later in life than they would otherwise, giving them more years in good health.

Olshansky would look at whether a treatment changed people’s chances of dying as they got older. Do illnesses show up later, and do people really survive longer? “It’s important that total mortality be the measurable endpoint,” he says. A treatment might prevent a heart attack, but that wouldn’t protect a patient from dying later from cancer. Researchers would have to count deaths from all causes to see whether a given treatment actually bought people more time.

Suppose a treatment delayed several illnesses and helped recipients live longer. That could change how people planned their later years, even if medicine never caught up with aging. People could have more good years after they stopped working, and some might choose to stay on the job longer. Before making those plans, people would need to know how much longer the treatment could keep them alive and well.

For now, gains in life expectancy have been getting harder to come by. In a 2024 study in Nature Aging, Olshansky and his colleagues analyzed the death rates between 1990 and 2019 in places where people live the longest, as well as in the United States. Life expectancy was still generally growing, but the pace of that growth had slowed.

Olshansky explains that much of the big rise in life expectancy in the past came from keeping children alive and preventing mothers from dying in childbirth. Saving a child’s life can add decades to a population’s average life expectancy. But in countries where most people already live into their 70s and 80s, those gains have mostly been made. The remaining deaths happen mainly among older people, and saving an 85-year-old from one illness adds far fewer years. That’s why it’s important to slow down aging. If researchers could slow the biological process behind many diseases of old age, life expectancy might start rising faster again. That possibility, Olshansky says, is what makes the research worth pursuing.

If only a few people received an anti-aging treatment, the country’s life expectancy wouldn’t change, Olshansky says. To find out whether the treatment works, researchers would have to follow the people who took it and compare how long they survive with similar people who didn’t.

And the only way to confirm a promise of 20 extra years is to watch people live them. “A specific prediction of a numerical gain in either life expectancy or lifespan associated with an intervention would require an observed gain of the predicted amount,” he says. Encouraging early results could justify more research, but they couldn’t show how much time a treatment added. Scientists could have good news in 2029 and still need decades to learn how many years people actually gained.

Even then, one success wouldn’t mean we’d reached escape velocity. Kurzweil’s prediction needs each advance to arrive in time to add more years, then another advance to do the same, and another after that. Kurzweil has said that people could still die even if medicine delivered the gains he predicts. Olshansky’s worry is that grand promises could overshadow the research he’s most hopeful about: work that could actually slow aging.

Slowing aging alone would give people plenty to look forward to, deadline or not: more years, and the health to enjoy them. Finding out how many years would take patience, because the people in those studies would still be busy living.
