Democrats are back. So is economist Stephanie Kelton’s big idea

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

In the early days of the Biden administration, after the COVID-19 pandemic and overlapping recession, advocates of government spending to aid the recovery faced concerns that such programs would add to the seemingly ever-growing national debt. 

In addition to arguments about unemployment and the overall health of the economy, backers of spending bills like what became the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act had an increasingly influential economic idea on their side. Modern Monetary Theory, or MMT, arose from the work of numerous economists, but it was especially popularized by the work of Stephanie Kelton,  an economics professor who was a senior economic advisor to Senator Bernie Sanders’s presidential campaigns in 2016 and 2020 and served as chief economist for the Democratic staff of the Senate Budget Committee toward the end of the Obama administration. 

MMT, in essence, argues that deficit spending by governments with the power to issue their own currency isn’t inherently bad. And, as Kelton argued in her influential 2020 book The Deficit Myth, it can be used to promote a more prosperous and just economy. Common analogies between government deficit spending and consumers racking up credit card bills to spend beyond their means miss the mark, MMT’s advocates argue. 

“The work that I do explains why the federal government’s budget doesn’t work like a household budget, and why there is more capacity to spend on priorities, whether it’s healthcare, education, infrastructure, climate,” says Kelton. “Whatever it is, the spending capacity is there.” 

Since 2020, we’ve seen the end of the pandemic, the rise of inflation, and the reelection of Donald Trump, who despite pledges to shrink the size of government and limit international involvement has spent heavily on immigration enforcement and the war with Iran. Now, with a historic wave of Democrats set to take office in the House, Senate, and state governments across the country after campaigning on promises of affordability, MMT may again be poised to influence the direction of government. 

“I know that the book has been read, and the ideas have reached many of the candidates who are now getting prepared to take office in the Senate and House and across the country,” says Kelton, who today serves as a professor of economics and public policy at Stony Brook University, where she’s been based since 2017.   

‘Spending always has consequences’

One of the earliest academic proponents of what became known as MMT is L. Randall Wray, today a professor of economics at the Levy Economics Institute of Bard College. Kelton studied under Wray at the University of Denver early in her career, and the two were later both based at the University of Missouri-Kansas City, where they were affiliated with the Center for Full Employment and Price Stability. At first, their research on monetary theory struggled to gain acceptance in the academic world, Wray says. 

“We had funding, so we could have conferences, and we wrote academic papers,” Wray says. “We gave presentations at academic conferences, and I can tell you none of that had an impact. We were trying to change the economic discipline, and it did not want to be changed.” 

What did help make a difference was a blog, New Economic Perspectives, founded by Kelton in time for both the 2000s blogosphere boom and the questioning of established economic wisdom in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis and Great Recession. “I sort of thought it was a waste of time—this was very early in the blogosphere,” Wray recalls. “But the blog took off, and really, that’s how MMT got to be known.” 

New Economic Perspectives drew the attention of readers in both the formal economics world and beyond, sparking online debates with other prominent economists, including the Nobel laureate and longtime New York Times columnist Paul Krugman. “By the time Krugman heard about it, I don’t know the number, but I would say there were thousands of people in the blogosphere who understood MMT, and we didn’t need to debate Krugman because they would do it,” Wray says. 

In general, the academic researchers who developed and advocated MMT have been interested in communicating their ideas, says Pavlina R. Tcherneva, a professor of economics at Bard College and president of the Levy Economics Institute.  

“A number of us engaged in this research project have spent a fair amount of time communicating some of these ideas to policymakers at the local and national level,” she says, as well as promoting their ideas to an expanding audience via social media and traditional news coverage so they could have influence beyond the confines of academic economics. 

“It started as an academic exercise that was always very closely connected to the idea that we can have good things; that we can secure full employment, greater economic well-being, and price stability,” says Tcherneva, who is also the author of The Case for a Job Guarantee. 

Over time, MMT found adherents in the Sanders campaign and elsewhere, where policymakers were discussing ways government spending could benefit the economy, even as they continued to face traditional questions about how various proposed programs could be funded. In general, there’s been a broad shift by Democrats and their allies in the direction of MMT over the past decade or so, says J.W. Mason, associate professor of economics at John Jay College and senior fellow at the Groundwork Collaborative. 

“People have much less concern about deficits as a constraint on policy than they had, you know, 15 or 20 years ago,” he says. “Over time, we got to something that looked much more like a vision of the world in which people recognize deficits are not the constraint that we had imagined that they were, and I think that Stephanie Kelton and MMT certainly contributed to that shift.” 

Of course, the Biden administration’s recovery spending was closely followed by a wave of inflation. Critics of MMT have argued essentially as long as the theory has been en vogue that its application would lead to inflation, with increased government spending driving up prices overall, and some have portrayed the post-pandemic burst of inflation as proving the theory dangerous and unreliable. 

But MMT advocates like Kelton, who served on a Biden-Sanders economic task force in 2020 that helped craft some of the early ideas around the relief programs, say the theory has always taken into account the fact that excessive spending can lead to inflation. She says she cautioned the Biden administration about this without success, including through a 2021 opinion piece in The New York Times. In essence, says Kelton, while the government can print money as needed, it can “run out of things to buy” if the resources of the economy, including labor and productive capacity, are stretched to their limits, driving up prices. 

“Spending always has consequences, and one of the consequences can be inflation,” says Kelton, who is working on a book challenging preconceptions of inflation. “And so the whole MMT project revolves around recognizing where the limits are.” 

Conversely, she says, some policies that might seem expensive can actually have a deflationary effect. Moving to a Medicare for All-style healthcare system, for instance, would likely increase government spending but could cut overall spending on healthcare by bringing costs more in line with those in other countries. That could lead to deflation and job losses if officials don’t take care to balance that spending elsewhere. 

‘It’s not an operating manual’

The new Congress may also be able to tackle inflation by reversing unpopular moves by the Trump administration; for instance, taking steps to curb tariffs and wind down the war with Iran.

“We were drifting back down toward 2% when the Trump administration walked through the door, and then we got immediately tariffs, and we got a war,” says Kelton, who continues a stream of public appearances and media interviews promoting her ideas and is a founding fellow at the Sanders Institute, the Vermont think tank cofounded by the senator’s wife, Jane O’Meara Sanders. 

While MMT has so far been more publicly embraced by Democrats, Kelton says the economic theory itself is more of a framework or lens that doesn’t itself call for any particular policy. Advocates of tax cuts, for example, might use the theory to understand their potential effect in the same way that those calling for increased spending on social programs might.  

“It’s not an operating manual that tells you pull this lever, turn this thing, do this policy,” says Kelton. “It’s just a framework, so you can apply that framework and say, oh, it looks like the economy has enough capacity that we could do some tax cuts.” 

And like many influential theories, MMT has arguably had an influence on political and economic discussions even when it’s not invoked by name, enabling discussions of potential government programs that aren’t dominated by concerns about funding and the national debt. 

“My sense is that during the Biden era, the pay-for question was not such a big obstacle, in part because the MMT conversation was everywhere,” says Tcherneva. “It almost provided breathing room for people to speak about bold policies—the Build Back Better agenda—that we will try and attempt big things without it being hamstrung by the pay-for question.” 

With incoming Congressional Democrats seemingly looking for ways to make everyday life more affordable, including restored spending on healthcare and food aid, it seems likely that some will turn to MMT for a theoretical understanding—and justification—of increased public spending, even if that requires borrowing. 

“I think you’re going to see a lot of elected Democrats come in looking for a very aggressive public spending program,” says Mason at the Groundwork Collective. “And I think it’s going to be helpful to have some strong arguments that the federal debt is not the main thing we should be thinking about at this moment.”
