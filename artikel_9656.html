Postgres Recovery: Verified Channels over Support Tickets (With Prior Enrollment)

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Prefer a second verified channel for account recovery when it was enrolled before the lockout and still meets the account's assurance requirements; send cases without that evidence to a restricted support review. For a logistics password-reset flow, the deciding question is whether recovery can establish sufficient confidence to replace a credential without handing a stolen session a fresh route back in.

TL;DR: A previously verified second channel is the preferred routine path. A support ticket is the exception path when that channel is unavailable, disputed, or too weak for the requested access. Neither path should silently preserve existing sessions or treat successful delivery of a message as proof that the entire account is safe.

The relevant evidence comes from OWASP's authentication guidance: recovery belongs inside the authentication threat model, and sensitive account changes require appropriate reauthentication. Its password-recovery guidance also calls for consistent responses, expiring single-use reset tokens, and protection against excessive requests. Those controls matter before an auditor ever asks to see a ticket.

Consider the design case of a dispatcher who cannot sign in before assigning a shipment. The support queue is busy, and an existing browser session still appears valid. That creates two different pressures: restore legitimate work quickly, and prevent an attacker with that session from changing the recovery destination. This is a hypothetical design case, not a reported incident.

A channel verified before the recovery request provides evidence of access to an established destination. It does not automatically establish that the destination remains exclusively under the rightful user's control, that it is independent of the compromised credential, or that it meets the assurance needed for a privileged dispatcher account. Two addresses that depend on the same compromised mailbox are a poor basis for claiming independent recovery.

The limitation of the second-channel approach is loss of access to that preenrolled destination: it is not suitable when the destination is unavailable or its independence is in doubt. Use reviewed support in those cases. The trade-off is slower recovery and reviewer workload; support is not suitable as an automatic bypass when it cannot establish sufficient evidence.

The recovery flow should distinguish a destination enrolled during an authenticated, appropriately reauthenticated session from a destination offered after lockout. A ticket saying that the old phone is gone and supplying a replacement number is a request to change the trust relationship. Sending a code to that replacement cannot supply the missing evidence by itself. The support path needs a documented verification policy that doesn't depend on the very channel being replaced.

OWASP's forgot-password guidance warns against changing the account before a valid reset token is presented. Apply that discipline to the whole recovery state machine: accepting a request, delivering a challenge, validating the challenge, and committing a credential change are separate events. A plausible name, shipment reference, or company email in a ticket may help route the case, but the proposed policy should not accept operational knowledge as sufficient authentication evidence. Shipment information exists to move freight; repurposing it as a secret creates an avoidable dependency.

For US and EU operations, define what support may collect, who may inspect it, and when it is deleted before turning the exception path on. This is an operational design recommendation, not a claim that the regions share one legal rule or one required retention period. An audit-ready flow needs a reason for retaining each evidence field, with applicable legal requirements reviewed separately.

The implementation should have one recovery policy with two evidence paths. Both paths converge on the same restricted authorization to reset a credential; neither a successful challenge nor an agent's approval should create an unrestricted application session directly. OWASP recommends returning the user to normal login after password reset rather than automatically logging them in. That separation gives the normal authentication controls a chance to run.

For a Postgres-backed design, represent each recovery attempt with a random identifier, its account binding, the evidence method, an expiry, a consumed marker, and a policy version. These are proposed internal fields, not a public API contract. Keep the raw reset secret out of support tickets and routine logs. Use a cryptographically secure token generator, store the token securely, and enforce a short, explicitly chosen validity window based on the threat model and delivery behavior. OWASP does not supply a universal timeout that fits every logistics operation.

Consume the reset authorization and apply the credential change atomically, with a database condition that prevents a consumed or expired authorization from succeeding. Race two requests against the same authorization during testing: exactly 1 should commit. A preliminary validity lookup followed by an unconditional update leaves a race between the check and the write. That is a concrete implementation trap, even when both requests come from the same browser.

Session handling needs an equally explicit policy. The recommended default for this design is to invalidate existing sessions after successful password recovery, because the recovery path cannot establish that every previously issued session is legitimate. OWASP's forgot-password guidance describes offering session invalidation or performing it automatically; the automatic choice here favors session security over convenience. Drivers or dispatchers may have to sign in again, so the user-facing message must explain that effect before the change completes.

Rate limits should cover recovery requests and challenge attempts without making unauthenticated requests a convenient way to lock out a victim. Return a consistent public response for existing and nonexistent accounts, with comparable response timing, as OWASP advises. Internally, keep enough outcome detail to distinguish delivery trouble, abuse controls, expired attempts, and unsuccessful verification.

Public ambiguity can coexist with useful diagnostics.

Buying message delivery or a ticket queue changes the team's operational workload. It doesn't resolve which evidence permits an account takeover. The buy-versus-build decision should therefore separate transport from authority, especially when a platform team is trying to protect its roadmap from an expanding support integration.

The table keeps a transport delivery receipt and a ticket status from becoming accidental authorization signals. A support integration should submit a scoped decision through the recovery service, where policy, expiry, and consumption are enforced. An agent clicking “resolved” should have no independent credential-changing power.

Capacity planning starts with the exception queue. Let the arrival rate be eligible support cases per hour, and multiply it by average active handling time in hours to estimate the continuously busy reviewer capacity required before headroom. This is a planning relationship, not a staffing benchmark. Recontacts, shift coverage, fraud review, and bursts around dispatch handovers can all increase the actual requirement. If the preferred channel becomes unavailable, the fallback queue can receive demand that normal staffing never intended to absorb.

For an illustrative calculation, 12 cases per hour at 20 minutes of active handling each consume 4 reviewer-hours per hour. Those are hypothetical inputs, not measured results or a staffing recommendation. Four reviewers would leave no spare capacity under that arithmetic (before breaks, rework, or arrival variability).

Set separate service objectives for accepting a recovery request, completing eligible automated recovery, and resolving reviewed cases. Don't use one blended completion SLO: a healthy automated majority can hide a stranded support minority. Choose numerical targets from shipment operations and measured queue behavior rather than inventing a universal availability promise. Include duplicate attempts and abandoned reviews in the operational view so that a superficially fast queue doesn't conceal unresolved lockouts.

The release gate should exercise the trust boundary with adversarial cases, not merely confirm that an email arrives. Test an attacker attempting to enroll a new destination from an old session, a consumed authorization submitted again, a challenge completed after expiry, and a reviewer attempting to approve a case outside their scope. Include concurrent reset submissions and protected requests made with sessions issued before recovery.

A proposed logistics test fixture can use a synthetic dispatcher account assigned to a synthetic shipment, with no customer documents copied into the test environment. First recover through the preenrolled channel and verify that the credential changes once, old sessions lose authority, and ordinary login is required. Then exercise a support case that lacks acceptable evidence: the expected result is a documented refusal or further review, not a reset merely because the queue's response-time target is approaching.

Record an audit event for the recovery request, evidence decision, credential-change outcome, and session-revocation outcome. Bind them with the attempt identifier and include the policy version and responsible service or reviewer. Avoid recording passwords, raw reset tokens, or unnecessary ticket attachments. A useful audit trail explains why access changed; a large collection of sensitive material merely increases what the team must protect. OWASP's logging guidance supports recording authentication and authorization events while excluding authentication secrets.

Roll out by limiting eligibility to accounts with established recovery enrollment and checking completion, rejection, replay, and revocation outcomes before expanding. Monitor the second-channel path and support queue separately. Delivery acknowledgments are a transport signal; a completed credential change is an application outcome. The distinction determines which operator can actually fix a stalled recovery.

If a release makes recovery unreliable, pause the affected entry path, stop issuing new reset authorizations through it, and preserve already committed credential changes and revocations. Route exceptions only to a support process that has the evidence policy and reviewer capacity to handle them. Otherwise, clearly defer recovery rather than lower its proof requirements to improve a dashboard.

Rollback testing should verify that an older application version still rejects consumed authorizations and honors the session-revocation state written by the newer version. Treat compatibility of that security state as a deployment prerequisite. Reverting interface code must not resurrect a credential or session that the recovery transaction deliberately invalidated.

Choose the preenrolled second channel when it provides adequate assurance and the user can still access it. Choose reviewed support when that evidence is unavailable or disputed, accepting longer recovery time in exchange for a controlled exception decision. For logistics accounts, the practical boundary is permission to resume consequential work: recovery should restore that permission only after the credential and session controls agree that the transition is complete.
