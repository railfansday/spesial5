Should I Come Out of Retirement?

More than 60% of respondents in a 2026 Goldman Sachs survey of 5,106 working and retired Americans said they had taken work outside their primary job, most out of necessity rather than a wish for extra spending money. Nearly 70% had delayed a major financial goal.

Retirement is increasingly becoming a touchy topic for many, especially those of us who are used to a certain level of stability.

The news only makes matters worse. On Friday, October 30, we saw Nasdaq’s biggest 1-day drop since June 5, setting the internet ablaze with speculation. The index closed 7.8% below its October 6 record, though it was noted that the S&P 500 was only a few percent below its own record.

Then there’s inflation. Annual inflation reached 3.7% in September, up from 3.4% in August. Pair that with continuing international conflicts on several fronts, and you’ve got the perfect storm of insecurity.

A National Institute on Retirement Security survey found that 80% of Americans say that the nation faces a retirement crisis and 61% worry they won’t be financially secure in retirement.

The respondents blamed inflation (73%) and market volatility (62%) as contributing factors, and 76% feared Social Security cuts if Congress fails to act.

A New York Life survey of more than 2,000 adults found that 34% had already delayed retirement or planned to, and 46% of them expected to push it back 5 years or more. Their top reasons were a lack of savings (46%) and inflation (45%).

Those who have already retired aren’t immune to these insecurities. The Goldman Sachs sample included 1,494 retirees aged 45 to 75. A market drop may also hurt more when you’re drawing from a portfolio than when you’re adding to it.

So, wondering if you should go back to work feels like a warranted question. Plenty of retirees take the leap back. Doctors go back too, though plenty of them say they don’t miss it. In a recent r/emergencymedicine thread, retired ED physicians were split on the question.

One, 3 years out, said he doesn’t miss it at all and still has the occasional nightmare. Another quit at 61 with no plan, misses the people and belonging to the group and keeps looking for work without luck.

1.5 million retirees reentered the workforce by March 2022.

We spend decades planning for the day we stop working. Then some of us stop, and a year or 2 later we’re checking job boards or answering calls from our old group. If that sounds like you, this article is for you.

Let’s take a look at the reasons many doctors feel the urge to un-retire, what going back to work in 2026 entails and the logistics of it all.

Money is the top reason many retirees choose to go back, so let’s start there. A recent survey found that 48% of working retirees said they needed the income, while 45% said they worked for social and emotional benefits.

Divide 1 year of spending by your portfolio. An example in T. Rowe Price’s example illustrates a 62-year-old with $900,000 who spends $65,000 a year.

That’s a 7.2% withdrawal rate. She has a 64% chance of not outliving her money if she retires at 62, a 91% chance at 65 and a 97% chance at 67.

Now let’s imagine a physician-specific version.

Say you have $2,500,000 and spend $150,000 a year before taxes. That’s a 6% withdrawal rate. A part-time job paying $60,000 would trim your withdrawals to $90,000, which is 3.6%.

You lower your rate by 2.4 percentage points without changing how you live. Of course, taxes will reduce that paycheck so ask your planner for a clearer outline.

One retired doctor said he saved about 30% of his take-home pay in his last years of full-time work, and his spending ended up around half of his colleagues’.

He could walk away any day, and his group knew it. That kind of freedom is helpful even after you return because you’re able to negotiate better when you don’t actually need the job.

Before you take a job, figure out whether the problem is your portfolio or your spending. If your withdrawal rate looks fine but spending still makes you uneasy, a concrete spending plan may help more than a paycheck. Ask your planner to set a monthly amount you can spend without second-guessing.

Working longer also helps in 2 other ways. You can delay account withdrawals as well as Social Security. Benefits grow 8% for every year you wait past full retirement age until you reach 70, while claiming at 62 cuts them by almost 30%.

Continuing to work also gives you more time to contribute to your retirement accounts. If your new employer offers a 401(k) or 403(b), the 2026 limit is $24,500, plus an $8,000 catch-up at 50 and older or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63.

It depends on whether you’ve claimed it and how old you are. If you haven’t claimed, going back to work is straightforward and your eventual monthly SS check will continue to increase.

If you claimed before full retirement age (FRA), the earnings test applies. In 2026, Social Security withholds $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 if you’re under FRA all year.

In the year you reach FRA, the limit rises to $65,160, and the agency withholds $1 for every $3 above it. Only earnings before the month you reach FRA count.

If your part-time schedule pays more than $24,480, expect withholding. The money isn’t gone for good, though. Social Security recalculates your benefit at FRA to credit the months it withheld.

Only wages and self-employment earnings count, so investment income doesn’t trigger withholding. Once you reach FRA, working doesn’t reduce your benefit.

You’ll also keep paying Social Security taxes on your paychecks. Higher earnings can also make more of your benefit taxable, so ask your tax preparer to estimate that too.

What if you changed your mind after claiming early? If you claimed before FRA, you have 12 months to withdraw your application. You must repay what you received, and Social Security then treats your record as if you never claimed at all.

Coverage before 65 costs far more in 2026 than it did in 2025 since the enhanced ACA subsidies expired on December 31, 2025.

KFF reports that a 60-year-old with a $65,000 income now pays an average of $10,389 more per year, or $865 per month. The subsidy cliff is also back which means above 400% of the poverty level (which is $62,600 for a single person in 2026), you lose the premium tax credit entirely.

On top of that, the ACA allows insurers to charge adults in their 60s up to 3 times what they charge adults in their 20s.

So a job with group health coverage may save you thousands of dollars a year because it skips the marketplace and the income cliff.

A working physician said that if he left medicine early, he’d take a flexible management job of about 25 hours a week mostly for the group insurance and the 401(k) match.

If you’re considering the same, ask HR what hours are required for benefits and when coverage starts, since each plan sets its own cutoff.

Medicare changes much of the picture. If you delayed Part B because you had coverage through your own or your spouse’s current job, you get an 8-month special enrollment period after that job or coverage ends.

If you miss it, you’ll have to pay 10% more for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B, for as long as you keep it. COBRA and retiree coverage don’t count for that exemption.

Keep in mind that Medicare generally pays first if the employer has fewer than 20 employees. So a small practice’s plan may pay second and you’d still need Part B.

Don’t count on getting your old job back.

An AARP survey of more than 3,500 people ages 50 and older found that 74% believe their age will be a barrier to getting hired, and 64% of workers 50 and older have seen or experienced age discrimination at work. That stigma is even worse for women.

Another AARP report found that 37% of workers 50 and older experienced subtle age discrimination during a job search in 2025, up from 30% in 2024. These surveys cover all fields, not only medicine, so ask recruiters in your specialty what they are seeing firsthand.

Think about what you want the next job to be. 24% of workers 50 and older planned a job change in 2025, so you won’t be the only one looking. If your retirement was driven mostly due to clinical work, teaching, consulting or chart review may suit you better than your old schedule.

We’ve already established that money drives about half of all unretirements. Boredom and a wish to help others drive many of the rest.

In an AARP survey, 18% of unretirees said boredom or a desire to stay active was their main reason for returning to work. For doctors, these sentiments start even before they leave.

In a Jackson Physician Search survey, 44% of physicians feared losing their sense of purpose in retirement and 39% feared boredom (money was still the top fear at 53%).

In CHG Healthcare’s 2024 survey, physicians who wanted to keep working felt that way because of a love of medicine (58%), social connections (57%) and concern for patients (39%). Others stated not feeling emotionally ready (47%), wanting to keep their lifestyle (35%) and fearing boredom or isolation (33%).

In Medscape’s 2025 retirement report, the top reason physicians gave for not retiring earlier was feeling they still had more to offer (62%), ahead of not having enough saved (45%). In the 2023 report, 46% expected retirement to feel like losing a part of their identity.

Retired and soon-to-retire ED physicians on Reddit offer a mixed bag of insights. One commenter who was 4 months from retiring expects to miss the ego boost of being a doctor. The one who quit at 61 with no plan misses colleagues and most of his patients, though not the fatigue of rotating shifts or the dread of sending home someone who should have stayed. Several said sleep and a normal daytime rhythm were the biggest change.

Retirement also changes what stresses you out. A 2025 Mayo Clinic review of physician well-being in retirement says that stress moves from work problems to personal ones, including professional identity, family, health and the loss of relationships.

The journal’s late-career paper urges physicians to plan for social and emotional needs along with finances. If you can’t picture your days without the work and the people, look at part-time options before you retire fully.

Give your first year in retirement a fair shake before you decide. People who leave long, consuming careers often describe a rocky start to retirement.

One user on Reddit said that it took about a year to stop feeling guilty about doing nothing. Another lasted 2 years, took a part-time job and has stayed 10 years. A third said the new job mattered to them mostly for the community, since all their friends still worked.

If burnout pushed you out in the first place, the same job will push you out again. In 2026, burnout in healthcare is still running rampant.

Doximity’s May 2026 poll found that 82% of physicians say they’re overworked and 46% are considering early retirement because of it.

That number is up from 37% in 2025 and 21% in 2021. The jump is sharpest among women (54% in 2026 compared to 33% in 2023) and physicians ages 50 to 54 (56%, up from 35%).

Medscape’s 2026 burnout report corroborates this. 50% of male and 51% of female physicians say that their work causes their burnout, and 13%–14% are considering leaving medicine altogether.

In a MedCentral survey, 44% of physicians said they had considered leaving medical practice since the start of the year.

A recent AMA study in The Permanente Journal looked at doctors who had already left and found that 971 clinically inactive physicians were typically 48.1 years old when they left clinical practice. Their top reasons were the hassle factor (44.7%), stress (44.5%), unrealistic patient demands (41.1%) and lack of professional satisfaction (38.4%).

Most doctors don’t plan to go cold turkey, however. In CHG Healthcare’s late-career survey, 63% of respondents said they’d rather work less than not work at all and 94% had at least slight interest in locum tenens work.

26% of physicians from a Weatherby Healthcare survey say that they are interested in locum tenens work. That’s 7 points higher than last year and the highest in 10 years.

If the actual reason behind your retirement still rings true or any of the reasons stated above give you a pause, you may want to reconsider jumping back into the workforce. Unless financial insecurity is twisting your arm, there’s no point in going back to something that already burned you out.

Fortunately, there are many other work options for physicians that don’t involve selling your soul. Locum tenens and expert witness work are popular ways for physicians to keep working.

Rules vary by state so check what’s needed where you live. Florida requires a reactivation fee, proof of continuing medical education (CME) for each year the license was inactive or retired, a health history and a financial responsibility form.

Pennsylvania requires full Board review and possibly proof of competency. According to the AAMC some states require a reentry program after an absence that averages about 3 years.

The ED physician who quit at 61 let his board certification lapse and said he now wishes he’d kept it, because it might have opened insurance work.

Another retired ED doctor commented that he dreamed for 2 years about working shifts without a license or malpractice cover. The roles you want to work in may or may not require these credentials so check before you make up your mind.

Most carriers give retiring physicians tail coverage free or at low cost. But some carriers may cancel that tail or charge a premium if you resume practice because when you return to clinical practice and receive pay or compensation for clinical work, you violate the retirement condition under which the tail fee was waived. Check with your provider.

The AMA warns that some physicians’ retirement income may change if they take paid work. Check your pension plan before you accept a role.

Of course, there’s no universal right answer and no rulebook that says you must make one permanent decision.

Start by being honest about why you feel the urge to return. If it’s your portfolio that’s got you worried, earning an income may give you some much-needed breathing room.

If you miss the people, intellectual challenge or sense of purpose, you might find what you’re looking for in a very different role.

But if you’re simply uncomfortable with having more time than you’ve ever had before, give yourself a little longer before dusting off your resume.

Most importantly, don’t let a bad week in the market convince you that decades of retirement planning have failed. Nor should you feel obliged to return to medicine because you miss being a physician. It’s okay to miss parts of your career without wanting the whole thing back.

And no matter what you decide, don’t let your credentials lapse. Your medical license and board certification represent years of hard work and keeping them active preserves options you may not realize you’ll want later.

Even if you’re 100% certain you’ll never practice again, give your future self the benefit of the doubt. You don’t have to use those credentials, but you might one day wish you still had them.
