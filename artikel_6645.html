Why Fashion Skipped COP31

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Fashion’s presence at the United Nations Conference of the Parties (COP) — a global gathering of over 90,000 delegates to review climate progress — is shrinking.

In 2021, when COP26 was held in Glasgow, fashion made a splash. The likes of Stella McCartney, then-H&M CEO Helena Helmersson, and then-Puma CEO Bjørn Gulden took to the stage as part of a major event celebrating the UN Fashion Industry Charter for Climate Action’s more ambitious commitment to cut greenhouse gas emissions by 50% by 2030. Meanwhile, King Charles (then Prince of Wales) attended McCartney’s Future of Fashion exhibition, featuring material innovations like Bolt Threads’ now defunct leather alternative, Mylo, and Evrnu’s recycled cotton fibre, Nucycl.

Previous editions of COP featured a significant fashion presence from designers like Stella McCartney, pictured here with Prince Charles at COP26 in 2021.

Five years later, fashion’s presence at this year’s COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, is significantly smaller. “There was silence in the air when it came to fashion and COP this year,” says Rachel Kitchin, Stand.Earth’s senior campaign manager, who previously attended COP in 2023 and 2024 and remembers fashion-centric side events, report launches and overall energy around fashion’s role in the event. Now, due to the scaled-back industry presence, the environmental profit’s fashion team decided it wasn’t a priority to attend, Kitchin says.

Part of this shrinking presence has to do with the UN Fashion Charter pulling back. The Charter — which has over 100 signatories including Kering, Adidas and Inditex — was first launched in 2018 at COP24 in Katowice, Poland, with the aim of ensuring fashion stays aligned with the Paris Agreement goal of limiting global warming to 1.5C. It hasn’t hosted an event at COP since 2024 in Baku, Azerbaijan, although its signatories did publish a communiqué, or open letter, to coincide with COP30 last year, reaffirming its “commitment to bold and collaborative climate leadership”. Despite launching two reports in August on how carbon pricing and financing mechanisms can help unlock capital for decarbonization, a key theme at COP31, the Charter decided not to convene fashion industry leaders at this year’s conference. “It’s pretty striking how quiet [the Fashion Charter has] been,” Kitchin continues.

Without a flagship event to rally around, much of fashion has decided to skip this year’s COP. The organizers of the handful of fashion panels that have taken place in Antalya noted how challenging it was to find participants. “There’s not a huge presence, specifically [from] brands,” says Carolina Fernández, international partnerships manager at The Waste and Resources Action Programme (WRAP), who hosted a Blue Zone session on the future of fashion and waste.

A panel hosted by Wrap focused on one of COP 31’s key themes, circularity.

In many ways, the lack of fashion leaders at COP31 reflects the rollback of sustainability commitments that has taken place within the industry over the past couple of years, as other pressing issues — such as tariffs and the challenging economic climate — have taken priority for brands, alongside an ESG backlash in the US triggered by President Donald Trump’s re-election in 2024. A report by the Apparel Impact Institute published in September found that the fashion industry’s greenhouse gas emissions increased by nearly 14% over 2023 and 2024, having declined by just over 1% in 2022.

As a result, there have been cuts to ESG budgets, leading to a reduction in both headcount and their ability to attend international events. In recent years, the profile of other sustainability events, such as New York Climate Week, has grown — with brands choosing to focus their efforts there instead. “The [COP] agenda is less directly advancing fashion’s agenda than more industry-focused conferences and forums,” says Nicole Rycroft, CEO of Canopy, who was on the ground in Antalya this year. “Everybody’s got crowded calendars, and if travel budgets are a little tighter, folks have been prioritizing going to more industry-specific forums, where conversations with peers are a bit more guaranteed.”

Nicole Rycroft, founder and executive director of Canopy, presenting at TED Countdown House for COP31.

Nevertheless, COP still offers the opportunity for fashion to engage more directly with political leaders and other sectors, at a time when policy is desperately needed in order for the industry to meet its climate goals. Even though fashion’s supply chain touches multiple industries, across multiple continents, it still receives “limited attention” in the wider climate conversation, Fernández says.

There have been limited interventions from fashion brands during COP, despite a reduced presence on the ground in Antalya. H&M Group joined 180 other companies, including Ikea, Volvo Cars and E.ON, in calling for governments to close the electrification implementation gap, as part of the We Mean Business coalition.

“While we’re not participating with a delegation this year, COP remains important for us, bringing together global stakeholders to push forward the crucial actions needed to mitigate climate change,” says Kim Hellström, H&M Group’s senior sustainability climate manager. “We believe it is important for policymakers to get a clear signal from the business community about the importance of electrification and the need for a global ambition on this topic.”

This year COP is being held in Antalya, Türkiye from November 9-20.

Zara owner Inditex, together with the World Bank’s International Finance Corporation (IFC) and impact investment firm Social Investment Managers & Advisors (SIMA Funds), also used the backdrop of COP to announce a new $400 million Decarbonization of Textile, Apparel, & Footwear Suppliers Fund (D-TAFS) last week, dedicated to decarbonizing the fashion supply chain. So far, the IFC and Inditex have invested $60 million and $50 million respectively, while other financial backers include the Apparel Impact Institute, British International Investment and the Global Environment Facility.

“Transforming supply chains requires the industry to work together and manufacturers to have access to both technical expertise and the financing needed to make these investments,” Inditex CEO Óscar García Maceiras said in a statement. “D-TAFS can help take the next step: turning already identified improvements into investments and measurable results, and enabling the transformation of the industry.”

Still, considering that the key themes of this year’s COP included electrification, climate adaptation and circularity, it feels like the lack of a strong fashion industry presence on the ground was a missed opportunity. “When you look through the list of COP priorities, it really is check, check, check in terms of central issues that the fashion industry is facing,” Kitchin says. It’s where the Fashion Charter could play a stronger role: “I would like to see climate adaptation and workers’ rights incorporated into the [wording of an updated commitment].”

The panel organised by Fair Labor Association, discussed health and labor issues for workers exposed to extreme heat.

Given that Türkiye is one of the world’s largest textile exporters, as well as one of the biggest cotton exporters, a greater presence from brands could have led to more engagement with the textile manufacturers and recyclers who were on the ground, including RE&UP, Loopifyr and Kipaş Textiles. Circularity featured prominently on the agenda, with the COP31 presidency proposing a new global target to increase the material reuse rate to at least 15%.

For the Fair Labor Association’s Chaimae Azmi, who hosted a panel on the impact of heat stress on garment workers at the World Health Organization (WHO) Pavilion in Antalya, COP provides a crucial forum for cross-sector conversations involving the fashion industry. “We need to put intersectionality into practice by breaking down silos and working across themes and sectors to protect workers and the planet,” she says. “We also hope this leads to better integration of labor and human rights into wider conversations on just transition and climate adaptation.”

Though fashion skipping this year’s COP may be symptomatic of how far sustainability has slipped down the agenda, the industry cannot wait until next year’s gathering in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, to reconvene and ramp up action, Kitchin says: “Ambition is slipping away and time is running out.”
