5 Fantasy LitRPG Books Better Than 'Dungeon Crawler Carl'

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

LitRPG is having a moment, and it’s largely thanks to Dungeon Crawler Carl. While Matt Dinniman's book series isn't the newest entry in the video game-inspired literary genre, it's arguably one of its most recognizable titles. It has attracted 14 million readers and is even getting a live-action TV adaptation on Peacock, produced by Universal Television and Seth MacFarlane's Fuzzy Door.

Dungeon Crawler Carl won readers over with its clueless protagonist, Carl, and his ex-girlfriend's pampered tortoiseshell cat, Princess Donut, as they struggle to survive the depths of the World Dungeon, an 18-level death game reminiscent of Squid Game. While the series is undoubtedly a fan favorite, there are plenty of other books that deserve just as much attention, if not more. From an isekai-inspired adventure to a mother fighting to keep her children alive during an invasion, here are fantasy LitRPG book series that are better than Dungeon Crawler Carl.

Readers who want something with no fluff and all grind will find that The Primal Hunter gets the job done. When the universe suddenly becomes part of a multiverse governed by a mysterious System, introverted business analyst Jake Thayne finds himself trapped in a deadly 63-day survival tutorial alongside his coworkers. Armed with a bow and an unexpected knack for killing, Jake quickly discovers that he's much better at combat than he ever imagined possible.

Dungeon Crawler Carl also has plenty of RPG mechanics, including levels, skills, equipment, quests, and achievements. However, these are tied to a televised dungeon competition, where rewards, audience engagement, and entertainment shape how the game unfolds. The Primal Hunter takes a more traditional MMORPG approach. Its System provides a framework for progression through levels, classes, skills, professions, and stats. Jake develops his own build, improves his abilities, and grows stronger to survive increasingly dangerous encounters.

While there are emotional arcs, The Primal Hunter doesn't spend too much time on them, keeping the storyline entirely focused on Jake's growth and combat mastery. Readers will find him spending most of his time either in wilderness zones or alchemy labs. As the story progresses, he faces higher-tier monsters, cosmic-level challenges, and plenty of new abilities to master. It's straightforward and no-nonsense, without requiring too much emotional commitment, making it perfect for a less demanding read overall.

Carl might have his chonky Princess Donut, but Apocalypse Parenting takes things to another level: its heroes are literally a mother and her three young children. Their chances of surviving the situation seem pretty slim, but never underestimate the sheer power of motherhood. When Earth is selected for an intergalactic game show, Meghan Moretti finds herself in survival mode with her children while her husband is away. Think these monsters will spare them? Think again. Little do they know, her own kids will have to learn how to defend themselves, too.

Taking a more survival-focused, character-driven approach to LitRPG, Apocalypse Parenting follows an ordinary mother doing everything she can to keep her three children alive. Rather than focusing solely on fighting monsters or collecting rewards, its RPG mechanics revolve around practical survival skills. Meghan isn't just looking to become stronger for the next battle; she's thinking about securing drinking water by plugging bathtub drains, managing household resources, and preparing her family for the dangers ahead. These everyday decisions become essential to their survival.

Despite its alien sci-fi premise, the series uses its grounded family focus to create a surprising amount of claustrophobic, A Quiet Place-like tension. There's no grand quest to pursue or ultimate villain to defeat; Meghan's main priority is keeping her home and family intact. And when you're a parent, that kind of pressure hits a little closer to home.

The Wandering Inn is perfect for readers looking for something a little simpler and cozier than the grand adventures LitRPG is often known for. Erin Solstice is an ordinary woman from Earth who somehow finds herself transported to a fantasy world. Rather than searching for a way home, she takes refuge in an abandoned inn and eventually becomes its Innkeeper. Instead of pursuing quests or aspiring to become a hero, she focuses on turning the place into a humble home.

In typical LitRPG fashion, skill progression is often tied to killing monsters, completing quests, or finding treasures. Not in The Wandering Inn. Here, characters level up through everyday activities like cleaning, cooking, and even finding menstrual supplies, which can be surprisingly difficult when the creatures around them are unfamiliar with the concept of periods.

However, don't let its cozy, slice-of-life premise fool you. While Erin serves as the story's anchor, the series introduces hundreds of other perspectives across multiple continents, opening the door to storylines involving military strategy, medical drama, and goblin survival. This creates quite a contrast with the quieter life established at the beginning. Readers should expect plenty of whiplash, with monster attacks and wars threatening to destroy the inn just chapters after Erin has been baking bread or playing chess.

Carl might be snarky in Dungeon Crawler Carl, but Jason has even more bite thanks to his sharp tongue and sarcastic attitude in He Who Fights with Monsters. In an isekai-like twist, Jason Asano's life turns upside down when he wakes up naked in a monster-infested hedge maze, where magic and gods exist alongside video game-like mechanics. After surviving a blood cult, stopping an apocalypse-class monster, and discovering his own magical abilities, Jason eventually joins the Adventurers' Society.

Although Dungeon Crawler Carl also incorporates game-like mechanics, He Who Fights with Monsters offers a much more extensive RPG framework. Its detailed character sheets establish measurable attributes, abilities, and progression requirements, giving the fantasy world a clearly defined structure. More importantly, these mechanics are interconnected. Completing quests, defeating monsters, and collecting resources all contribute to character development, making the RPG system feel like a character in its own right. It's a great choice for readers who enjoy the more technical side of LitRPG.

Storytelling-wise, He Who Fights with Monsters takes a more philosophical approach, which makes sense given that its title comes from a Friedrich Nietzsche quote. What begins as a survival story gradually becomes a journey of self-discovery that explores Nietzsche's monster paradox. Jason must learn to fight corrupt gods and powerful elites, but the real question is whether he can do so without becoming just as monstrous as the enemies he opposes.

Alden Thorn has always wanted to be a superhero, but he's not exactly interested in punching monsters in Super Supportive. He'd rather be the guy keeping everyone else alive. That's not an easy dream in a world where an alien System decides who gets magical abilities and what powers they receive. After becoming one of Earth's Avowed, Alden gets his chance, but he'll have to figure out how to make his unusual support abilities work in a world that often values fighters more than healers.

Unlike Dungeon Crawler Carl, Super Supportive doesn't treat its RPG mechanics like a game people happen to be playing. Much of the fun comes from watching Aldren experiment with his abilities rather than simply chase higher stats. He starts with the Meister of Cudgel class but eventually trades it for Chainer, giving him access to powerful wordchains.

Sleyca also takes her time with Alden's character development, giving readers all the more reason to root for the protagonist. Between school, personal struggles, and training on Anesidora, there's plenty happening outside combat. The story also explores alien cultures, interspecies politics, and Earth's complicated relationship with the Artonans. It's a slower, more character-driven LitRPG, but readers who enjoy watching a protagonist gradually figure out his powers will find plenty to get invested in.
