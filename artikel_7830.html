Maintaining healthy indoor air quality is essential for protecting members of your household from hidden carcinogens, allergens, and harmful particulates. Pollutants can enter your living space from a range of sources, including building materials that emit volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and poor ventilation that traps moisture and encourages mold growth.

While clues like window condensation, musty odors, or unexplained respiratory symptoms can signal trouble, invisible, odorless threats such as radon and carbon monoxide often go undetected without the right equipment. We spoke to indoor air quality experts to identify the top five indoor air pollutants in U.S. homes and how to fix or mitigate them.

Particulate matter—including tiny airborne particles like pollen, smoke, soot, and other debris—is one of the most common indoor air pollutants, says Dr. Jaspreet Dhau, an expert on indoor air quality and purification and vice president of research and development at Molekule in Palm Beach Gardens, Florida. “Fine particulate matter, particularly PM2.5, which is a particle measuring 2.5 microns [micrometers] or smaller, is a concern because it can travel deep into the respiratory system,” says Dhau. Common sources include cooking, burning candles, using a fireplace, vacuuming, and even opening windows when outdoor pollution levels are high.

VOCs are gases released by certain household products and materials such as paint, solvents, flooring, furniture, adhesives, cleaning supplies, air fresheners, and personal care products. “VOC levels may be higher during and after a home renovation, when new furniture is brought into a home, or when products containing chemical ingredients are used without adequate ventilation,” says Dhau. For example, certain pressed wood furniture and building materials can release formaldehyde, he explains.

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a combustion byproduct, a pollutant released when something burns. “In a typical home, sources can include gas stoves, fireplaces, wood-burning stoves, furnaces, water heaters, candles, and tobacco products,” says Dhau. “Carbon monoxide is especially dangerous because it’s colorless and odorless, making it difficult to detect without a working CO alarm.”

Biological contaminants such as dust mites and pet dander are airborne pollutants that originate from living organisms, says Dhau. “Dust mites are a frequent issue and tend to collect in mattresses, carpets, upholstered furniture, and bedding. They can enter homes through open windows, pets, clothing, and HVAC systems,” he explains. Signs you might have a problem include frequent dust buildup on surfaces, allergy symptoms, excess pet hair and dander, and dust accumulating around air vents, adds Bill Myers, indoor air quality expert and group manager for technical sales at Panasonic Eco Systems North America.

Everyday activities such as cooking and showering introduce moisture into the home. “When excess moisture is not adequately removed, particularly in bathrooms, basements, and other low-ventilation areas, conditions can become more favorable for mold and mildew growth,” notes Meyers. You might notice foggy mirrors that take a long time to clear, condensation on windows, musty odors, bathrooms that remain damp long after use, and visible mold or mildew.
