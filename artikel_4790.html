The Correlation Between Sports and Suicide

The list of professional athletes who have killed themselves is long. It includes all-pro football players Junior Seau, Mike Webster, and Jim Tyrer; “Miracle on Ice” hockey star Mark Pavelich; Olympic beach volleyball medalist Mike Whitmarsh; Olympic freestyle skiing medalist Jeret Peterson; Olympic women’s cycling medalist Kelly Catlin; pro golfer Erica Blasberg; Cy Young Award winner Mike Flanagan; Yankees pitcher Hideki Irabu; and Angels pitcher Donnie Moore.

One reason why athletes have a high risk of suicide is that people who have enjoyed adulation and success throughout their young lives sometimes have a hard time adjusting when their playing days end. The roar of the crowd is gone, the adrenaline rush that accompanies top-level competition is no longer there, big paychecks most likely have ceased, locker room camaraderie is absent, and trainers and agents aren’t around to provide round-the-clock personal care. Most athletes who leave the game are still young by professional standards, whereas others in their 30s have begun to establish them­selves in long-term careers; athletes are just starting out and may not be prepared for life after sports.

According to one university researcher, 77 former Major League Baseball players have killed themselves. More than half were between their late 20s and late 40s, when their absence from the game was the most pronounced and future plans were uncertain, leading the researcher to recommend that MLB players receive counseling after they retire.[1]

Another factor is that many professional athletes leave the game with beat-up bodies. They were in peak physical shape when they first started playing, but overuse of joints and muscles plus injuries have taken a toll, mentally as well as physically. It’s hard to say goodbye to the limelight, but even harder sometimes to adjust to life afterward—chronic pain, decreased mobility, and—if they have suffered concussions—possible and permanent brain impairment.

In recent years, the results of studies on CTE—brain degeneration caused by repeated head trauma—have raised troubling concerns. At this time, there isn’t an accurate diagnostic test for CTE in living brains—it can only be found postmortem—but Chris Nowinski, a behavioral bio-scientist who played football in college, says that 99 percent of former NFL players whose brains have been examined posthumously have had CTE, and more than a few have died by suicide.[2]

Horse racing presents its own challenges. First and most obviously, it’s extremely dangerous. According to the president and CEO of the Jockey’s Guild, two jockeys die each year, on average, from racing, and 60 others are paralyzed. The concussions that jockeys get from falling off a horse at high speeds are as violent as the collisions of football players, and jockeys wear less protective gear. Add to this the challenge of maintaining a low weight, which results in eating disorders, plus frequent criticism from owners, trainers, and bettors, and it’s not surprising that jockeys suffer a variety of mental health issues. Three years ago, the horse racing world was stunned by the suicides of two jockeys. One of them was a friend of Mike Smith, who won the Triple Crown in 2018 at the age of 52 and is in the Racing Hall of Fame. Smith said that he knew other riders who killed themselves. “This is not all of a sudden just happening,” he said. “It’s been going on. You just never heard of it.”[3]

To date, only one female athlete has been diagnosed with CTE—a former Australian rules football player who played seven games in 2017, retired later that year, and killed herself in 2022 at age 28. Her family donated her brain to the Australian Sports Brain Bank, and the director there reported that he found “multiple CTE lesions.”[4] Here in the United States, two former World Cup stars, Brandi Chastain and Michelle Akers, have expressed public concern over memory-loss issues after they retired, and said that they will participate in a CTE study at Boston University.

Players and former players aren’t the only people at risk of suicide, however. Studies show that when a city’s most popular sports teams win a national championship, there is a temporary decline in the suicide rate. Conversely, after a loss, the rate goes up.

The U.S. men’s hockey team’s 1980 Olympic victory over the Soviet Union—the so-called “Miracle on Ice”—came at a time when the Iran hostage crisis had gone on for 111 days, and Russia’s invasion of Afghanistan was a month old. Millions of people in America had never seen a hockey game, didn’t know the rules or the names of any of the players, yet were glued to their TV sets and radios, cheering the home team to victory. It’s not surprising that the U.S. suicide rate was unusually low that day, February 22, 1980, compared with other February 22nds in the 20 years before and 20 years after.[5] (It may have been unusually high in the Soviet Union, although there are no data available to verify this.)

Suicide expert Thomas Joiner, who conducted that study, has conducted other studies that show a link between the success or failure of a sports team and suicide. For example, he found that fewer suicides occur on Sundays in America when the Super Bowl is played than on other Sundays during the year. Also, he determined that there is a correlation between the suicide rate and the national rankings of the football team at Florida State University, where Joiner is a tenured professor, and Ohio State University, which is a perennial football powerhouse. When the team was highly ranked, the suicide rate in the two cities—Gainesville and Columbus, respectively—was lower, Joiner found.[6]

Other studies support this. In one study of 30 metropolitan areas in the U.S. over 20 years, researchers noted that the suicide rate went down when the area’s pro team made the playoffs and went on to win a champion­ship.[7] Conversely, the defeat of a high-profile soccer team in England led to increased cases of self-poisoning, according to a different study.[8] In a third study, suicides of young men increased in Quebec when the Montreal Canadiens hockey team lost an early-round playoff series.[9]

People joke about “living and dying” with their favorite team, but the sense of belongingness that someone feels rooting for a team can be undone when that team loses and its season is over. It’s easy to say that no one should take sports so seriously, but as a fan myself, I’m aware of the lure.
