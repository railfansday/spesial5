Prediction Markets and Betting on Elections

Federal law prohibits online gambling platforms such as DraftKings or FanDuel from wagers on election outcomes. On the prediction markets of Kalshi and Polymarket, a person can buy an event contract on an election. This election cycle, nearly $1.9 billion has been traded on these markets.

Both online gambling platforms and prediction markets involve future events. What’s the difference between wagers and event contracts? That may be a distinction in search of a difference. Regardless, the effects of these platforms and markets on individuals, our elections, and democracy matter enormously.

With online betting, a person may place a wager on the outcome of a sports event, a card game, or just about anything else but an election. If a person has a winning bet, the online platform, a.k.a. the house, must pay out. The house has an interest in a particular outcome. To gamble legally online, a person must be at least 21 years old.

In a prediction market, participants buy event contracts about the outcome of future events, which might include sports, military operations, election outcomes, and when a celebrity couple will get married. Prediction markets set prices by probabilities. An event contract selling for $.25 that the local football team will win the day’s game implies there is a 25 percent likelihood of the team winning. Purchasing an event contract is a financial transaction and not a wager. Participants in a prediction market trade directly with the other participants. The market is neutral on the outcome. Kalshi charges a fee for each transaction, but they do not pay out like the house does with gambling. This is the crux of the argument that prediction markets are not a form of online betting. To buy event contracts, a person must be at least 18 years old.

Tomato, tomahto. I believe that prediction markets are online betting by another name. This form of online gambling poses significant risks to individuals and to our democracy. I have four concerns that weave together.

Nearly 75 percent of Kalshi’s event contracts involve sports. It is young men who are fueling the online betting and purchase of event contracts. The younger a person is when he starts betting, the greater the likelihood of developing a problem with gambling and experiencing gambling-related harm. This may begin with people visiting the platforms more often and spending increasing amounts of time there. Dynamic pricing in the prediction market makes that more likely. When new information is available, public sentiment shifts, or there are changes in risk tolerance, the price of the event contract shifts. These pricing changes keep bettors tethered to the platform and making trades with each other.

The price of an event contract is the result of artificial intelligence (AI) crunching massive amounts of data. Most bettors just look at the results of the crunching, follow those results, and trade their contracts repeatedly. Following along because AI has indicated probabilities is a new version of the bandwagon fallacy. Old-school bandwagon fallacy involves the assertion that a claim is true because the majority of people believe it is true, which prompts more people to hop on the bandwagon. AI now functions as the supermajority for most people, which makes individuals less willing and even able to seek their own evidence and argument.

For those young people who grew up playing video games, online gambling sites will be familiar as entertainment or excitement. Prediction markets allow people to bet on the outcomes of elections as if they were games. Free and fair elections are the backbone of a democracy. Now they are viewed as akin to a sporting event. The stakes are enormously high with elections; their consequences extend for years. Voting affords the opportunity for people to make their voices heard and their preferences known. Young people arguably have the greatest stake in elections because they will have to live with the consequences for longer periods of time. Choosing candidates and buying event contracts based on probabilities of their winning as opposed to their characters or their platforms is not genuine voter engagement.

Betting on elections becomes a matter of an individual’s personal gain from an election outcome. It telescopes a person’s interest in an election to the probability of that person winning a bet. Winning the bet becomes more important than the consequences of the election. I am not naïve to assume that personal interest hasn’t governed people’s voting patterns, but these bets radically change the calculus. A person’s vote will follow the bet. This makes it less likely that people would vote for something beyond their self-interest, such as the well-being of others or the common good.

Prediction markets can harm individuals, the common good, and even democracy itself.
