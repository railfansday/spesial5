Beware of Rehab Recruitment Scams

Imagine you’ve been struggling with alcohol addiction. Every waking moment is spent either drinking or thinking about drinking. You don’t even want to drink anymore, but you just keep doing it. In a rare moment of lucidity, you decide it’s time to get help. This opportunity for help may come only once. Your life depends on getting genuine help right now.

But what if the person who answers the phone when you call is a salesperson, not a qualified admissions agent, and gets paid for every person they send to a specific program? What if the center offering the highest kickback this month provides a low quality of care or cuts you loose too soon? What if your family has spent their life savings to get you this care?

Situations like this are troublingly common. What’s behind them, and how can patients and their families avoid being scammed?

The following composite scenario lays out many of the practices involved in this kind of impersonation and the patient brokering that often follows.

A woman—we’ll call her Melissa—decides to seek help for opioid addiction. She googles a trusted treatment center in her town—we’ll call it Hometown Recovery—and dials the number that appears at the top of the page.

When an agent answers, Melissa breathes a sigh of relief. She believes she’s talking with someone at Hometown. The agent neither confirms nor denies Melissa’s assumption and asks what kind of insurance she has. If Melissa had Medicaid, that would likely be the end of the call. If she had private insurance, the agent would recommend a specific program “based on her insurance.”

But let’s say Melissa doesn’t have insurance at all. The agent offers to get insurance for her, for free. Melissa suspects something is off. She asks, “Is this Hometown Recovery?” The agent says, “We work with them. I can get you there,” and keeps asking questions. Melissa presses, and after she asks several times, the exasperated agent says, “Yes.”

Melissa accepts the offer for free insurance and is admitted to a program 600 miles away. She’s on a plane the next day and arrives just as withdrawal symptoms begin. She enters the center’s detox unit immediately.

In four days, she is thinking a little clearer. She wonders how she ended up here when she wanted to go to Hometown Recovery. She begins asking questions and ultimately says she wants to leave. Staff members respond that she needs to stay in detox for seven days because that’s what her insurance specified.

She agrees to stay, and at the end of seven days, she enters the center’s primary treatment program. She’s three weeks into residential care when the insurance company realizes her policy is fraudulent and cancels it. The profit-driven treatment center terminates her care. Melissa is far from home with no money, nowhere to go, and no way to get home.

Meanwhile, Hometown Recovery never heard from her. If Melissa has relatives back home who can help, she may be able to get there. But if—like so many people with her circumstances—she doesn’t have anyone, she is now homeless in a strange city after receiving partial treatment.

She took the brave step of asking for help, and she’s been robbed of an opportunity to heal. The call center hijacked her recovery, and this could prove fatal for Melissa.

Impersonation, in the business sense, involves one entity advertising or presenting as another. It’s often a way for unscrupulous businesses to capitalize on the good reputation of more established ones. In the treatment world, some independent or treatment-center-owned call centers buy ads using other centers’ exact names.

When Melissa googled her local treatment center, she saw an ad that read, “Hometown Recovery? Call Us Now!” But the call center she reached had purchased ads featuring “Hometown Recovery” as a keyword. This is an impersonation scam, and it’s illegal.

Perhaps worse than ad scams, some call centers impersonate free addiction helplines—someone trustworthy to talk to, confide in. And, like the search ad scammers, they recruit vulnerable people, either into the facility that operates the call center or the one paying the highest kickback. If you’re starting to feel as though these call centers are treating humans as a commodity, you’re not alone.

It’s truly appalling, but patient brokering (or body brokering) is when agencies or individuals receive kickbacks for funneling patients to specific treatment centers. So, instead of a person whose life is at stake being matched to the program that can best care for them, they’re turned over to the highest bidder.

Call centers are one patient brokering channel. But even police, people working in recovery, or those involved in meeting-based programs may receive kickbacks for recruitment. Likewise, some people who do street outreach—even very publicly on social media—are paid by treatment centers to bring in patients. Social media itself and websites disguised as general help are also common brokering channels.

Whether and how an influencer, agency, or trusted professional partners with those centers is scrutinized by a complex network of laws and regulations.

What happened to Melissa likely violated several federal laws and regulations, as well as some state statutes, depending upon jurisdiction. And as you might expect, the most meticulous impersonators and brokers will operate in places where state laws are more favorable, or enforcement is low.

Federally, FTC truth-in-advertising laws apply to impersonation, and health care fraud statutes are commonly used to prosecute brokering. Both practices are getting more attention recently at the state level as well, and brokering is now a felony in a few states, including North Carolina, Florida, and Arizona.

With sentences ranging from probation to four years at the high end, it’s clear that brokering laws don’t have the teeth that they should, which is possibly one reason prosecutors seem to prefer fraud charges. Given the danger it can place vulnerable people in, including some documented cases of overdose death following brokered placements, consequences are insufficient to say the least. Some advocates and survivors make a compelling argument that brokering is indistinguishable from human trafficking.

With brokers potentially lurking everywhere, they may feel impossible to avoid. And the last thing we want to do is make patients or their families feel wary of every encounter—not trusting the whole industry could present a major barrier to receiving deeply needed care.
