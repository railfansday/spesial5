From our personal relationships to our professional lives, trust impacts how we view others and experience the world around us. Addiction can make trust particularly difficult; it may leave loved ones questioning whether they can rely on someone's word. However, being trusted is often confused with the simpler idea of being believed.

To explore this further, I spoke with Amber Hollingsworth, a licensed addiction counselor and certified provider at Soberlink with more than 20 years of experience. She provides insight on how honesty, consistency, and accountability help rebuild trust, particularly in relationships affected by addiction.

Someone may believe what you tell them in a particular moment but still question whether they can rely on you over time. For example, a spouse may believe their partner is committed to recovery but still struggle to trust that things will be different this time. Past experiences can make it difficult to feel secure, even when their partner is making progress during their recovery.

Trust can be looked at as what you feel comfortable counting on others to do. Belief is a cognitive process; when someone believes you, they may accept that a specific fact or story you have shared is true, often based on evidence or a well-supported explanation. Trust, on the other hand, is an emotional process because it involves making yourself vulnerable to another person based on your confidence in their character.

A 2025 study found that consistently demonstrating trustworthy behavior was the strongest factor in whether people learned to trust others. According to Hollingsworth, “Apologies can change minds for a moment, but consistency can actually change nervous systems."

When trust becomes damaged, it can ultimately impact how people interact with each other. For instance, a spouse may begin to pay closer attention to behaviors and look for signs of alcohol use to confirm their suspicions. This can take a significant emotional toll as time goes on.

In this situation, accountability can be extremely useful. You need to build trust in many cases during the recovery process from addiction. This can be done by showing consistency, clear communication, and providing measurable evidence where appropriate. Amber expresses that, "Trust doesn’t happen just because you promise harder. It happens when you remove uncertainty.”

Rebuilding trust takes time, and is different for everyone. Entering recovery is an important step, but loved ones may need to see consistent changes before they feel comfortable relying on someone again.

A 2025 review examined the use of remote monitoring technologies, including smartphones, breathalyzers, and wearable devices, in alcohol use disorder treatment. These tools can help track alcohol use and inform treatment, but they cannot address the relational harm caused by dishonesty or broken promises. Rebuilding trust still requires honesty, open communication, and consistent behavior.

Hollingsworth emphasizes this importance and indicates: “Openness is so important in building trust, because it shows others that we aren’t trying to hide anything. Even if you’ve been struggling, being open about your successes and setbacks can help build trust with your loved ones. Being transparent shows your intent, and that can be just as important as your actions themselves. You earn belief with a story, but you earn trust with a system. Radical honesty, non-defensive listening, and verifiable accountability turn maybes into I knows.” Ultimately, rebuilding trust is about giving people reasons to feel confident in you again, whether in recovery, marriage, friendship, or the workplace.
