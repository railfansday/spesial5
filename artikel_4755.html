What If 'I Know' Is Our Biggest Limitation?

Posted November 16, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Mentally and physically, I was about as flexible as a broomstick. But I had promised myself that when I arrived in Seattle, I would try things that would get me out of my comfort zone—like yoga.

I was a physician with fairly rigid ideas about what made sense and what didn’t fit my worldview. I was also in the habit of always being two steps ahead, planning what came next. Yoga had other ideas. Standing on one leg left no room to plan whether the union of body, mind, and spirit made sense. All I could do was stay in the present moment and focus on one simple task: keeping my body from falling over.

Had I insisted on what I thought I already knew, I could easily have decided yoga wasn’t for me. Instead, I got curious. That curiosity led me to discover the enormous benefits of meditation, helped me make new friendships, deepened my spiritual connection, inspired me to change my career, and led me to meet my wife Danielle at a yoga retreat. I couldn’t have predicted any of it. Looking back, I simply let myself wonder and wander into the unknown.

We begin life curious. Babies reach for things just beyond their grasp, and children ask endless questions about how things work and why people behave the way they do. But as we grow older, we accumulate knowledge, experiences, and beliefs that help us navigate the world. And the more familiar things become, the less likely we are to question them. We start confusing what we know with what we believe, and what we believe with what is actually true.

We form fixed ideas about other people, about how life works, and especially about ourselves. Without realizing it, we turn our assumptions into invisible boundaries. Eventually, our need for certainty can become a surprisingly restrictive force in our lives.

We can travel to 50 countries, try 20 different hobbies, and still be rigid in our thinking. How often do you dismiss something before you give yourself a chance to explore it? You might avoid a new hobby because you’re convinced you won’t be good at it. You decide someone doesn’t like you based on how they looked at you or something they said. Or you accept your inner critic’s verdict that you are not good enough, that you cannot change, or that certain things aren’t possible for you. But are these conclusions facts, or are they interpretations you have never questioned? Genuine curiosity means being willing to question what we believe we already know, particularly about other people.

I recently heard a wonderful story about a woman called Grace, in her late 80s, who, much to her children’s surprise, decided to volunteer at a homeless shelter. She had held rather rigid opinions about people whose backgrounds and circumstances were very different from her own, and everyone expected her to give up after a few days.

Instead, Grace found her role. When new residents arrive, she guides them around, helping them pick up sheets, towels, and food, and walks with them for about an hour. Along the way, they tell her all about their lives. Then she says, “Honey, I love you,” and gives them a hug.

Today, the residents love her. And they are all coming to her birthday party.

Grace had walked in seeing people through the lens of stereotypes. By listening, she discovered their shared humanity and qualities that inspired her: resilience, tenacity, and kindness, even from people who had experienced very little kindness themselves. She told her daughter, “I thought I had something to offer the residents, but it turns out what they give me is so much more profound.”

But to become genuinely curious, we also need the humility to recognize that there is more we don't know than we know. The moment we accept "I know" as the final answer, we close ourselves off to other possibilities. Intellectual humility means being willing to question our own beliefs, acknowledge that we might be wrong, and consider perspectives that differ from our own (Porter et al., 2022).

This openness can also transform how we relate to others. Studies have found that curious people tend to respond less aggressively, even when provoked, and that asking follow-up questions and showing genuine interest makes us more likable to others (Kashdan et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2017). Similarly, a meta-analysis of 515 studies found that getting to know people from different social groups can help reduce prejudice and challenge stereotypes (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006).

Grace's experience at the shelter illustrates this beautifully. By spending time with people she might otherwise have judged from a distance, listening to their stories, and becoming genuinely interested in their lives, she discovered not only unexpected friendships but also qualities she deeply admired.

The next time you catch yourself judging others or yourself, feeling defensive, or becoming convinced that something cannot change, pause and ask:

Perhaps the greatest limitation in our lives isn’t what we don’t know, but what we believe we already know. Curiosity asks us to make peace with how much we don’t know, to let go of the need to have all the answers, and to be willing to say, “I might be wrong” or, simply, “I wonder what would happen if…”

That willingness took me from a yoga mat to a life I never could have planned.
