Helping Your Child Navigate Emotions When You're Overwhelmed

We’ve all been there. Your 4-year-old can only find one of their spaceman boots and is in total meltdown; your middle schooler begs not to go to school because they’re convinced they have no one to sit with at lunch; your teen has stormed off when you simply asked if they started their homework. Meanwhile, you are just trying to make it through the finish line of the day. Somehow, it’s not looking good.

Our overwhelm meets their distress. It’s a stressful emotional gridlock.

Our overwhelm often comes from feeling like we have to change course: Do an abrupt swerve to stop or fix what our kids are feeling. And of course, it’s not like our kids don’t want that, but it’s not like that’s all they want either. Witness how, when we make a beeline for solutions, kids often respond with: “You don’t understand! You’re just trying to fix it! You never listen to me!”

But rather than whisking them away from their feelings, our best move is to go toward their emotions with them.

What is the best way for kids to process their emotions? I have created a process I call the Seven Shifts, and it works for any age—parents and kids alike.

The Seven Shifts is a repeatable throughline for any emotional situation; it involves first honoring what you’re feeling so you can settle back into a more thoughtful (versus reactive) state, then toggling to what the feeling is pointing to: the need for us to do our good work—zoom in and understand what our thoughts are telling us, then zoom out to question interpretations and consider other possibilities that are more helpful and… true.

Shift 1: Empathize—don’t fight or fix the feeling; just make room for it

Notice how just saying to yourself: “I’m worried for my child, or even I’m frustrated and too tired, and that’s OK” allows your body to soften. You’re not fighting where you are. You aren’t saying you don’t care; your caring is not up for debate. You are validating your own experience—it’s a place where people land. Lots of people.

Same for your child: Instead of rushing to reassure or correct—get to what I call the “nodding head of agreement.” Your child sees that you see where they are: “This is really upsetting you. This is a lot you’re carrying. You really didn’t want it to be like this.” It’s not about agreeing with the logic; it’s accepting without judgment—this is where your child has landed.

Often in a blur of competing feelings, we feel lost—and when we feel lost, we spiral. The antidote? Just start naming the feelings—there may be several, and they may seem like opposites—like loving and frustrated. They are what I call “Emotion Neighbors”—opposites can sit side by side and not add up: feeling frustrated and sad, hopeful and exhausted.

Kids may not be able to name their feelings—ask: Does it feel mad, like something isn’t OK with you, or more hurt, like something is missing?

Shift 3: Settle inside—slow down and reset

The body registers all the stress of our thoughts. Tiny resets, a big exhale, a sigh, help you shift out of crisis mode and into connection. Bringing your calm to your child helps them find their calm, too. Sometimes it’s invisible—just your slow breathing helps them find their calm. A regulated nervous system is the foundation of everything that follows.

What are the thoughts underneath the feelings? When we can find those gems—not necessarily positive—they tell us what we’ve been telling ourselves; knowing the narrative we’re reading helps point to what we need to reinterpret. When thoughts operate freely under our awareness, we get stuck in spirals of fear, frustration, or helplessness. Ask your child: “What’s your brain telling you? What is the alarm, going beep-beep-beep, in your mind?”

Shift 5: Zoom out—prism it to see multiple perspectives

Now that we’ve unearthed those gems, hold them up to the light and get different perspectives—translation: the things we say to ourselves unchecked we’d never say to a friend in a similar situation. We can fact-check our beliefs and edit accordingly and encourage our kids to do the same.

Help kids get in the habit of what I call “second-opinion thinking”: What would they tell a friend in that situation? What would a friend tell them? What would a hero or mentor say? Notice you’re not saying: “You’re thinking the wrong thing.” It’s an invitation: “Those thoughts sound like what anxiety says; can we fact-check it and see what else is more true?”

Shift 6: Take action one small step at a time

Equipped with a newer, truer story, is there a step you want to take that could help—like allowing yourself a “time out” and coming back to your child when you’ve had a second to reset? Or reminding yourself of your child’s strengths rather than just focusing on what’s wrong?

With your child, ask: “What’s a step that you could try—approaching a fearful situation? Saying something assertive to a friend? Scheduling grief time every day?”

Shift 7: Take stock and give credit where it is due!

We are the heroes of our lives, and our successes are in the tiny moments—notice them, and give credit where credit is due. Help them notice the progress they are making—and the way they made it—especially when things are hard. That’s how they will know how to value the steps along the way, not just the great moments across the finish line.

When we allow ourselves to turn toward—not away—from our emotions, the seven shifts become a two-for-one route toward understanding. Kids gradually learn the way from our responses and increasingly trust that they’ll come out with a better understanding of themselves and the world on the other side of the journey.

Here’s to better emotional navigation all around.
