'You Can See Everything': When Ambition Exceeds Ability

Posted November 5, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

One of the more fascinating conversations emerging from Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's new documentary, You Can See Everything, concerns the psychology of Elizabeth Holmes.

Is she autistic? Is she a psychopath? Is she deliberately presenting herself in a particular way to elicit sympathy? Does she genuinely believe what she's saying, or is she manipulating everyone around her?

As a clinical psychologist, I'm interested in these questions. But I'm not Elizabeth Holmes's psychologist. I haven't evaluated her, and I certainly can't diagnose her from a documentary, no matter how much access its filmmakers were granted.

What I can comment on is something that struck me as far more interesting than the question of diagnosis: What happens when our ambitions exceed our abilities? And perhaps more importantly, what happens when we mistake the strength of our convictions for evidence of our competence?

Throughout You Can See Everything, we catch glimpses of the philosophy that appears to animate Holmes's extraordinary ambitions. Her workspaces feature inspirational quotations, including those attributed to Winston Churchill, the British prime minister whose leadership helped guide Britain through World War II.

Churchill is an interesting figure for Holmes to admire.

For readers who need a quick history refresher, Churchill became Britain's prime minister in May 1940, at one of the most perilous moments of the Second World War. Nazi Germany was rapidly conquering Western Europe, France was on the brink of defeat, and Britain faced the possibility of invasion.

Churchill refused to capitulate. Through his speeches, strategic leadership, and determination to continue fighting, he helped sustain British resistance until the Allied powers ultimately defeated Nazi Germany in 1945.

He also possessed an extraordinary belief in his own importance. In 1906, decades before becoming prime minister, Churchill famously remarked: "We are all worms, but I do believe that I am a glow-worm." Churchill believed he was destined for greatness long before history proved him right.

In that sense, it's not difficult to see why someone like Elizabeth Holmes might find him inspirational. Both appeared to possess an almost unshakable conviction that they were meant to do something extraordinary.

But there is an important difference between Churchill's story and the one unfolding in You Can See Everything. Churchill didn't simply wake up one morning, decide he was destined to defeat the Nazis, and appoint himself an expert in military strategy. By the time he became prime minister in 1940, he had spent decades developing the knowledge and experience that would inform his wartime leadership. He graduated from the Royal Military College, served as a soldier and war correspondent, and entered Parliament in 1900, nearly 40 years before taking the helm of Britain during World War II.

Importantly, he also made serious mistakes. His association with the disastrous Gallipoli campaign during World War I damaged his political career. His judgment was frequently questioned, and he spent years outside the highest levels of political power. Those years gave him something that confidence alone never could: Experience. By the time Churchill confronted Nazi Germany as prime minister, he had accumulated nearly half a century of military, political, and leadership experience.

Which brings me back to Elizabeth Holmes and Billy Evans.

One of the more striking elements of You Can See Everything is watching the couple pursue a new ambitious scientific venture. We see Billy studying complex scientific concepts, including through educational videos played at accelerated speed, as he works toward developing another blood-testing technology.

Trying to understand something outside your area of expertise is good, but watching scientific videos at twice their normal speed doesn't confer twice the expertise. And two semesters at Stanford, in Holmes's case, do not make someone a scientist qualified to revolutionize medical diagnostics.

Psychology offers a useful framework for understanding this distinction. Consider the Dunning-Kruger effect, which describes how limited knowledge or skill can sometimes interfere with people's ability to recognize the limitations of their own performance.

That doesn't mean everyone who overestimates their abilities is experiencing the Dunning-Kruger effect, and it certainly isn't a diagnosis of anyone in this documentary. But the broader psychological lesson is important: We don't always know what we don't know.

Developing expertise requires more than accumulating information. It requires opportunities to discover where our understanding is incomplete, feedback from people who know more than we do, and the humility to acknowledge mistakes, revise our assumptions, and occasionally accept that the thing we desperately want to be true simply isn't.

In other words, becoming competent requires a willingness to be corrected. And that can be particularly difficult when we've built our identity around the belief that we're destined for greatness.

If you want to change the world, no amount of self-confidence or ambition will suffice without the experience and skill to go with it.
