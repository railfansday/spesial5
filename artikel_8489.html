How to Read Your Bank Statement – and Catch Fraud, Errors & Fees Every Month

Read your bank statement the week it arrives, since federal rules give you a 60-day window, starting when the bank sends it, to report a debit card purchase or other electronic transfer you didn’t make. Check that every charge is yours and every fee has a reason, and call the bank the day something looks wrong.

That 60-day window comes from Regulation E, the federal rule for electronic transfers such as debit card purchases, ATM withdrawals, and online transfers, though not paper checks. Miss it, and you can be stuck paying for unauthorized transfers made after day 60, ones the bank shows it could have stopped had you told it in time.

The statement does the math for you. Each one covers a stretch of dates called the statement period, usually a month. It starts with the beginning balance, what the account held on the first day, and adds deposits and other credits, the money that came in, such as paychecks, refunds, and interest. Then it subtracts withdrawals and other debits, the money that went out, such as card purchases, ATM cash, checks, and fees, and what’s left is the ending balance. Your part is checking each line against what you expected.

Report a problem by calling the phone number the statement lists for errors. Fees you expected still add up, and a year of statements shows what bank fees cost you.

The statement shows only what reached the bank, so balancing your checkbook is how you account for checks and payments that haven’t reached it yet.

Keep any statement that backs up a tax return for at least three years, the standard window in which the IRS can charge more tax, and any tied to a dispute until it’s settled.
