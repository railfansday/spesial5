Celluloid Sounds: Fewer Women Composers on Screen, But Some Hit the Right Notes

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

The stats for women composers in Hollywood have not improved. Just eight of this year’s top 100 grossing films feature original scores by women; the past two years have seen percentages between 7 and 9%, all down from 14% in 2023.

But female composers continue to be in the awards conversation, and not all for their music. The film is a timely look at four musical artists who lost priceless, beloved instruments in the Palisades and Eaton fires of January 2025: composer Starr Parodi, whose 1928 Steinway piano was heard in dozens of MGM scores including “The Wizard of Oz”; songwriter Diane Warren, who lost a Yamaha grand; jazz bassist John Clayton, whose string bass was nearly 200 years old; and Altadena educator Susie Chung, whose brown Steinway was played by Lady Gaga in “A Star Is Born” and on the 2019 Oscarcast.

“I believe my piano has a soul… I have a little regret that I left her alone,” Chung says in the film, and that sentiment is echoed by the other musicians. “Instruments have hearts, they have souls, they have lives,” says Warren, a 17-time best song nominee. Two days before the fires, Warren was filmed performing her Oscar-nominated song “The Journey” on the piano in her Malibu beach house.

Clayton’s bass, once owned by the legendary Ray Brown, had accompanied Ella Fitzgerald, Frank Sinatra, Nat King Cole and Duke Ellington. Recalling its past, he said “that bass and the music that it made was bigger than any of us could ever be.”

Parodi describes her lost keyboard as having “this beautiful, haunting, bell-like sound… warm and thick,” and she tells the harrowing story of watching her house go up in flames via their doorbell camera and later unearthing the remains of her MGM piano in the rubble.

Thousands of instruments were reduced to ash during the L.A. fires. “I felt it was a story that was really important to be told,” Karpman says. “When you live with these instruments, they have personalities, they see history.”

In addition to the interviews and performance footage, she added lively, colorful animation to the story. Animator Tempe Hale sought “to make the instruments come alive again, (to convey) the kind of fun and glory and energy that these instruments have.”

Karpman also composed the score for her film, incorporating both orchestral and choral sounds to suggest the music still being made, in the beyond, by those lost music makers.

Scottish composer Anna Meredith wrote one of the year’s most unusual scores for the romantic fantasy “Wicker,” which stars Olivia Colman as a fisherwoman and Alexander Skarsgård as her artificial husband, made for her by a basket weaver in their medieval village.

The entire score is played by just four people: a violist, a cellist, a recorder player and Meredith herself. “I’d much rather start small with a tiny lens, and see what you can do with it, rather than have every sound in the world, where it could just sound like any old thing. You have to really think creatively,” she says.

Directors Alex Huston Fischer and Eleanor Wilson reached out to Meredith during shooting (to write music for a wedding scene), and she quickly became part of a team that included several women in key roles (including editor Sofi Marshall). The composer decided that the timeframe and the nature of the story demanded wooden instruments for a “timeless quality.”

Meredith used as many as 10 different recorders, “from tiny little baby ones, only 20 centimeters; lots of ones in the conventional treble tenor range; and some bonkers instruments, including a bass one and a contrabass one which was so tall we had to move to a different recording studio. We had to find one with a skylight to fit the neck of that one, like seven feet tall.”

The recorders represented the fisherwoman and her wicker husband, while the strings (the darker sounds of viola and cello) represented the villagers. “It’s not nice string sounds,” she says, “it’s slithery, nasty sounds. Their sound worlds are, initially, kept quite different, and then the strings sort of ‘infect’ the recorders as the villagers start to impose on their relationship.”

The directors “wanted the score to be bold,” Meredith adds, “and it’s loud as well. The music is upfront. I’m very proud to be part of a film with a strong female story and so many important female elements, both on and off the screen, that have been part of it.”

For “Tenzing,” the untold story of the Nepalese-Indian Sherpa who accompanied Edmund Hillary to the top of Mount Everest in 1953, Norwegian-born, Stockholm-based composer Rebekka Karijord insisted on cultural authenticity. “I felt, early on, that we needed to have a collaboration with Nepalese musicians that isn’t like an exotic tone or a color,” she says. “It had to be a deep collaboration.”

She enlisted a Nepalese spiritual leader and Buddhist nun, Ani Choying Drolma, to contribute traditional Buddhist chants; Nepal’s premier flautist and throat singer Manose Singh; and actress Thinley Lhamo, who plays Tenzing’s wife Dawa in the film and is also a highly regarded singer of Tibetan opera.

Lhamo “has the most amazing voice,” Karijord says. That voice becomes, in a sense, the sound of the mountain itself. “I wanted to have something that could transcend the physical effort, that could pull us into the spiritual aspect, and her voice really solved that for me throughout the score.”

In addition, she had the sound team record a number of unique sounds from the region—yak bells, goat bells, the sound of prayer wheels in the temples, Tibetan singing bowls and gongs—which she and her percussionist sampled and added to her musical library for incorporation into the score.

The film details England’s drive to be the first nation to reach the summit of the world’s tallest peak, Karijord points out. “It needed the big cinematic muscles, a meeting between Western and Eastern. If I could make the mountain, this goddess, beautiful and pristine and dangerous and magnetizing, then we will understand why’re drawn to her—both Tenzing as a little boy growing up at the foot of the mountain, and these Englishmen wanting to conquer it.”

Karijord employed a 40-piece string section from the London Contemporary Orchestra, in part to convey the danger of crevasses, avalanches and rumbles in the ice, along with a handful of woodwind and brass players and a 12-voice choir. Textural elements, such as the sound of breathing, also became a factor in the score.

Regarding the few female composers scoring studio films, Karijord notes that “it’s such a deeply empathetic craft to score films, and it’s a deeply collaborative craft. I know a lot of women who are both very empathetic and collaborative and brave. So I think there should be more of us doing it.”
