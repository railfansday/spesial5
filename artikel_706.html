Rose Byrne on What Lured Her to ‘The Good Daughter’ and Why That Complicated Ending Wraps the Series

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The star and executive producer unpacks the thrilling Peacock mystery also starring Meghann Fahy and if what happens to the young girl at the center of it is fair.

[This story contains major spoilers from The Good Daughter finale.]

Rose Byrne keeps outdoing herself. Her explosive limited series The Good Daughter co-starring Meghann Fahy quickly became the top show on Peacock. Just last year, she grabbed a best actress Oscar nomination and won both the Golden Globe and Independent Spirits Award for If I Had Legs I’d Kick You — the film nobody saw coming. She also wrapped season two of her Apple TV comedy Platonic.

Even for an actress with a pedigree like Byrne’s spanning over 30 years, projects like The Good Daughter are rare opportunities, she shared last year while still filming in the Atlanta Metro area. “Sam is the sort of role written for a man,” the Australian actress confessed. “It’s very dominant, kind of self-possessed, and she has physical trauma. Like she has scars, and she has a history. It’s the kind of story that Byrne, who also serves as executive producer alongside fellow Aussie, Big Little Lies Emmy winner Bruna Papandrea, sees as bestselling author and fellow EP Karin Slaughter’s superpower. “She writes incredibly rich female characters who are complicated and complex, and not all good and not all bad, and who have a lot to work with,” Byrne continued.

For the first time ever, Slaughter, who has had other books grace the screen with Pieces of Her for Netflix and, of course, Will Trent for ABC, adapted her own work and served as showrunner. The psychological thriller revolves around estranged sisters Samantha and Charlotte Quinn who, as teens, suffer a brutal attack in response to their father Rusty’s (an excellent Brendan Gleeson) work as an attorney that has left them scarred. Partial blindness and a permanent limp serve as enduring physical reminders for Sam. Spared many of the physical scars, Charlie’s show up in other ways. And they both lost their beloved mother Gamma (an effective Olivia Williams).

As the older sister, Sam sacrificed herself to ensure Charlie would be hurt the least, but that act created emotional and physical distance between the two. Rusty’s failing health has brought Sam back home from New York City, where she is a patent lawyer, to Pikeville, Georgia. It’s his defense of Kelly Wilson (impressive newcomer Shayna Benardo), a special needs high school girl seemingly behind a horrific school shooting that killed the school’s principal and a freshman girl that keeps her there.

What seems like an open-and-shut case is anything but. Because Charlie witnessed part of the shooting and is also an attorney, the two women can’t avoid each other. Any degree of separation disappears in a small town, especially for a polarizing case like this one, so emotional landmines abound. As the mystery surrounding Kelly’s actions lingers, the attack that the sisters survived years before also figures more prominently, forcing both Sam and Charlie to tackle all they have left unsaid and unresolved.

“One of the pleasures of this is that I wrote everything from the perspective of two women. So much to probably Meghann and Rose’s dismay, one of them always has to be in every single scene, because we get their perspective, and it’s a very internalized kind of storytelling,” Slaughter said on set. “But also, in a way, it shows their growth as sisters and as daughters, and gives me an opportunity to frame something that happens every day in America, basically through these very personal stories of these sisters who have been victims of violence before.”

On set when speaking to The Hollywood Reporter, Byrne noted “it’s rare you get to work side by side with a fellow actress.” That female energy is also directly sustained behind the camera with Oscar and Emmy-nominated director Steph Green (New Boy, Duster, Watchmen) and Australian cinematographer Zoë White (The Handmaid’s Tale). Below, she probes beyond the many plot twists to delve into aspects of the series that move her professionally and emotionally. Of course, we asked if there would be more and if she felt what happens to Kelly, the young girl at the center of the mystery, is fair.

In addition to playing Sam, you’re also an executive producer. What made you want to take on roles in front and behind the camera?

Being an EP was really like more of a seat at the table, particularly with casting. Getting Meghann Fahy on board was a huge deal for us. She’s so effervescent as Charlie; so magnetic and funny and heartbreaking. You just believe her straight away. She jumps. She lights up the screen, and she’s just gorgeous. I adore her. She’s so extraordinary, so talented.

And then Brendan Gleeson, who’s a legend at this point — so extraordinary and such a gifted storyteller — and so magnanimous with his work. I’ve known him since I was 23 when I did Troy. So that was a great thread. I worked with his son Domhnall Gleeson, so it was a lovely full circle to then have him playing my father. And that’s a tough character; it’s very polarizing. There’s a lot of fury that my character has at her father, a lot of anger and disappointment, and Brendan makes that hard because he’s so charming, but that’s what makes it interesting.

Reading the screenplay and the novel… it’s a classic origin story for Sam, where going back to where she came from, she’s dealing with all of her truths and all of the lies, and trying to uncover the past through this case; understanding her father more, but it felt to me almost a role written for a man, not particularly something you would see necessarily for a female character. That really intrigued me.

How did you pull off Sam’s physical transformation to convey her disability or disabilities?

Well, she has dysarthria, [her] speech disability from her trauma, and so I worked with my voice coach Kate Wilson, who’s extraordinary. She’s the head of voice [and speech] at Juilliard. I’ve worked with her for years and she’s incredible. She’s the most alive, passionate, compassionate teacher, and she’s really very sage. She and I delved in with what that would sound like, listening to tapes and recordings, and also working on the accent of Georgia and how my character had lived in New York for 20 years, and what it would sound like returning, and when it would return, and in what situations. When I return to Australia, my accent becomes a lot broader, and you know how you’re influenced by your environment.

Then with the limp, I worked with this wonderful PT trainer who works in rehab with people who’ve suffered injuries and so on. We homed in on what her limp would look like, and then did a million videos and would send them to Steph and figure out how far to go with it because we didn’t want it ever to be comical. Sometimes what reads on screen is very different in real life.

The younger Charlie [Drew Ann Cheek] and Sam [Audrey Grace Marshall] really match the older Charlie and Sam.

I was very front seat with the casting of that, so there was a lot of weighing in with everybody about who we felt the best lead was and, also, physically you have to have a lot of resemblance to them. I thought the girls were so wonderful, and that’s again a very hard job to do. I thought they just captured both Meghann and myself in really specific ways, and we watched a lot of footage of them as well. With Sam, obviously there’s a real before and after physically with what she goes through, so that was something I had to separately have work on. But establishing them as young women was so important.

While Sam and Charlie, in particular, experience extraordinary circumstances, the series seems to center on this whole idea of women holding onto very traumatic events or violations but still functioning in their lives.

Which we do every day. If you read the statistics about it, like one in six women have been sexually assaulted. A lot of people in America have suffered gun violence in their families. These statistics are horrific, and we worked very closely with RAINN who work with survivors of rape and incest and other horrific things, and they were incredible. We always had resources on set if people were being triggered or needed to talk. It was a safe space.

To Steph Green, our director, my first question was: “How will this be portrayed? I’m not interested in anything gratuitous or exploitative.” And Steph was so determined to tell the story in a way that had none of those tonal qualities in it. So absolutely the story has elements of trauma, but it’s dealt with in a very delicate way that I think is powerful but never gratuitous or exploitative.

Speaking of Steph, the way she, and Zoë White the cinematographer, handle the traumatic flashbacks is such an innovative technique that I don’t think I’ve seen.

I agree. I remember her telling me about that, how she was going to focus on hands and this and moments and shoot close-ups of various things to sort of link the story. And it’s beautifully told. The sense of memory was really vivid, I felt, [in] a lot of those flashbacks to me. I feel like they achieved something quite innovative.

When Rusty dies, everything is understandably sad. But the coffin scene at the funeral home manages to be both light and heavy. Did it come at a time when you guys kind of needed a break?

Meghann was so heartbreaking in that scene, and she’s so funny, too. That was actually great because what was harder was the flashback scene when we’re in New York, and it precipitates the estrangement. That scene was a lot harder because we’re through a closed door, sort of screaming a monologue and it’s a flashback, and I’ve got a wig on. That to me was more of a leap of faith. I think that was a harder needle to thread. Whereas the coffin scene, as you called it, was so gratifying to be able to look at her and really have a scene like two actors opposite one another and have such rich material. It was definitely strange for Brendan because he was in the coffin for a little bit. That’s no fun. But that was a scene I was both scared and excited to do.

There are so many powerful scenes, what are some of your favorite moments, or what stands out most to you?

The scene that haunts me so much is when Charlie confronts Judith over the kitchen table. I remember reading it in the novel, and I remember the first one I read in the screenplay, and my hair on my body was just standing on end. It was so suspenseful, so tightly drawn, and the history alone between these two women across each other from this table to then reach this boiling point of this scene was just masterful storytelling.

And it’s interesting because Sam and Charlie don’t have that traumatic relationship with them that Kelly has. Then considering that she’s special needs. So it’s unfathomable that the Pinkmans could do something like this to Kelly, right?

Yeah Karin, she’s a master storyteller, and just when you think you know where something is going. I found myself reading the novel and reading the screenplay [drawn to] the turns of what these people do and the perspectives that she puts on each situation. But that scene still haunts me.

I think it’s a very, very complicated answer and question. It’s such a nuanced and complicated story that has led her to this horrific situation that we made her in at the beginning. I really was fascinated with the process of that. I spoke to many lawyers who work in the criminal justice system about the process and how it works, and where you end up, and how you interrogate a witness, and like the defense team, and all of that. And Karin so beautifully captures how hard that system is and how it fails again and again and again for people, but how also you can have some wins. But it’s very nuanced and it’s very complicated.

It was a learning curve for me to even begin to understand that. So I think [Karin] has such a great understanding of that, and also the genre of a thriller, you know, and creating the suspense of that. Right until the very last episode, you’re really on the edge of your seat, as she just twists and turns and uses the perspective of one character then the other, and you constantly have shifting alliances, and she’s just very unflinching in her portrait of things. And that’s brave.

That’s why I ask if what happens to Kelly is fair because it’s very clear in the end how manipulated she was. And given her mental capacity, she wasn’t really aware of what she was doing.

Do you see a continuation to The Good Daughter?

I don’t. It feels contained to this series, like the novel, to me. I have hope for the characters that they would, I mean, selfishly, continue to have a better relationship with one another after all the trauma and all the things they’ve weathered or the storms they’ve weathered. But it felt, to me, pretty contained about this specific thing they had to go through.

I’m sure you take lessons from each project that you do. What is your biggest takeaway from this one?

I think diving back into this genre was really interesting for me [because] I hadn’t done it for many years. [There are] tropes that are really fun in there, and the challenges of a very complicated plot at times, and being ahead of it for myself. But Karin, the author, is really ahead of everybody. So, it was interesting. Also to work with an author who then wrote the screenplay, that was also something I’d never done creatively, and how fun that was. Also, we would have rigorous conversations where we would disagree about things, and that was fun too. I think I learned a lot.

All six episodes of The Good Daughter are streaming on Peacock.
