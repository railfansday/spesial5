Anthony Boyle on Becoming Sam Bankman-Fried for ‘The Altruists’ and Finding the Grey in History’s Villains: ‘I’ve Played More Real People Than Not’

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

A little over 10 years ago, Anthony Boyle was about to celebrate his first major screen role. It wasn’t a huge part, but “The Journey” was a sizeable career leg-up for the Northern Irish actor, then barely out of drama school and with a resume largely led by a blink-and-you’ll-miss-it appearance on “Game of Thrones.” It was also a subject extremely close to home.

In Nick Hamm’s road trip drama about Northern Ireland’s peace process, Boyle — who grew up in a predominantly Catholic neighbourhood in West Belfast — was playing a young Ian Paisley, the fire-and-brimstone Protestant minister once described as a “foghorn on legs” (by Ozzy Osbourne, no less). With the film premiering in Venice, it was the perfect introduction. “And they cut me from it!” he says with a laugh, speaking from an upmarket gym in central London with a street entrance so discreet it’s impossible to find without assistance. “I’d done the whole voice and everything — and they had me in the freezing sea getting baptized in a big white gown. And they cut me!”

Boyle even went to cinema to see the film, just to double-check. “I thought, maybe they put it in a flashback, or perhaps an end credit scene like Marvel movies.” They had not.

More than a decade on from this cinematic slight and his well-rehearsed rendition of Paisley’s booming baritone is yet to be utilized on screen. But audiences are now able to hear another accent Boyle’s been carefully crafting, and one that couldn’t be more different: the high-pitched, slightly nasal and mumbly voice of convicted crypto king Sam Bankman-Fried for “The Altruists,” out now on Netflix.

As it happens, since his Paisley faced the cutting room floor, Boyle — now 32 — has become TV’s go-to guy to play a wide assortment of other real-life people, both at home and abroad. At the same time as he’s swiftly risen up call sheets, over the last two years alone he’s stepped into the shoes of U.S. WWII navigator Harry Herbert Crosby in “Masters of the Air” and Abraham Lincoln’s assassin John Wilkes Booth in “Manhunt,” both for Apple TV, IRA commander Brendan Hughes in Hulu’s “Say Nothing” and brewery heir Arthur Guinness in Netflix’s “House of Guinness.”

“I think I’ve played more real people than not,” he notes. “I’ve just got a very white, generic face — if you squint, you go ‘well, he sort of looks like him.’ It’s how I’ve stayed employed.”

There’s much more to it than that, though. Most of Boyle’s roles have been compromised and troubled individuals, sometimes cast via history as the villain. And yet he’s offered them nuance and a sense of vulnerability — serving up delicious shades of grey between the black and white.

Playing Sam Bankman-Fried — the mop-topped and scruffy former crypto poster boy now serving a 25-year sentence for stealing billions of dollars of customer funds — only cements Boyle’s status as one of the most exciting new character actors around.

For all the headlines and eye-watering amounts of money, there was much grey area to explore.

As the show charts across its eight episodes, Bankman-Fried — alongside business partner, on-again off-again again love interest and fellow socially-awkward nerd Caroline Ellison (played to perfection by Julia Garner) — were followers of the “effective altruism” movement, and set out to use their tech wizardry to make a fortune and give most of it away. That’s not what happened, of course.

“A crime was committed. People lost their jobs, families broke apart,” says Boyle. “But I do feel he was motivated by good. You know, the path to hell is paved with good intentions, and all the rest of it.”

Boyle was prepping for what he describes as “that fucking accent that makes me sound like I’m on helium” months before he knew anything about “The Altruists.”

In a bizarre coincidence, he’d been cast as Bankman-Fried in a different project, a film he says was “more of an Aaron Sorkin, ‘The Social Network’ thing.” Excited by the opportunity, he had dived into the research, soaking up the “hours of footage” of him online. “That guy loved an interview — so there was so much for me to pull from.” The accent he created was a cocktail of a “sort of lisp that kids have when they spend too much time on the PlayStation” and “Californian vowel sounds,” mixed in with some stuttering.

Boyle had even taken it out to test-drive on the streets, the streets in Bankman-Fried’s case being “World of Warcraft” and Magic: The Gathering conventions. He’d done similar things previously. Before playing Wilkes Booth in “Manhunt,” he had hung out with cowboys. “I love getting into who they are. That’s the most exciting part of the job,” he says.

But then, as movies so often do, it fell apart. He was distraught.

Months later, while shooting another film, he got a call — there was a different Sam Bankman-Fried project in the works, this time a series and at Netflix. Garner was already attached. “I was like, no, I can’t — my heart’s already been broken by this before. I’d spent months researching this guy.” But he read the script and found it “so funny and curious, it’s like a love story — and so different than the movie.”

For an initial Zoom with creator Graham Moore and director James Ponsoldt, Boyle put his studies to good use, buying a jumper from budget retailer Primark and covering it in “Pot Noodle stains” and back-combing his hair to make it look as bushy as possible. He also launched into character from the off. “I was like, ‘Hi guys, I’m just off the phone with Mark Zuckerberg, God that guy was keeping me forever,’” he says in full Bankman-Fried tones.

Weeks later at the first screen test with Garner — “the best actor I’ve ever worked with… she’s so good,” says Boyle — he was delighted to discover that she had the same approach. “I’d only ever seen her with a short pixie cut looking very beautiful, but she comes in with her wig on and the nose and teeth and her body language is so different — she’s awkward, bumping into people.” Realising what he was up against, he introduced himself as Sam, with the helium voice, and they immediately started talking in character.

As part of his deep dive into Bankman-Fried, Boyle was even able to speak to the man himself in prison (apparently in the “same cell block as P Diddy”). There had been plans to visit, but the convict had breached prison rules by giving Tucker Carlson an unauthorized interview and was moved from Brooklyn to LA. With the first week of filming already underway in Toronto in early 2025, there wasn’t time, so instead, via a temporary email address he was given, Boyle sent “well over 100 questions.” While he promised he wouldn’t reveal the contents of the exchange, he claims Bankman-Fried “answered most of them” and was “very forthcoming.”

“You’re also just sort of bowled over immediately by his intelligence,” he says. “This is a guy who walks into rooms in dirty shorts and t-shirts and gets VCs to give him billions and billions of dollars.”

But the billions of dollars weren’t what Boyle — who admits he’s never had any interest in crypto or wealth accumulation — wanted to ask about. “Knowing about financial crime doesn’t help me when I’m playing the role,” he says. “When I’m creating a character, what I’m interested in is like someone’s childhood and inner thoughts, feelings, hopes, dreams, nightmares, that kind of thing.”

Earlier this year, Boyle was in Cannes with Clio Barnard’s “I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning,” the film that he actually went off to make after the first Bankman-Fried project fell through.

A moving slice of Gen Z social-realism set and shot in the British city of Birmingham, the drama follows five close friends from the same council estate. Now reaching their 30s, they’re realizing that economic reality has largely thwarted whatever ambitions they once had. Boyle, who stars alongside fellow fast-risers Lola Petticrew, Joe Cole, Jay Lycurgo and Daryl McCormack, plays a frustrated and depressed father-of-two who, despite his university degree, finds himself forced to deliver takeaway food. It’s gritty and bleak at times, but filled with warmth, empathy and camaraderie (accompanied by comedy dancing when they start hitting the drinks).

For Boyle, it was “the kind of film that made me want to be an actor — when I watched films like ‘This Is England’ and ‘Quadrophenia’ and Ken Loach, films that have that political motivation.” As a kid from a working-class neighborhood himself, it was also a world and community he recognized, with the film reflecting the “love and light and people helping each other” that he remembers. Crucially, it wasn’t all grey skies and “people crying into cups of tea.”

Barnard, he says, was his first time being directed by a “real auteur.” She’s completely changed his outlook on acting. Whereas projects were previously selected based on his character, Boyle now says he “wants to work with people like her who have that vision and can create that kind of cinema.”

“I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning” was also another chance to act alongside Petticrew, arguably the Emerald Isle’s other brightest new talent and recently hailed for her chilling performance as a vengeance-seeking serial killer being chased by Emmy Rossum in Hulu’s “Furious.”

As it happens, Boyle and Petticrew grew up in the same part of Belfast and have been “besties” since they first met aged around 11 or 12. “We were just hanging out with each other on the streets, and then wanting to be actors and doing am-dram together,” he says. “We were the two little weirdos, because nobody else wanted to act.”

Around the age of 15, they appeared together in a short film from director Mike Lennox called “Rough Deal” (Boyle played a drug dealer, Petticrew his little sister who then took the drugs and overdosed). “It was for schools or something,” says Boyle. A little over 10 years later and Lennox, now an Oscar nominee and BAFTA winner, was one of the directors and exec producers on “Say Nothing,” FX’s drama about the Troubles. “And in his pitch deck, he had put forward Lola and I.”

Boyle was asked to play the younger Brendan Hughes, one of leaders of the 1980 hunger strikes whose mural he walked past on his way to school, while Lola was up as the key role of IRA volunteer Dolours Price. They did the audition tape in Boyle’s mum’s living room.

“We put her ironing board out and a few books of fucking Narnia and a John Grisham that my dad was reading and duct-taped my iPhone to a World’s Best Mum mug, and did it,” he says. “And that’s how we booked most of the jobs we’ve got. Lola always comes and helps me for a big job.”

“The Altruists” and “I See Buildings Fall Like Lightning” (which recently won Boyle the joint best actor award at the Rome Film Festival) are the latest highlights in a career still very much in its ascendancy. But casting directors excited by what they see may have to form an orderly queue — his upcoming schedule is already stacked.

The reason we’re talking from a swanky celeb-friendly gym in London isn’t because Boyle enjoys working out, but because he’s in the middle of shooting Sky series “Charmer,” playing a seducer-for-hire who tests the fidelity of his wealthy clients’ partners. There’s full-frontal scene, so he figured he should get in shape. “My character’s self-obsessed and goes to the gym every day, so it wouldn’t make sense if I was built like a cigarette,” he says. Sky, thankfully, paid for extortionate gym fees.

Once “Charmer” wraps in January, Boyle’s back into bowler hat, booze and brawling territory for the second season of “House of Guinness.” He says there are two projects incoming afterwards, including a “tennis movie,” although he won’t offer any further details. Another feature, “The Worst” shot in France this past summer during the World Cup, is now in post. Boyle actually replaced fellow Belfast native Jamie Dornan (“Ireland’s best looking man”) in the dark comedy, in which he plays a name-dropping talent agent alongside Keira Knightley and Alicia Vikander.

And then there’s “Close to Home,” the aptly titled series that finally takes Boyle back to his roots. It’s his most personal project to date, about a graduate returning to post-Troubles Belfast and based on the novel by Michael Magee, which happens to be his favorite book. “There’s so many similarities to his life and mine, and it’s the first time I’d read something that felt like, Jesus Christ, these are thoughts only I thought I’ve had,” he says, adding that Magee “sat next to my brother in school.”

But for “Close to Home” he’s not just the lead (and co-starring with another fast-rising local, “Kneecap” breakout Jessica Reynolds), but getting behind the camera too as his first executive producer credit. “It was great to be involved in the casting and location meetings and just every little thing you can think of — I just loved it,” he says.

Shooting “Close to Home” in Belfast earlier this year coincided with Boyle actually moving back to his home town after a decade flitting between London and New York.

He’s triumphantly returned, 10 years after his Ian Paisley was deleted, now as a leading man, a celebrated chameleon who can seemingly take on any historical figure thrown at him — and their voice, no matter how high-pitched or nasal — and even a filmmaker in his own right.

Not that the old friends he’s getting to hang out with again seem to care.

“My best mate — my best mate in the world — hasn’t seen anything I’ve been in,” says Boyle. “He says to me, I’ll watch you when you’re in a film with The Rock. That’s all he watches. He once tried to watch a period drama I was in and got about 10 minutes into it and was just like ‘This is so boring. There were no fights, no car chases.’ So we don’t speak about work.”
