Eric Coomer (of Dominion Voting) Loses Libel Lawsuit over The Deep Rig

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The court holds that Coomer didn't prove by "clear and convincing evidence" that defendant knew the statements were false (or at least were likely false).

From Judge Tom Barber (M.D. Fla.) Tuesday in Coomer v. Byrne:

This highly contentious defamation action arises from The Deep Rig, a documentary film concerning alleged fraud in the 2020 presidential election[, which was financed by defendant Patrick Byrne, who also appeared in the film].…. Plaintiff Eric Coomer formerly served as director of product strategy and security for Dominion Voting Systems, a supplier of election hardware and software with its United States headquarters in Colorado. Among other things, the film included statements by non-party Joseph Oltmann claiming that he infiltrated a conference call involving members of Antifa and heard comments from a speaker identified by another anonymous participant on the call as "Eric" and "the Dominion guy."

According to Oltmann, the speaker stated on the call in substance that President Donald J. Trump would not win the election because he had "made sure of it." Oltmann clams he later determined through research and by recognizing Coomer's voice that the speaker had been Coomer. Oltmann further stated that he concluded there was a "high probability" Coomer affected the election. Coomer denies participating in any such call or manipulating the results of the 2020 Presidential election and alleges that Oltmann's story presented in the film was false and defamatory.

Coomer sued, and the court earlier held that he had adequately "alleged sufficient facts to avoid dismissal" and to proceed to discovery. But then Byrne sought summary judgment, arguing that, after discovery, Coomer hadn't introduced evidence to support his claim. And the court concluded that Coomer had indeed not uncovered enough evidence to prove "actual malice" by clear and convincing evidence:

[U]nder Colorado law, where the alleged defamatory statement relates to a matter of public concern, a defamation plaintiff must prove actual malice on the part of the defendant, and must do so by clear and convincing evidence, even if the plaintiff is not a public figure. Coomer has expressly conceded that the statements at issue concern matters of public concern, [and] that Colorado's actual malice standard applies ….

Coomer himself asserts that Byrne believed the 2020 election was rigged immediately after the election. Byrne testified that he believed Oltmann's story and remains highly confident that the gist of Oltmann's story was true. Although the actual malice standard focuses on Byrne's subjective state of mind and beliefs, Byrne's statements as to those beliefs are not necessarily dispositive and Coomer may seek to establish Byrne's subjective lack of belief in the story through circumstantial evidence. The relevant question, then, is whether the evidence Coomer points to is sufficient to support a jury finding, under the clear and convincing evidence standard, that Byrne in fact subjectively disbelieved or had serious doubts about Oltmann's story.

Coomer points to circumstantial evidence that includes the improbable nature of Oltmann's allegations, the lack of independent investigation by Bryne and the lack of corroboration for Oltmann's story, published reports and opinions rebutting claims of election fraud, and Coomer's denials and other suits for defamation filed prior to the publication of the film. It would be reasonable to conclude from a totality of the evidence that, had Byrne acted reasonably, he would have concluded that Oltmann's story was probably false and that Coomer probably did not attempt to tamper with or "rig" the 2020 Presidential election. However, the question for the jury at trial would not be whether Byrne reasonably should have reached that conclusion. It would be whether he did in fact reach that conclusion or at least harbored serious doubts about Oltmann's story or was highly aware it was probably false….

[R]egardless of whether a finding of actual malice might be supportable under the preponderance standard, the evidence is insufficient to support the same conclusion under the much higher clear and convincing standard. Byrne's lack of skepticism, failure to investigate, and disregard for government reports and conclusions by others debunking claims of fraud the 2020 election are equally consistent with negligence and a belief that there was essentially a coverup going on, in line with the grand conspiracy theory portrayed in the film.

Coomer points out that Byrne did not include Oltmann's story in his own book of the same title, but Byrne explained that he wanted to present an argument based on publicly available information, which, unlike a single witness's account of an unrecorded phone call, would be taken seriously as supporting a nationwide investigation. Any inference from the totality of this evidence to a conclusion that Byrne actually disbelieved or seriously doubted Oltmann's story would be weak at best and insufficient to support a finding of actual malice by clear and convincing evidence….

Allowing a jury to infer from evidence that a reasonable person would have doubted Oltmann's story to a conclusion that it is not only possible, but highly probable and free from serious or substantial doubt that Byrne, did, in fact, have serious doubts about it would require the Court to ignore not only the standards just discussed but human nature as well.

The world has always had people with zealously held beliefs who are quite vocal and eager to share their views with anyone who will listen. These people are frequently labeled, pejoratively, as extremists, fanatics, "kooks," "crazies," conspiracy theorists, and the like. They typically have a lot to say on their subjects of interest, and modern technology has given them a powerful microphone to convey their thoughts well beyond the limits of their own communities or personal echo-chambers.

They may make statements that reasonable people consider to be wrong, and sometimes their statements are, in fact, objectively and demonstrably wrong. But those with zealously held beliefs hold firm even when overwhelming objective evidence may be to the contrary. This is because they genuinely and sincerely believe what they say with every fiber of their being, even when objective evidence may suggest otherwise. Many are highly intelligent and sophisticated people, but they never seriously doubt the truth of their statements and remain steadfast in their beliefs no matter what. While these speakers sometimes espouse beliefs and make statements many might consider to be extreme, offensive, irresponsible, and factually wrong, their beliefs are, nonetheless, sincerely held and those speakers believe everything they say is completely true.

Even aside from extreme examples, people in general are hardly paragons of reason and often do not reach or maintain their beliefs by dispassionate assessments of the evidence. They believe what they do, especially on important political, moral and religious issues, as a result of unconscious biases, predispositions, prior emotional, philosophical, and political commitments and intuitions, as well as a disinclination to exert the effort or risk the emotional discomfort involved in exposing themselves to contrary evidence and viewpoints.

As John Kenneth Galbraith observed, "[f]aced with the choice between changing one's mind and proving that there is no need to do so, almost everyone gets busy on the proof." Operating within an echo chamber while believing oneself to be well-informed and reasonable has become increasingly easy to do in the age of slanted news sources and targeted media constantly telling their constituencies what they want to hear.

Of course, people with sincerely or even zealously held beliefs may make statements that are both untrue and inflict serious harm on others. Under the actual malice standard, unless it can be proven that the speaker subjectively believed his or her statements to be untrue, the law imposes no liability. That is a policy choice that might deserve reexamination by the appropriate decision makers—the United States Supreme Court and the Colorado courts. As [this] Court noted in the Trump Media & Tech. Group Corp. v. WP Co. LLC (M.D. Fla. 2026) case, the actual malice standard has been criticized by courts and commentators on a number of grounds, with which [this] Court largely agrees.

In short, if the law did not require "clear and convincing evidence" that the defendant subjectively doubted or disbelieved the published statements, it is likely that Byrne's motion for summary judgment would have been denied, and a jury would have had the opportunity to weigh in on this matter. However, under the law as it currently stands, Coomer's evidence is insufficient to meet these standards and summary judgment for Byrne is therefore required….

Peter Ticktin and David Lawrence Perry II (The Ticktin Law Group) represent Byrne.
