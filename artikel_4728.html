How Video Games Make Money by Tracking Your Behavior

Many video games cheat. Even games that seem fair on the surface use clever tricks to manipulate you into spending more time and money than you otherwise would.

Researcher Ayo Omolewa decompiled a number of popular games and apps, including Block Blast, Subway Surfers, and Candy Crush, to see how they work. He discovered that many of these games twist and change to maximize the amount of money they think they can make from you. Like a living thing, these apps analyze how you play, how skilled you are, and how much pain you’re willing to put up with and then adjust themselves accordingly.

In January 2026, the game Block Blast by developer Hungry Studio was the most downloaded game in the world. About three hundred million people played it that month.

In Block Blast, players work to drag and drop randomly selected tiles into a grid. When one places a tile in a way that completes a row or column, the row or column is removed, like finishing a row in Tetris, making room for further tiles. The game ends when there is no room to place the remaining tiles.

It is designed to feel like a luck-based strategy game. Which of the three blocks should you place first and where? “If I put this first block into the top corner, it will clear that row, which should give me space to place the second one…”

However, because this is a free-to-play game, the developers need to find ways to make money. I wrote about how free-to-play games make money by manipulating players in more detail here.

In this game, it’s by making you watch ads. In addition to a rotating set of ads below the playable area, whenever players lose, they can either restart the level and lose their progress, or watch an optional advertisement.

Making a game like this would be relatively simple, but based on Omolewa’s research, the developers appear to have added extra code to maximize the chance players would watch ads. Omolewa found that the game tracks more than 250 variables for each player. The game uses this data to determine when you’re most likely to watch an ad and then pounces.

It turns out that the set of tiles the game offers you isn’t random after all.

For example, it keeps track of how many ads you’ve watched in a row. Once you’ve watched three in a row, you’ve demonstrated that you’re willing to sit through advertisements. It then watches your score and waits until you’re at least 10% above your high score. It knows that you’re invested, because you’re doing better than you ever have.

It then waits until an ad is worth at least 3.5 times the money as the last ad you watched. Once you meet these criteria (you’ve watched three ads in a row, you’re doing well, and the next ad will earn the developers enough money), it triggers the “death algorithm.” (“Death algorithm” isn’t a phrase I invented; it’s literally in the game’s code.)

At this point, the game stops offering random sets of tiles and starts looking for a set of tiles that is impossible to place. Every turn from then on, you will have a 20% chance of receiving that set and losing. There’s no way to win with those tiles. If you want to continue, you will have to watch the ad.

Once it’s worth enough to the developers, they force you to lose. No amount of skill or strategy can salvage your game. Only watching an ad can.

Omolewa found several algorithms like this within the game's code. Some keep track of how often you play the game. Others consider what model phone you have, what country you’re playing in, even how you originally came across and downloaded the game. These are all used to calculate how much they will earn from you watching an ad and how likely you are to play tomorrow to determine exactly how much pain they can put you through without becoming so frustrated that you stop playing. You can read his full breakdown here.

In other words, what appears at first to be a simple block placing game is really a money-making machine that tracks your location, phone, and everything you do in the game to make you lose exactly when you’re going to make them as much money as possible.

Although this game seems to use this information only to get users to watch more ads, others track you in similar ways to keep you coming back and spending money. He found in Subway Surfers’ code that the game becomes easier or harder depending on how much money you’ve paid over time, how long it’s been since you’ve watched an ad, and more than 150 other variables.

Candy Crush may similarly adjust the current difficulty, but also changes the rewards for watching an ad and the prices of in-game bonuses based on how well you’ve been performing, how close you are to beating the current level, how well you’re doing in the weekly rankings, how often you watch the optional ads, and how often you’ve given the app real money. Even the language-learning app Duolingo seems to monitor variables like what kinds of notifications get you to open the app most frequently and tracks what Wi-Fi network you’re on to see if you’re home before pushing a reminder notification.

Games and apps like these leave nothing to chance. They’re constantly tracking and analyzing everything you do: every time you open the app, every click, every payment, your skill level, your willingness to watch ads, and hundreds of other variables to tweak the game to maximize the chance that you’ll watch an ad or give them money. Keep this in mind the next time a game asks you for something. They’ve probably been building a profile on you and using it to their advantage.
