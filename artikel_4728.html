Sometimes You Need to Travel to See Your Life Clearly

My financial advisor’s corner office literally hovered above Seattle’s Lake Union. A bald eagle soared past the window. I wondered what it would be like to work in a room with a window—never mind a view.

I was in the middle of a divorce, and a friend had insisted I see her financial planner to get my financial bearings. I took a deep breath, ready to be obedient to whatever plan this woman would lay out for me. The fear I’d been waking up with every day was killing me—fear about paying my rent, my student loan, my car payment, my credit card debt, my health insurance. I also had fear about the future and fear of digging out of my past.

I sat up straight and pulled my baseball cap back on my head. Ready.

She paused and then said, “If you want to retire at age 67 and live at the same standard of living you have now..."

My current standard of living wasn’t anything to write home about. I thought to myself, You mean living in a dingy little apartment with rats in the backyard and a dishwasher falling out of the wall. But I just waited.

She cleared her throat. “You’re going to need to save $12,000 per month between now and then.”

I suddenly couldn’t breathe. “How much?” I finally asked.

Now I flat-out just stared at her until we were both uncomfortable.

I gave her my credit card to pay her the $1,000 we’d agreed on for her advice, and gathered my things. We shook hands. I left the building, got into my half-paid-off Kia Soul and drove myself "home."

When I got there, I plopped myself down on the sofa that a friend had donated to me when she found out I was getting divorced.

I poured myself a glass of wine and cried. Then I opened my computer to look at my savings account. I had $831.26. I had a sudden urge to escape the reality of my life.

Still crying, I pulled up Expedia to see how I could get out of town for what I had in my account. If I could never retire, I might as well get out of dodge for a week now. There were cheap tickets to Panama City. I booked a round-trip airplane ticket for $492 and posted a note on social media. Did anyone know anyone I could stay with in Panama?

When I got to Panama, I stayed in a hostel for a few nights and wandered through Casco Viejo, the city's historic district. I visited the Panama Canal, marveling at the audacity of it.

Then I traveled up through David and eventually made my way to Boquete, a mountain town filled with expats surrounded by lush green hills.

There I stayed with Diane, the friend of a friend—an 82-year-old retired US citizen who was living permanently in Panama, enjoying a life that seemed rich in all the ways that mattered. She had a condo, a community, adventure, beauty, and enough money to live comfortably. She even had a new boyfriend.

One evening over dinner, Diane said something amazing to me. “You know what? There are a lot of ways to live a life. You can make up different rules if you want. Look at me? I’m living on less than I get from my Social Security checks.”

That’s when I had my epiphany: Other people’s stories—even the stories our whole culture buys into—are just stories. I didn’t have to let the word “retirement” or any other word intimidate me. I could dream outside the box.

We all carry around ideas about how life is supposed to work. And these expectations become so deeply embedded that we stop recognizing them as assumptions. We mistake them for facts.

When our lives don't measure up, we get anxious or conclude we've failed.

In Panama, I started to understand that there were many ways to build a satisfying life. Some involved more money. Others might require moving halfway around the world.

By the time I boarded the plane to go back to my crappy apartment, I could see possibilities for my future that had been invisible to me before.

Of course, you don't have to leave the country to change your perspective. You could read a book, take a class, or have a conversation with someone whose life looks different from yours.

But there is something extra powerful about physically removing oneself from familiar surroundings.

However we do it, when we become strangers in a new place, we can see the world differently.

1. The future isn't a single destination.

When someone tells you what your future must look like, remember that they're working from their own assumptions—not from yours.

2. Sometimes you need new information, not a better plan.

I could have gone home from the financial planner's office and spent months trying to figure out how to earn enough money to meet her recommendation. Instead, I went somewhere that exposed me to an entirely different way of thinking about what I needed.

I've thought about that trip many times over the years because it gave me back a sense of possibility during a really scary time in my life.

That's what I think a “second wind” really is.

It's not necessarily a dramatic reinvention. Sometimes it's just a moment in time when you realize that the life you've been imagining isn't the only life available to you.

If you're feeling stuck, if the “numbers” don't add up, or if the future you've been told to prepare for feels impossible, consider this: You may not need to figure everything out all at once. You just might need a fresh perspective.
