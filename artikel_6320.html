MIT researchers built a tiny new computer from squishy materials that works like a human brain

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

A soft film 2 nanometers thick lets a tiny device hold a memory for seconds and then fire like a neuron, all within one part.

Computers left moving parts behind a long time ago, trading gears and wheels for electrons flowing through chips that don’t change shape.

At a size far smaller than a single brain cell, a new device goes back to motion. Once squeezed, it takes a few seconds to recover its shape, so it can add up signals and fire, like an artificial neuron.

Because of that delay, one tiny part holds a short memory and acts on it. That job usually takes extra components such as capacitors.

Farnaz Niroui, an associate professor in MIT’s Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science (EECS) and a member of its Research Laboratory of Electronics (RLE), led the team that built it. Chemist Jeremiah Johnson of MIT’s Department of Chemistry joined them.

It’s a laboratory demonstration, and its biggest energy savings are a projection rather than a measurement.

Part of the inspiration came from animals that handle information where it arrives. For example, many of an octopus’s nerve cells are in its arms, which can sense and respond without waiting on the brain.

“You can think of an octopus as continuous computing matter, with computing, memory, sensing, and actuation distributed throughout its body,” Niroui said.

Mechanical computing goes back at least to the gears of the ancient Antikythera mechanism.

Modern mechanical computers have mostly stayed at the scale of millimeters. Shrinking them further runs into a basic problem. Surfaces brought within a few billionths of a meter of each other pull together and stick.

To get around it, the researchers laid a soft polymer film called PDMS, about 2 nanometers thick, between two gold electrodes.

Each device’s working area is a square 200 nanometers on a side, so more than 20 of them could fit across even a small neuron.

The top electrode is free to move. When the researchers applied a voltage, the two electrodes pulled toward each other and squeezed the film. More current crosses a thinner gap, so the current rose as the film thinned.

Niroui described the film as a “nano-spring” that keeps the metal surfaces from collapsing together for good. From the current, the team calculated that the gap shrank from about 2.3 nanometers to 1.6 nanometers.

They confirmed the squeeze with light. As the voltage rose, light scattered off a device shifted toward red, while a copy built with a stiff ceramic layer instead of PDMS showed no shift.

Peter Satterthwaite, a graduate student who co-led the work with fellow graduate student Sarah Spector, explained why that matters.

“PDMS is viscoelastic, which means that after being compressed, it takes time to return to its original state,” Satterthwaite said.

“This allows the devices to dynamically remember the history of forces and voltages applied to them, and convert that history into an electrical response.”

A pulse lasting 50 milliseconds squeezed the film by about 8 percent, and the current recovered as soon as the voltage stopped. A pulse 10 times longer squeezed it by about 30 percent, and the current returned gradually over several seconds.

So what the device does now depends on what happened to it a few seconds ago. When the team waited only half a second between tests instead of 40 seconds, the film hadn’t fully recovered, and the tests started from a narrower gap.

A neuron in the brain collects incoming signals a little at a time. If they arrive slowly, the charge drains away. If they arrive close together, the charge builds past a threshold, and the neuron fires a signal to its neighbors before resetting.

The neuron device did the same thing. The team sent it a string of 2.5-volt pulses, each too weak to trigger it alone, spaced closer together than the film could recover. After 16 pulses, the current jumped, and the device then relaxed back to where it started.

“We have this complex functionality, which is the basis of biological computing, all contained in one nanoscale device,” he said.

Its memory lasts seconds, while most other artificial neurons hold theirs for millionths or thousandths of a second. The team wrote that this suits tasks that need context over a few seconds.

The researchers also wired two devices to one shared resistor. Once the first device fired, the second couldn’t fire until the first had relaxed, the way one active neuron in a brain can hold back its neighbor.

As built, the device used 54 nanojoules of energy in tests. By the team’s calculation, it only needs to store about 2 femtojoules of electrical energy each time it fires, and a femtojoule is a millionth of a nanojoule.

At 200 nanometers across, the device is more than 100 times smaller than a typical neuron. Cut the current that leaks through it and shrink it further, the team wrote, and it could beat a real neuron on energy efficiency.

The team presented that kind of efficiency as a way to meet the growing demands of artificial intelligence.

“The performance highly relies on the memory introduced using the soft polymer,” Spector said. “We can intentionally engineer this over a large design space to meet the requirements of the desired applications.”

Every measurement was made in a lab, on devices kept under nitrogen gas at 80 percent humidity, and the devices held up better in humid conditions than dry ones. Devices from separate batches behaved alike but not identically.

The team showed single devices and one connected pair, not a network trained to do a task. The authors wrote that practical use will take progress on the materials, the device design and the training methods.

Spector said the material could also suit devices that work with the body, such as prosthetics that process touch quickly or low-power health patches worn on the skin. The team also mentioned robots and environmental sensors.

Next, the team wants to build sensing into the same material, so a single squeezable layer can sense, remember and compute.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.
