A dinosaur found by a Montana rancher on her land in 2019 sold for $47.3 million, over five times its top estimate

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

A Nanotyrannus skeleton nicknamed Jodi sold for $47.3 million at Sotheby's in 2026, raising concerns about scientific access to rare fossils.

A Nanotyrannus skeleton nicknamed “Jodi” sold at Sotheby’s in New York on October 27, 2026, for a final price of $47.3 million. Its pre-sale estimate was $6 million to $9 million.

Jodi Pierson found the skeleton in 2019 on her privately owned land near the town of Jordan, in Garfield County, Montana.

Six bidders competed for more than 12 minutes, the auction house said. Jodi is now the second most valuable dinosaur ever sold at auction.

The record belongs to “Gus,” a Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton that sold at Sotheby’s New York for $50.1 million on July 14, 2026.

According to Guinness World Records, Gus broke the record set by “Apex,” a Stegosaurus also sold at Sotheby’s New York. Apex fetched $44.6 million on July 17, 2024.

Sotheby’s reports its results including the buyer’s premium, the fee added to the winning bid. The hammer price for Jodi has not been made public.

Jodi was the only lot in its own dedicated sale. Neither the buyer nor the seller has been named.

Its listed provenance is a private collection that acquired the skeleton in 2021.

Sotheby’s lists Jodi as Nanotyrannus cf. lancensis, from the Hell Creek Formation, about 67 million years old. The abbreviation “cf.” marks a provisional identification.

A crew of seven, including Pierson and Clayton Phipps, dug up the skeleton in a single field season. It comprises about 191 fossil bone elements from one individual.

Its virtually complete skull has 53 bones and roughly 50 teeth. The rarely preserved wishbone is intact, and about half of the belly ribs, or gastralia, survived.

Missing parts were restored with 3D-printed and sculpted elements, and the mounted skeleton measures about 19.6 feet (6 meters) from nose to tail. It stands about 9 feet 6 inches (2.9 meters) tall, not 6 feet 5 inches (2 meters) as first listed.

The auction house describes the animal as an adult, based on its overall size and degree of bone development. It cites no peer-reviewed study of the skeleton.

At the Frieze Masters art fair in 2023, the same skeleton was exhibited as a juvenile T. rex.

For decades, many paleontologists argued that Nanotyrannus fossils were simply young T. rex. The debate centered on a skull collected in Montana in 1942 by the Cleveland Museum of Natural History.

In 1988, a team that included museum curator Dr. Michael Williams named it Nanotyrannus lancensis. In December 2025, a study in Science found that the skull’s throat bone had stopped growing, indicating a mature animal.

In October 2025, a study in Nature concluded that the tyrannosaur in the “Dueling Dinosaurs” fossil was a fully grown Nanotyrannus lancensis. Growth rings and fused vertebrae put its age at about 20 years.

That animal had larger forelimbs, more teeth and fewer tail vertebrae than T. rex, the North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences reported.

After examining more than 200 tyrannosaur fossils, the researchers also named a second species, Nanotyrannus lethaeus, after the River Lethe of Greek mythology.

“This fossil doesn’t just settle the debate. It flips decades of T. rex research on its head,” Lindsay Zanno, head of paleontology at the North Carolina museum and a co-author of the study, said in 2025.

Before the Gus auction, the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology issued a statement on scientifically significant fossils. It called for them to be permanently curated in accredited museums, universities and other public research institutions.

Without provisions for long-term access, private ownership may permanently diminish opportunities for future research, education and public engagement, the society warned. It also recognized the important role of private landowners, amateur fossil enthusiasts and commercial excavators.

“The discovery of an important fossil is only the beginning of its scientific story,” said Kristina Curry Rogers, the society’s vice president at the time.

“Many of the most significant advances in paleontology have come years or even decades after a fossil was collected,” she noted. New analytical methods and technologies allowed scientists to ask entirely new questions of specimens already housed in museums.

After the Jodi sale, Curry Rogers and Zanno published an opinion piece on fossil auctions. They wrote that they hope the new owner will make Jodi available for scientific study and public education.

Under Montana law, fossils are part of the surface estate, so they normally belong to whoever owns the land.

The provision, enacted in 2019, says the word “minerals” does not include fossils unless a deed or other instrument clearly states otherwise. A landowner can sever fossil rights from the land through a clear and express grant.

Sotheby’s offered Jodi with full rights and documentation certifying its condition, authenticity and the legality of its ownership.

The federal Paleontological Resources Preservation Act of 2009 does not apply to state, private or Indian lands, according to the Bureau of Land Management.
