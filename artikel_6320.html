Do You Trust Your Own Intuition? Here’s How You Sharpen It—And Win at Life, Researchers Say

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

It’s all about honing your instincts through practice and experience.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Think about the phrase “it’s just like riding a bicycle.” At first, it’s hard, and you have to pay attention to what you’re doing, but after you’ve practiced for many hours, bike riding becomes so natural you don’t even have to think about it; you just hop on and go. In fact, you can hardly explain how you learned in the first place or exactly how you’re keeping your balance as you pedal on. You just trust your body to do it. The same applies to cognitive activities: As a child, you had to really think about how to arrive at the answer of 2+2, but now, you just know it’s four, without thinking.

Intuition is about finding the solution automatically and without deep thought, says Wim De Neys, a cognitive psychologist and CNRS research professor at the University of Paris. At its core, your intuition is a behavior your brain develops based on patterns and experiences over time, a kind of automation for something you’ve done countless times. That’s why intuition is often linked to expertise. Trusting your gut may sound ill-advised, so why wouldn’t you want to think things through? But it turns out that generating your instincts for decision-making can both help you make the right choice in times of confusion and stress, and also free up mental resources for other tasks.

So, can you train your intuition to sense better choices?

Well, it’s not that simple, according to De Neys, whose research focuses on how intuitive and deliberate thinking develops over our lifetimes. You can’t sidestep the deep reflection needed to build the foundation of your intuitions. But you can tap into them in a more meaningful way, especially over time.

“Having gut feelings and intuition and even valid intuitions, there’s science to back it up,” De Neys says. “It exists, but it’s always tied to expertise, and expertise means it’s something that you’ve done a lot.”

Intuition isn’t some overall sense; instead it’s tied to one area of expertise, like an interviewer being able to pick up on the candidate who’ll be the right fit for the job, or an experienced doctor delivering a diagnosis. While you won’t have expert intuition about everything under the sun, there are a few ways you can boost your own decision-making.

First, you need to get better at pattern matching, and that means practice. Intuition is tied to pattern-matching, Jeff Larsen, PhD, a professor of psychology at the University of Tennessee, explains. For example, chess masters earn their distinction by practicing so much that when they see the board, they don’t have to stop and think about what the two or three best moves will be. Others with less expertise may watch a game and think, “that was a silly move,” but they don’t realize the master player has spent years building up pattern recognition, or an accurate gut feeling, Larsen explains.

Second, stop second-guessing your intuition. You can do this by asking yourself, “How might I be wrong?” One approach is to ask a friend who may disagree with you or might have an outside perspective. This can also help you understand that, often, an instinctual choice you made in the heat of the moment is correct. Even if the choice isn’t exactly right, it’s probably good enough to keep you safe. For example, when you’re driving down the interstate, you have some intuition about whether you can make a lane change or not, and whether you need to slow down or speed up to avoid an accident. Thinking about the consequences of your decision-making can also help give you more confidence in your intuition.

Sometimes you have to trust your gut on a truly life-changing decision. When it comes to marriage, the information overload can sometimes feel overwhelming. However, when it comes to close relationships, you’ve presumably experienced many behavioral exchanges with your partner, and this allows for substantial information-gathering to “feed your gut,” Michael Olson, PhD, a professor of psychology at the University of Tennessee, explains.

Since you can’t possibly keep this massive load of information in your conscious memory at all times, it translates into a neat summary of all those behavioral exchanges. Now you have a gut feeling that this person is right for you—or not.

And the research agrees. A study Olson coauthored, published in Science, found that the “explicit,” or “conscious” evaluations couples in the study made of their partners while in the so-called initial “honeymoon stage” didn’t mean as much when stretched over four years. Instead, those who reported positive gut feelings over this time period had a more accurate idea of their happiness in their relationship.

Your gut doesn’t just extend to your love life. Trusting it can also help you to be successful in business. Elizabeth Rea, an evidentiary clairvoyant psychic medium, now teaches established founders and CEOs to tap into their own intuitions, through her company, Insightful. As a medium, Rea has also assisted U.S. federal special agents investigating cold murder cases.

When working with clients, she asks them to relax what they consciously believe. Sometimes so-called gut feelings may come to us in the form of chills down our spine or another physical sensation. But since we’re living in a left-brained culture that prizes making decisions using logic and analysis, those instincts often get passed off as coincidences and ignored, Rea says. Sometimes, gut feelings may also be ignored because of self-doubt. Your intuition often whispers to you, she tells clients. Honing these signals will help you improve your self-trust—thereby improving your intuition.

Overall, training yourself to trust your intuition will help you become a better decision-maker. You’ll make the right choices without the agonizing process of picking through pros and cons and running scenarios in your brain for days on end.

“It’s a lot more cost-efficient if we can offload [decision-making] to our intuition,” De Neys says. “Often that’s going to be helpful because then it frees up resources for something else that might be more important at that point in time.”
