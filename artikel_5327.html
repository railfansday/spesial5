Squeezed by rising costs, Gen Z is still buying wellness and lip gloss

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Gen Z is still spending. It’s just far more deliberate about where the money goes, and beauty and wellness keep making the cut, executives from Ulta Beauty, Pacsun, and Upstart said at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit on Tuesday.

The squeeze on young consumers is real. Upstart cofounder and CEO Paul Gu told Fortune‘s Ellie Austin that the economy looks strong on paper, with record retail sales, a solid job market, and stocks near all-time highs. The catch for people in their twenties, he said, is simple: “Stuff costs too much money.”

That pressure shows up in the data. Deloitte’s 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey found that 55% of Gen Z are delaying major life decisions, such as starting a family or launching a business, because of their finances.

Credit is part of the problem. “We see that something like nearly 60% of all Gen Z consumers just don’t even bother to apply for credit because they assume they’re going to be rejected,” said Gu, who took over as CEO of the AI lending company in May. “I think they’re basically right. You can’t really go to a bank without a long credit history and expect to get approved for a meaningful amount of credit when you need it… That number, by the way, is almost three times as high as what you would see for older Americans.”

For retailers, that squeeze hasn’t meant cheaper baskets. Ulta Beauty president and CEO Kecia Steelman, No. 39 on Fortune‘s 2026 Most Powerful Women list, said young shoppers aren’t choosing between feeling secure and treating themselves. “It’s a little bit of a mix of both,” she said.

“We don’t see the consumer trading down, and we don’t see this segment of the consumer demographic trading down either,” Steelman said. “Value is the perception of: Is it worth my investment in the dollars that I have?… They’re being a lot more conservative, but I just happen to be in a great category that they’re choosing. They’re prioritizing beauty and wellness.”

The numbers back her up. Ulta, a Fortune 500 company with $12.4 billion in revenue last fiscal year, raised its full-year guidance in August after second-quarter comparable sales rose 3.8%. That tracks with what Bank of America has called Gen Z’s “treat culture”: small, frequent purchases that many young people see as necessities.

Pacsun CEO Brieane Olson sees the same discipline. Young shoppers put “a lot more intentionality behind purchase than we’ve seen in the past,” she said, calling their decisions “purposeful and intentional.”

Olson pointed to Pacsun’s annual youth research, which found that wellness “ranks at the very highest in terms of what they are trying to achieve.” In the 2026 edition, conducted with GlobalData and published in September, 59% of more than 6,000 respondents aged 11 to 24 named being mentally healthy as a top goal by age 35.

Social media may spark interest, but both CEOs said purchases still tend to happen in person. Ulta has 47 million loyalty members, and 75% of its sales come from people who shop only in stores, Steelman said. Just 5% come from shoppers who buy only online. Gen Z also makes up 65% of Ulta’s store associates.

“TikTok has definitely changed the way that things can go viral in a very short period of time. But it’s not the thing that’s really driving the Gen Z consumer,” Steelman said. “I think that’s where they have the discovery. But human connection is still what makes them make the decision.”

Olson said Pacsun is seeing “a large return to in-person shopping” among 16- to 24-year-olds. The retailer also gives young customers a direct say through its youth advisory council. A 13-year-old member recently suggested an after-hours shopping event for friends, Olson said, and it grossed close to what Pacsun’s stores bring in per hour on Black Friday.

For companies that want Gen Z’s business, Gu said the starting point is to stop turning them away. Young people apply for credit and get rejected, he said. “This is like the daily experience of young consumers, and that is no way to build a relationship with them.”

Allie Garfinkle is a senior writer and editor at Fortune, where she runs Term Sheet; leads coverage of private capital, investors, and startups; and co-chairs the Brainstorm conference series.
