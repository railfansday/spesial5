Submillimetre hot-electron transport in manganese-doped quantum dot solids

Colloidal quantum dot solids are promising for photoconversion and photochemistry, but their performance is fundamentally limited by rapid hot-carrier cooling, low carrier mobility and short transport ranges1. Here we demonstrate a spin-exchange-driven phototransport regime in manganese-doped quantum dot films that enables the generation, long-range transport and regeneration of hot carriers. Ultrafast spin-exchange-mediated Auger upconversion promotes electrons into high-energy, delocalized states before phonon cooling2,3, establishing a transient high-mobility conduction channel. Crucially, spin-exchange interactions not only generate hot carriers but also enable their repeated re-excitation, thereby sustaining long-range drift. This mechanism yields room-temperature mobilities of about 150 cm2 V−1 s−1 and submillimetre drift lengths—far greater than those of conventional quantum dot solids and approaching values characteristic of crystalline semiconductors. Time-of-flight measurements confirm drift-dominated transport, with transit times scaling linearly with distance and inversely with electric field. These results establish a route to long-range energy transport in nanoscale materials and enable the use of hot carriers in photoconversion, photochemistry and transit-time-limited photodetection.

Photoconversion processes underpin technologies ranging from photodetection and photovoltaics to photoemission and photochemistry. Their ability to exploit excess photon energy is limited by rapid phonon-assisted cooling of nonequilibrium (‘hot’) carriers to band-edge states, typically within picoseconds, severely restricting their lifetime and transport range. Stabilizing and transporting hot carriers therefore remains a substantial challenge for advanced solar-energy conversion4,5,6,7 and high-energy photochemistry8,9.

Recent studies have shown that hot carriers can be manipulated using ultrafast spin exchange (SE) interactions in manganese (Mn)-doped colloidal quantum dots (QDs)10,11,12. These interactions mediate subpicosecond Auger-type energy transfer between QD excitons and Mn spin states, reaching energy-transfer rates of about 10 eV ps−1 (ref. 11), well above typical phonon-related energy-loss rates (≲1 eV ps−1)13,14. By outcompeting intraband cooling, SE-Auger processes provide an efficient pathway for harvesting hot-electron energy, enabling phenomena such as visible-light generation of solvated electrons2,15 and carrier multiplication12.

Here we demonstrate that SE-Auger upconversion in Mn-doped QD films promotes photoelectrons into high-energy, delocalized transport states, enabling rapid, long-range hot-carrier transport through the QD solid. Transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements reveal time-of-flight (ToF) dynamics with drift mobilities of about 150 cm2 V−1 s−1 and submillimetre transport distances—well beyond those typically observed in QD solids. These results establish a route to long-range hot-carrier transport for photoconversion and hot-electron-driven photochemistry.

We prepared undoped (reference) and Mn-doped CdSe QDs overcoated with a thin protective CdS shell (Methods). We focus on CdSe/CdS QDs with an initial core radius of rCdSe ≈ 1.65 nm. The undoped sample exhibits a photoluminescence (PL) peak at about 2.15 eV and a band-edge (1S) absorption feature at about 2.23 eV (Fig. 1a, black curves).

Mn-doped samples were synthesized by diffusion doping of the CdSe cores, involving partial Cd2+ → Mn2+ cation exchange near the surface followed by Mn diffusion into the core interior16,17 (Methods). The Mn content was controlled by the doping time (tMn = 5–30 min). Here we examine samples prepared with tMn = 10 and 20 min, referred to as ‘lightly’ and ‘heavily’ doped QDs, respectively.

The lightly doped QDs show spectral features similar to those of the undoped reference (Fig. 1a, blue curves), indicating no appreciable change in the effective CdSe core size. The heavily doped sample exhibits an approximately 130-meV blue shift of both PL and absorption (Fig. 1a, red curves), consistent with a reduction of the effective CdSe core radius to about 1.35 nm owing to formation of a higher-bandgap MnxCd1−xSe surface layer.

Mn incorporation strongly modifies single-exciton PL dynamics measured at low excitation levels (⟨N⟩ < 0.1, in which ⟨N⟩ is the average number of excitons generated per QD per pulse). The PL lifetime increases from τPL,0 = 35 ns in undoped QDs to τPL,Mn = 61 ns in lightly doped QDs and to an average of 225 ns in heavily doped QDs (Fig. 1b).

This behaviour arises from ultrafast (subpicosecond) bidirectional SE-mediated excitation transfer between the QD exciton (X) and the internal Mn spin-flip transition (EMn = 2.1 eV)2,10,18. In the forward process, the QD exciton relaxes while promoting the Mn ion from the spin-up 6A1 ground state to the spin-down 4T1 state (Fig. 1b, inset). This occurs through two concerted spin-conserving transfers: a spin-up Mn 3d electron fills the QD valence-band (VB) hole, while a spin-down QD conduction-band (CB) electron is simultaneously transferred into the Mn 3d shell. In the reverse process, spin-flip relaxation of the excited Mn ion (Mn*) regenerates a QD exciton.

The rapid forward and backward transfers establish a quasi-steady-state distribution between excitonic and Mn-based states, with occupation probabilities pX and pMn related by pX/pMn = exp[−(ΕX − ΕMn)/δ0], in which ΕX is the exciton energy (determined from the PL peak energy) and δ0 = 103 meV is an experimentally determined characteristic energy3,11. Because the intrinsic Mn* lifetime is very long (τMn ≳ 1 ms; ref. 18), decay of the coupled X–Mn system is governed by its excitonic component, 1/τPL,Mn ≈ pX/τPL,0.

For the lightly doped sample, in which EMn ≈ EX, pX ≈ pMn, giving τPL,Mn ≈ 2τPL,0 ≈ 70 ns, close to the measured 61 ns. For the heavily doped sample (ΕX − ΕMn = 188 meV), the model yields τPL,Mn ≈ 7.2τPL,0 ≈ 252 ns, again close to the observed 225 ns. The prolonged lifetime of the coupled X–Mn excitation facilitates multiexciton formation and thereby supports the SE-Auger processes underlying the long-range hot-electron transport discussed below.

Although slowing single-exciton decay, Mn doping greatly accelerates multiexciton dynamics, which in undoped QDs are governed by Auger recombination, for which electron–hole recombination energy is transferred to a third carrier within the same QD19 (Fig. 1c, bottom inset). In the undoped sample, this process appears as a fast decay component in transient absorption (TA) traces (Methods), emerging near ⟨N⟩ ≈ 1 (Fig. 1c). From this component, we extract a biexciton Auger lifetime of τA = 85 ps (Fig. 1c, top inset).

In Mn-doped QDs, the TA decay also accelerates near ⟨N⟩ ≈ 1 (Fig. 1d, lightly doped sample), but the corresponding time constant is nearly two orders of magnitude shorter, about 910 fs, and decreases further to about 230 fs at higher excitation levels (Fig. 1d, top inset). These subpicosecond dynamics arise from SE-Auger recombination of hybrid multiexciton states comprising intrinsic QD excitons and excited Mn ions2,3. In the electron re-excitation pathway (Fig. 1d, bottom inset), two concerted spin-transfer events promote a spin-down Mn 3d electron into a hot QD CB state, whereas the Mn ion is restored to its ground-state configuration by transfer of a spin-up electron from the QD band-edge CB state. As a result, the hot electron is elevated above the QD band edge by EMn.

Previous studies established that the SE-Auger rate depends mainly on exciton multiplicity and only weakly on the number of excited Mn ions3. Accordingly, the observed time constants of 910 and 230 fs are consistent with SE-Auger decay of hybrid biexciton (X–Mn*) and triexciton (XX–Mn*) states, respectively3.

A key consequence is the very high energy-gain rate associated with SE-Auger upconversion (rgain). For τSE-A = 230 fs, rgain = rSE-A = EMn/τSE-A ≈ 9 eV ps−1, far exceeding typical phonon-assisted energy-loss rates (rphon ≲ 1 eV ps−1)13,14. Thus, SE-Auger upconversion can outcompete phonon cooling and efficiently generate and manipulate hot carriers.

A high SE-Auger upconversion rate could enable Auger-assisted hot-carrier transport with strongly enhanced photoconductance. Specifically, theory predicts that Auger excitation above the QD ionization threshold can promote electrons into delocalized, ‘quasi-free’ states with greatly increased mobility20. Experimentally, short-lived bursts of enhanced carrier mobility were previously observed in undoped PbSe QD films21 and attributed to Auger-generated hot electrons that remained confined within individual QDs. The corresponding transport range was, however, limited to the approximately 50–100-nm near-contact region. Here we use ultrafast SE-Auger interactions to promote electrons above the confinement barrier, as originally foreseen in ref. 20, and show that this enables submillimetre transport.

The concept of Auger-enhanced conductivity is illustrated in Fig. 2a. If an electron is excited above the QD confinement barrier—that is, effectively ejected from the QD—it can access a high-mobility transport band formed by closely spaced, delocalized peripheral states22. In typical CdSe QDs, however, direct ejection of a band-edge electron by a single conventional Auger upconversion step is unfavourable because the transferred energy, approximately equal to the bandgap Eg, is smaller than the confinement barrier. Ejection can still occur from higher-lying transient states (Fig. 2a) but rapid phonon cooling usually returns carriers to the band edge before Auger upconversion can take place.

Owing to its ultrafast character, SE-Auger upconversion can overcome this limitation by ejecting a nonthermalized electron before it loses its excess energy to phonons11. Band-edge electrons can also be expelled from the QD through two sequential SE-Auger upconversion steps2.

To investigate the role of SE interactions in phototransport, we performed TPC measurements using Auston switch devices in which undoped or Mn-doped QD films were incorporated into a 70-µm gap of a 50-Ω microstrip transmission line21,23,24,25 (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 1; Methods). The QDs were excited by 150-fs laser pulses focused into a narrow stripe (70–80 µm full width at half maximum, FWHM), whose position within the gap was varied by translating the sample. The TPC signal was recorded with a 20-GHz sampling oscilloscope, yielding an overall temporal resolution of approximately 50 ps.

Dark-conductivity measurements (Extended Data Fig. 2) show that the QD films are highly resistive at equilibrium, indicating that the TPC response is dominated by photogenerated rather than equilibrium carriers.

In most experiments, the QDs were excited at Ephot = 3.6 eV, more than 1 eV above the band edge. Under these conditions, SE-Auger interactions can efficiently eject electrons from Mn-doped QDs11, whereas ejection by means of slower conventional Auger processes in undoped QDs is much less likely. Thus, any strong enhancement of photoconductance provides a direct signature of SE-Auger-driven hot-carrier generation and transport.

In Fig. 2c,d, we compare pump-intensity-dependent photocurrent transients for undoped and Mn-doped QD films, respectively, measured at a device bias of V = 100 V and Ephot = 3.6 eV.

For the undoped sample, the excitation range corresponds to ⟨N⟩ from 0.23 to 5.38 excitons per QD. Increasing pump fluence does not substantially alter the overall TPC profile; the main effect is an acceleration of the early-time decay at ⟨N⟩ ≳ 1, similar to the behaviour observed in TA measurements (Fig. 1c) and likewise indicative of multiexciton Auger recombination. A biexciton Auger lifetime extracted from the fast TPC component (about 110 ps; Fig. 2c, inset) agrees reasonably well with that obtained from TA (about 85 ps; Fig. 1c, inset).

The ⟨N⟩ dependence of both the early-time TPC peak amplitude (time t = tpeak) and the long-time signal (t = 400 ps > τA) is also well described by the Auger recombination model (Fig. 3a, black symbols and lines). In this model, the peak photocurrent contains contributions from both single-exciton and multiexciton states, whereas after Auger decay, the long-time signal originates only from single excitons (Supplementary Note 1).

These results show that TPC dynamics in undoped QD films are dominated by carrier recombination, with no detectable signatures of transit across the electrode gap. The corresponding transit times are therefore much longer than the characteristic recombination times because of the low carrier mobility.

In contrast to the undoped samples, photocurrent transients from Mn-doped QD films change markedly with increasing pump fluence (Fig. 2d, heavily doped sample). At ⟨N⟩ ≳ 1, the TPC traces develop a pronounced step-like component whose amplitude grows approximately cubically with ⟨N⟩ (IPC,400ps ∝ ⟨N⟩3.05; Fig. 2d, inset), where IPC,400ps is the photocurrent amplitude measured at t = 400 ps after excitation. This superlinear growth cannot be explained by the increase in carrier population alone and instead indicates an increase in carrier mobility by orders of magnitude.

The onset of this rapid photocurrent growth coincides with the threshold for SE-Auger recombination, suggesting SE-Auger-driven activation of electrons into a higher-energy transport band above the QD confinement barrier, as proposed in ref. 20. The absence of this effect in undoped QDs further points to the critical role of ultrafast SE-Auger upconversion, which can intercept an energetic photoelectron before intraband cooling and promote it into a peripheral, delocalized state outside the QD (Fig. 3b).

The excitation-level dependence of the TPC step amplitude (evaluated at t = 400 ps) further supports its SE-Auger origin. As detailed in Supplementary Note 2, the data are well described using the previously established (n − 1)2 scaling of the SE-Auger rate with exciton multiplicity n (Fig. 3a, red symbols and line)3.

Further evidence for electron ejection from the QD is provided by pump-fluence-dependent TA spectra (Fig. 3c). At ⟨N⟩ < 1, the spectra are dominated by a state-filling bleach at 2.34 eV, whereas at ⟨N⟩ ≳ 1, they evolve into a derivative-like lineshape characteristic of electron ejection11,26. The resulting spatial separation of the ejected electron and the hole remaining in the QD generates an internal electric field (Fig. 3c, inset) that redshifts the band-edge transition through the Stark effect, producing induced absorption below the original bleach.

A step-like TPC signal with an abrupt cut-off is a hallmark of ToF photocurrent observed in high-mobility photoconductors, in which photocarriers are rapidly swept from the photoconductive gap by drift towards the electrodes27,28,29. In this regime, the photocurrent duration is determined not by carrier relaxation but by the carrier transit time, ttr = d/vd = d/(μF), in which d is the transport distance, vd the drift velocity, μ the carrier mobility and F the electric field.

To test for ToF transport in Mn-doped QD films, we measured photocurrent transients as a function of both transport distance and electric field, F = V/L, in which L = 70 μm is the electrode gap. Figure 4a shows TPC traces for heavily doped QDs at three distances from the positive electrode, d = 15, 45 and 65 µm. The TPC pulse duration increases systematically with d, a defining signature of carrier transit27 and evidence that the photoresponse is dominated by high-mobility hot electrons generated by SE-Auger upconversion2,30,31.

The complementary holes remain largely confined within the CdSe cores and are not expected to contribute appreciably to the fast TPC response. However, we do not detect persistent charge accumulation in the photoconductive gap, probably because the non-zero dark conductivity of the QD films (Extended Data Fig. 2) enables charge neutralization between successive excitation pulses.

The measured TPC profiles are well described by the error-function form of the ToF expression for a Gaussian photoexcitation profile (dashed lines in Fig. 4a; Supplementary Note 3 and Extended Data Fig. 3). Minor deviations include an initial capacitive spike29,32 and a weak long-time tail associated with delayed electrons, discussed below.

Modelling of the d = 15 μm trace yields a drift velocity of vd = 24 μm ns−1 and an effective lifetime τ0 = 7 ns. In conventional ToF models, τ0 describes recombination and/or trapping of mobile carriers. Here it instead represents the effective lifetime of the high-mobility hot-electron population. Whereas hot electrons normally cool to the band edge within a few picoseconds14,33, in Mn-doped QDs, their lifetime is extended by SE-Auger regeneration (Fig. 4b). Long-lived Mn excitations persist during carrier transit and can repeatedly re-excite partially relaxed electrons into the transport band before complete cooling. Regeneration is imperfect, however: if a relaxing electron encounters a QD lacking an excited Mn ion or otherwise unable to support SE-Auger re-excitation, it relaxes into a low-mobility band-edge state. Thus, τ0 represents the average survival time of the regenerated hot-electron population.

ToF fits at longer transport distances yield an apparent decrease in vd from 24 μm ns−1 at d = 15 μm to 13 μm ns−1 at d = 65 μm, whereas τ0 remains essentially unchanged (Fig. 4a). We attribute this reduction to the increasing contribution of delayed electrons that progressively lose mobility as regeneration becomes less effective, broadening the trailing edge of the TPC transient. The shortest-distance value, vd = 24 μm ns−1, therefore provides the best estimate of the intrinsic drift velocity of the regenerated hot-electron population.

We also estimate the transit time with the commonly used empirical approach, in which ttr is identified from the inflection point of a log–log TPC plot, determined from the intersection of power-law fits to the pre-transit and post-transit regions27,28,34,35 (Fig. 5a). The resulting ttr values scale linearly with d (Fig. 4a, inset), as expected for ToF transport. The slope yields vd = 21.5 ± 0.4 µm ns−1, in good agreement with the value obtained from error-function modelling of the d = 15 μm trace (vd = 24 µm ns−1).

At a lower bias (V = 70 V), ttr again scales linearly with d (Fig. 4a, inset and Extended Data Fig. 4), yielding vd = 15.3 ± 0.4 µm ns−1. The ratio of drift velocities at 70 V and 100 V (0.71) closely matches the voltage ratio (0.7), confirming the expected linear dependence of vd on electric field. Bias-dependent measurements at fixed distances (d = 45 µm and 25 µm; Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 5) provide an independent test: ttr increases with decreasing bias following the expected 1/V dependence (Fig. 5c).

Figure 5d summarizes all distance-dependent and bias-dependent measurements (25 data points) by plotting ttr versus d/F, with the horizontal axis scaled by a factor of 10 for convenient unit conversion, as described in the figure caption. For ToF transport, ttr = d/vd = (1/µ)(d/F), so the data should follow a straight line with slope 1/µ. The experimental data indeed collapse onto a single linear trend (Fig. 5d, black line), yielding an average electron mobility of ⟨µe⟩ = 150 ± 11 cm2 V−1 s−1, consistent with values obtained independently from the distance-dependent and bias-dependent data (Fig. 4a, inset and Fig. 5c).

The ToF behaviour is reproducible across independently synthesized QD batches and several Auston switch devices. Further measurements yield electron mobilities of around 150–170 cm2 V−1 s−1 (Extended Data Fig. 6), confirming the robustness of the high-mobility hot-electron transport regime.

Although the present study focuses on 3.6-eV excitation, long-range hot-electron transport is also observed under near-band-edge excitation at 2.4 eV (Extended Data Fig. 7). In this case, the onset of the rapid TPC increase shifts from ⟨N⟩ ≈ 1 to ⟨N⟩ ≈ 2, consistent with the SE-Auger ionization model. Because ejection of a near-band-edge electron requires two sequential SE-Auger upconversion steps, ionization occurs only once three excitons are generated, in agreement with previous observations of SE-driven photoionization2. Above this threshold, the transport characteristics are essentially unchanged: bias-dependent and distance-dependent TPC measurements exhibit the same ToF behaviour and yield ⟨μe⟩ = 164 cm2 V−1 s−1, close to the value obtained under 3.6-eV excitation.

The measured drift mobility, ⟨μe⟩ ≈ 150 cm2 V−1 s−1, is about an order of magnitude higher than values typically reported for strongly coupled, device-grade QD films (10–20 cm2 V−1 s−1) (refs. 1,36) and approaches the room-temperature electron drift mobilities of bulk wide-bandgap II–VI semiconductors (approximately 200–600 cm2 V−1 s−1) (ref. 37). This comparison serves only as a benchmark of mobility magnitude and does not imply equivalent transport mechanisms or device operation.

The high hot-electron mobility also enables long transport distances. Using τ0 = 7 ns and vd = 21.5 µm ns−1 (V = 100 V) gives a drift range of about 0.15 mm. Such transport distances are extraordinary for colloidal QD solids and place Mn-doped QD films in a transport regime more commonly associated with crystalline semiconductors than with ligand-linked nanocrystal assemblies. Collectively, these results demonstrate a high-mobility transport regime in which SE-Auger excitation promotes photoelectrons into a delocalized hot-carrier transport band, whereas repeated SE-Auger regeneration sustains their long-range drift (Fig. 4b).

We report a phototransport regime in which SE-Auger upconversion dynamically promotes electrons into a delocalized, high-mobility band above the QD confinement barrier, yielding carrier mobilities of about 150 cm2 V−1 s−1 and submillimetre transport distances. Unlike conventional band-like transport, this mechanism does not rely on miniband formation or strong electronic coupling between neighbouring QDs. Instead, repeated SE-Auger activation regenerates high-energy transport states following partial relaxation, sustaining hot-electron transport over nanosecond timescales.

Long-range hot-electron transport can enable transit-time-limited photodetection in the GHz regime, ultrafast optical-to-electrical signal conversion and modulation and electrical sampling of ultrafast optical signals. Beyond optoelectronics, SE-enabled stabilization and transport of hot carriers offer a strategy for harnessing high-energy electrons in photochemical processes. Their high mobility, expanded wavefunctions and strong reducing power make them attractive as both energetic charge carriers and agents of remote chemical transformations.

Cadmium oxide (CdO, 99.99%, Aldrich), octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA, 99%, PCI), trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO, 99%, Aldrich), trioctylphosphine (TOP, 97%, Aldrich), diphenylphosphine (DPP, 98%, Aldrich), selenium (Se, 200 mesh, 99.999%, Alfa Aesar), 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%, Aldrich), oleic acid (OA, 90%, Aldrich), oleylamine (OAm, 70%, Aldrich), manganese(II) acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(ac)2·4H2O, 99.999%, Strem) and tributylphosphine (TBP, 95%, Aldrich) were used as received.

CdSe core particles with a wurtzite crystal structure were synthesized following a previously reported method17. Specifically, 100 mg of CdO, 490 mg of ODPA and 5 g of TOPO were loaded into a three-neck flask and degassed under vacuum at 150 °C for 1 h. Under nitrogen flow, the mixture was heated to 320 °C and maintained at this temperature until it formed a clear solution. Subsequently, 1 ml of TOP was swiftly injected into the flask. After the temperature increased to 380 °C, 1 ml of a 1.4 M TOP–Se solution was rapidly injected. At this stage, 1 mol% of DPP was added to the TOP–Se solution to control the growth kinetics17. After 3 min, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature by removing the heating mantle.

The resulting nanocrystals were collected by adding ethanol followed by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm. This washing procedure was repeated three times. The purified nanocrystals were then redispersed in toluene.

To incorporate Mn into the synthesized CdSe nanocrystals, we used a diffusion-doping procedure16 following a refined protocol of ref. 17. To remove residual Cd species, the pre-synthesized CdSe nanocrystals dispersed in toluene were precipitated with ethanol. 75 mg of the precipitate was dissolved in a mixture of 27 ml of degassed ODE and 2.7 ml of OA. The mixture was heated to 60 °C under vacuum for 1 h. After cooling to room temperature, the nanocrystals were again precipitated with ethanol and collected by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm. The resulting precipitate was added to a mixture of 510 mg of Se, 3 ml of degassed ODE and 3 ml of TBP.

Separately, 90 mg of Mn(ac)2·4H2O, 45 ml of ODE, 1.8 ml of OA and 3.9 ml of OAm were loaded into a flask and degassed under vacuum at 100 °C for 1 h. Under nitrogen flow, the mixture was heated to 300 °C, after which the previously prepared CdSe nanocrystal/TBP–Se solution in ODE was swiftly injected. The reaction temperature was maintained at 300 °C and the doping level was controlled by the reaction time (typically 5–30 min). After completion of the reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature. Magnetic circular dichroism measurements indicated approximately 2–5 internal Mn2+ dopants per QD, depending on the doping time17.

Owing to the large abundance of surface trap states, as-prepared Mn-doped CdSe cores do not exhibit detectable PL. To suppress surface defects, the cores were coated with a CdS shell following procedures reported in ref. 17.

To prepare a Cd-oleate precursor solution, 123 mg of CdO, 2.45 ml of OA and 10.8 ml of ODE were loaded into a flask and degassed at 100 °C under vacuum for 1 h. Under nitrogen flow, the temperature was increased to 250 °C and maintained for 1 h until a clear solution formed. The precursor solution was then cooled to 100 °C and maintained at this temperature until injection during CdS shell growth.

A TOP–S precursor solution was prepared by stirring a mixture of 18 ml of TOP and 44.3 mg of sulfur powder overnight in a glovebox.

For shell growth, 75 mg of Mn-doped CdSe QDs were dissolved in a mixture of 28 ml of ODE, 2.4 ml of OAm and 1.2 ml of OA. This mixture was degassed at 100 °C under vacuum for 1 h. Under nitrogen flow, the flask was heated to 220 °C and 0.4-ml aliquots of the Cd-oleate and TOP–S precursor solutions were alternately injected dropwise at intervals of 2.5 min for two cycles.

A 100-nm gold layer was evaporated onto the backside of a 1-mm glass substrate to serve as the ground plane. An approximately 200-nm-thick film of undoped (reference) or Mn-doped CdSe/CdS QDs was then deposited on the top surface of the substrate through repeated spin-coating cycles, accompanied by ligand exchange and rinsing steps. During deposition, QDs dispersed in octane (20 mg ml−1) were spin-coated at 1,600 rpm for 30 s.

To facilitate interdot charge transport, the QDs were cross-linked by means of solid-state ligand exchange, replacing the original long-chain ligands with short bifunctional 1,2-ethanedithiol (EDT) molecules. For ligand exchange, 60 µl of a 1 M EDT solution in methanol (MeOH) was dropped onto the QD layer, allowed to soak for 60 s and then spin-coated at 1,200 rpm for 30 s.

The film was subsequently rinsed by spin-coating pure MeOH to remove excess ligands. These three steps—QD deposition, ligand exchange and rinsing—were repeated five times to obtain a QD layer approximately 200 nm thick. The resulting film was annealed at 150 °C for 30 min.

Finally, two 200-nm-thick gold contacts separated by a 70-µm gap were deposited on top of the QD film through a shadow mask. Together with the gold ground plane on the backside of the substrate, these contacts form a microstrip transmission line with a nominal impedance of 50 Ω.

To verify that ligand exchange and film fabrication preserved the quantum-confined electronic structure of the QDs, we performed further TA measurements on 200-nm-thick Mn-doped CdSe/CdS QD films prepared using the same ligand-exchange procedure as used for the TPC devices. The TA spectra of the film samples were compared with those of the corresponding QDs in solution under identical excitation conditions (3.6-eV, 150-fs excitation; ⟨N⟩ = 0.3). The results are presented in Extended Data Fig. 8.

To characterize the equilibrium electrical properties of the ligand-exchanged QD films, further dark-conductivity measurements were performed using 120-nm-thick Mn-doped CdSe/CdS QD films deposited on glass substrates and contacted by thermally evaporated interdigitated Au electrodes comprising 25 pairs of 2-mm-long fingers separated by 10 μm. The corresponding current–voltage characteristics are presented in Extended Data Fig. 2.

Electrostatic simulations of the Auston switch geometry were performed using the Electrostatics interface of COMSOL Multiphysics to calculate the electric-field distribution, effective gap capacitance and effective RC constant (Extended Data Fig. 1).

TA measurements were performed using a standard pump–probe set-up in which absorption changes induced by a short pump pulse were probed in both the temporal and spectral domains using variably delayed probe pulses. The TA system was built around a regeneratively amplified ytterbium-doped potassium gadolinium tungstate (Yb:KGW) femtosecond laser (PHAROS, Light Conversion) generating approximately 190-fs pulses at 1.2 eV.

The laser output was split into two beams. One beam seeded a harmonic generator (HIRO HHG, Light Conversion) to produce second (2.4-eV) or third (3.6-eV) harmonic pump pulses, whose duration was shortened to about 150 fs owing to nonlinear optical pulse compression. Pulses from the second beam were delayed relative to the pump using an optical delay line (up to 5 ns) and focused onto a 3-mm-thick sapphire plate to generate broadband probe pulses in the form of a femtosecond white-light continuum.

The pump beam was focused onto the sample into a spot with a diameter of about 120 µm, whereas the smaller-diameter probe beam (<90 µm) was centred near the middle of the pump spot. Probe pulses transmitted through the sample were detected using an Avantes AvaSpec-Fast ULS1350F-USB2 spectrometer. The measured TA spectra were corrected for chirp (spectrally dependent delay within the white-light continuum probe pulses) using the procedure described in ref. 2.

In Auger recombination studies of undoped and Mn-doped QDs, we monitored the pump-induced band-edge absorption bleach (ΔαBE), which tracks the decay of band-edge excitonic populations14.

To minimize sample degradation during TPC measurements, the fabricated Auston switch devices were mounted in a nitrogen-filled, airtight chamber equipped with optical windows and electrical feedthroughs. Under these conditions, no measurable changes in the TPC response were observed during the complete characterization of a device, including pump-fluence-dependent, transport-distance-dependent and bias-dependent measurements.

The chamber was mounted on a translation stage, allowing the laser beam position within the electrode gap to be varied. One of the top gold electrodes was biased using a DC power supply (Keithley 2430), whereas the other electrode was connected to a 20-GHz-bandwidth sampling oscilloscope by means of a coaxial cable. To minimize timing jitter, the oscilloscope was triggered by electrical pulses generated by a high-speed Si photodetector driven by a portion of the laser beam split from the excitation path.

The QDs within the photoconductive gap of the Auston switch were excited at 2.4 eV or 3.6 eV using 150-fs pulses of the second or third harmonic of an amplified Yb:KGW femtosecond PHAROS laser, respectively. The laser beam was focused with a cylindrical achromatic lens into a narrow stripe aligned with the edges of the top gold electrodes. The spatial profile of the excitation beam was approximately Gaussian, with a stripe width of about 70–80 µm (FWHM), measured with a beam profiler. The excitation position within the photoconductive gap of the Auston switch was varied by translating the sample in a plane perpendicular to the laser beam. The position was initially calibrated using a beam profiler and monitored in real time on the sample with a CMOS camera.

As discussed in Supplementary Note 3, the nonlinear dependence of SE-Auger upconversion on excitation density narrows the lateral spatial profile of the mobile hot-electron population relative to the laser beam profile (Extended Data Fig. 9). Across the film thickness, the initial hot-electron population is weighted towards the illuminated surface. However, because the optical absorption length is comparable with the QD film thickness, excited Mn ions are generated throughout the layer. Consequently, SE-Auger regeneration remains active across the film thickness, even if the hot-electron population spreads vertically during transit within the photoconductive gap.

The vertical electric field is negligible over nearly the entire photoconductive gap and becomes appreciable only within approximately 1 μm of the electrode edges. By contrast, the horizontal electric field remains nearly uniform across the gap, with an average value within 10–15% of the nominal field F = V/L. These calculations confirm that the electric field is predominantly horizontal, thereby validating the one-dimensional drift approximation used in the charge transport analysis. The computed electrostatic field distribution yields an effective gap capacitance of approximately 0.2 pF, corresponding to an estimated RC time constant of 10 ps for a 50-Ω transmission line. This value is shorter than the experimental temporal resolution of the TPC measurements (about 50 ps), indicating that the measured response is limited not by the device RC constant but by other components of the measurement circuit and the oscilloscope.

The measurements were performed on a 120-nm-thick Mn-doped CdSe/CdS QD film deposited on a glass substrate and contacted by thermally evaporated interdigitated Au electrodes comprising 25 pairs of 2-mm-long fingers separated by 10 μm.
