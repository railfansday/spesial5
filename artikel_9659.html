My Laptop's Wi-Fi Got Better When I Disabled 6 GHz

My laptop could get online, but the Wi-Fi signal was weak. The devices right around it seemed fine. That was the frustrating part. I had a problem I could see, and everything I would normally check first seemed to be working.

I wanted to get back to work. “Is there a flush or something?” I asked.

I tried a quick Wi-Fi reset on the laptop first. I wanted to see whether the weak signal was a one-off that would clear on a fresh connection. It came back just as weak. I could reproduce the problem without touching the router.

There is a Windows command that sounds like the thing I was asking for. ipconfig /flushdns clears cached DNS lookups. I’d try it if the Wi-Fi link looked healthy and the laptop was having trouble looking up website addresses. Here I could already see a problem with the wireless link. Microsoft’s ipconfig reference

I opened the app for my eero mesh network and checked the devices near the laptop. Full bars on the others. If several of them had been struggling in the same spot, I would have looked at coverage and the nearest mesh node first. One weak connection gave me a reason to look at how that particular laptop had joined.

There are two ways to narrow this down further. Move the laptop close to the access point it’s using and watch its signal. If it improves, check whether it kept the same band and mesh node. That helps separate the effect of moving closer from the effect of joining a different radio. A nearby phone hotspot lets you try the same laptop on another network. If it works there, look for differences in how it joins your home network. If the weak signal follows it, I’d take a closer look at its adapter or driver. Microsoft’s Wi-Fi troubleshooting guide

In the same app, I pulled up the laptop’s connection. It was on 6 GHz. Until then I’d been treating “we’re all on the same Wi-Fi” as an answer. The network name can be the same even when the devices are on different bands. And 6 GHz can have a harder time reaching through walls than 5 GHz. That gave me something concrete to try. How would the laptop do on 5 GHz where I was sitting? Microsoft’s guide to Wi-Fi bands and home layout

You can take the same reading on Windows with netsh wlan show interfaces. Look for the signal and the band, if Windows shows it. If your router app lists the connected band and mesh node, write those down too. You’ll want the same information after you reconnect. Microsoft’s wireless command reference

In Windows I went to Device Manager → Network adapters → my Wi-Fi adapter → Properties → Advanced and set the adapter to prefer 5 GHz. I disconnected, reconnected, and went back to eero to see which band it had picked. Intel’s guide to wireless adapter settings

If I’d stopped at the signal bars, I could have crossed 5 GHz off the list. The preference still left 6 GHz available to the adapter. I needed a way to take it out of the running for this one laptop.

The adapter had a separate setting for 6 GHz. I turned it off on the laptop, reconnected, and checked eero again. This time it was on 5 GHz and the connection worked.

If you get stuck at the same step, check that your network offers 5 GHz and look for a separate 6 GHz setting in the adapter’s Advanced tab. The options depend on the adapter and driver. Write down the original setting so you can put it back, then check which band the laptop actually joins after you reconnect.

My first question when it worked was whether to turn 6 GHz back on. If I wanted to find out whether the problem was repeatable, that would be my next test. I’d record the signal on 5 GHz, turn 6 GHz back on, and check that the laptop actually rejoined it. I’d keep the laptop in the same place and check the mesh node both times. If the weak signal came back on 6 GHz with the same node, I’d have a much better case for leaving it off in that spot.
