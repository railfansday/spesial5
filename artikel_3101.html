The Stock Market Is Flashing a Signal Seen Only 6 Times in 156 Years. History Suggests These Stocks Could Take the Biggest Hit.

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

The stock market is teetering. And these stocks could have the most to lose.

By Steven Porrello – Nov 27, 2026 at 5:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

As of this writing (after the market close on Nov. 25), the S&P 500 index (^GSPC +1.17%) has declined in nine of the past 10 trading sessions. Yet despite the sell-off, the benchmark index remains up about 5% for the year; ditto for the Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI +0.97%) and the Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +1.62%) indexes, which are also up in spite of recent pullbacks.

This continues a trend. For four years, the stock market has appeared invincible, unfazed by tariffs or wars. That resilience, however, may have reached its breaking point. And if the scales are is falling from investors' eyes, the result might spell bad news for a certain group of stocks.

For starters, there's no way to predict the future based on the past. This is even more the case when we're observing a pattern that has occurred only a handful of times in history.

Nevertheless, much wisdom can come from studying and observing past markets, which can give us some clues about the risks of historically high valuations.

One way to gauge the stock market's valuation is the Shiller price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio, also known as the CAPE ratio.

Like the traditional P/E ratio, the Shiller is a quick way to evaluate whether stocks are cheap or expensive. Unlike the traditional P/E, however, which relies on a single year of earnings, the Shiller uses average inflation-adjusted earnings during the past 10 years. This helps smooth out the effects of economic cycles (like recessions and bull markets); it's not perfect, but it can come in handy when comparing an index's valuation over time.

Since January 1871, the Shiller P/E has averaged roughly 17.5. As of Nov. 25, it stood at about 37.9, or more than double its historical average. Suffice it to say, stocks are historically expensive.

Only six times in history has the Shiller P/E clocked in above 30, with most of those occurring after 1999. The previous five were followed by painful stock market declines. And if recent history can tell us anything about today's premium valuations, it's that growth stocks could take the biggest hit.

Just months before the bubble popped in 2000, the Shiller P/E reached an all-time high of roughly 44. What followed was one of Wall Street's most painful bear markets, during which the Nasdaq plunged 80% from its peak in March 2000 to its trough in October 2002.

Although the dot-com damage was widespread, the losses were not evenly distributed. Indeed, growth stocks bore the brunt. Between 2000 and 2002, the Russell 1000 Growth Index (IWF +1.32%) plunged 57%, compared with a decline of 26.5% for the Russell 1000 Value Index (IWD +1.04%).

A similar disparity emerged during the bear market of 2022. As the Federal Reserve aggressively hiked interest rates to combat surging inflation, the Russell 1000 Growth Index tumbled about 32%. Its value counterpart, meanwhile, declined 19%.

Does this mean investors should abandon growth stocks right now? Not quite. Although growth stocks are historically vulnerable to sharp pullbacks, bear markets also present some of the best opportunities to buy them.

For example, shares of the iShares Russell 1000 Growth ETF (exchange-traded fund) purchased at the trough of the dot-com bear market would have delivered a roughly 1,600% return if held through today. Its value counterpart also delivered strong returns, albeit considerably smaller (about 998%).

Even if you had not timed the market perfectly, your chances of outperforming value stocks would have been strong. For example, an investment in the Russell 1000 Growth ETF in January 2003 would have delivered more than 1,444%, compared with 837% in the value index.

No bull market lasts forever, and if history is any reliable guide, today's premium valuation should give us reason for caution. But history doesn't stop there: It also suggests that the next bear market, whenever it comes, could present an extraordinary opportunity to buy great companies at bargain prices. One doesn't have to predict when that time will come, but being prepared to take advantage of it when it does could be a wise and prudent move.
