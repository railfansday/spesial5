Piolet d'Or Team Shares Insider Tips for Climbing in Peru's Huayhuash Range

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Marc Toralles, Ruben Sanmartin, and Bru Bussom of Spain were awarded a Piolet d’Or for a highly committing new route on Yerupaja Grande (6,634m), the highest peak of Peru’s Huayhuash range. ExplorersWeb asked the young climbers about the ascent and the unique character of the Andes. They offered some tips for climbers looking to add Peru to their list of high-altitude destinations.

In the last few years, the Andes have reportedly been affected by climate change. “The main effect is that the huge snow structures on the summits are exposed to warmer temperatures and become more fragile,” Sanmartin said. “Basically, they fall down more easily.”

“That means that climbers need to pick their route carefully [when judging] what lingers above them,” Busson explained.

Bru Busson, left, and Marc Toralles. Photo: Ruben Sanmartin

This was especially noticeable last year on Siula Grande. “There was a snowfield midway up the face as we trekked by, and on our way back, it was not there anymore,” said Marc Toralles. “Somehow, the melting process in the Andes, with its 5,000m and even 6,000m [altitudes], is similar to what we are seeing every year in the Alps, where we all work as mountain guides.”

For Toralles, the best thing in the Andes is how accessible the mountains are. “It’s so easy to go climbing there,” he said.

There are no lifts or paved roads, but you can go, climb a peak, and return in three or four days. Something like that in the Himalaya would take weeks. All you need is to get to Huaraz by plane, bus, or car from Lima, where you may stay some days to acclimatize (there are many options), and then go to the mountains.

If your planned peak is not in the right conditions, you will surely find some other one to climb. Also, the options are infinite for ice climbing, alpine climbing, rock, trekking, anything.

The team’s base camp. Photo: Spanish Yerupaja Grande team

Red tape is minimal. There are no permits for peaks. Instead, climbers and trekkers pay a small fee to cross the comunidades (the areas) where they want to climb, and climbers can hire arrieros (riders with mules) to help carry equipment and supplies to base camps or higher. On this occasion, the climbers kept their logistics basic, with little beyond a slightly larger base camp tent.

With some of the more accessible places, it is advisable to have someone watching over your base camp while you are away climbing.

Once you leave the beaten track, you’ll be alone. “There are no crowds in Huayhuash,” Busson said. Why aren’t they more popular? The climbers explained that Huayhuash peaks are typically more difficult than popular options in the Cordillera Blanca, also in Peru. But they note this lack of popularity is changing fast. “We are seeing much more people in recent years, both climbing and trekking,” they said.

Thanks to the many possibilities, the three Spanish climbers didn’t need to do much planning. “We know Huayhuash pretty well, we are familiar with the terrain and the climbing style, and we are aware there are always options,” said Toralles, who has climbed there four times.

That said, the line the Spaniards climbed on Yerupaja last year was not improvised. They had that route in their mind for some time. “We heard of someone who had tried but called the attempt off, so the route was there to be climbed,” Toralles said. “In addition, it didn’t require a long time, and the timing worked for me, since I was traveling to Peru anyway to do some guiding.”

Busson and Sanmartin joined immediately. To acclimatize, they had worked double shifts the previous weeks around Chamonix, where both are professional guides. “Working on the Mont Blanc massif intensively was great training and acclimatization,” they said. “We were ready by the time we set foot on the mountain.”

The best time to climb in Huayhuash is July, August, and the first half of September.

Once on the mountain, they carefully checked the conditions of the face and the line they would follow. “In the Andes, you have to assume that the summit ridge snow formations, the famous ‘mushrooms,’ the big cornices, and the seracs on the sides of the face are mostly in a delicate state. You need to assume that you will progress on deep, unstable snow, and the entire structure may have deteriorated and be prone to crumble,” Busson noted.

To deal with them, the climbers used some custom-made tools: They attached alitas (little wings) to their ice axes for a better grip on foamy, loose snow. They also carried small snowshoes for some sections.

Ice axe with ‘alitas’ (wings). Photo: Nick Sheldon/Mountain Project

Conditions in the Andean mountains are always variable. “You’ll find different terrain depending on the altitude and the orientation,” Sanmartin added. “This is why technical routes on these peaks require a high level of commitment, which, as happened on our route, may be even higher than the intrinsic difficulty of the pitches,” Toralles explained.

With this in mind, the Spanish team named the route they opened Essence of Commitment.

Marc Toralles ready for the ridge, with large snow pickets and an ice axe with ‘wings’ attached. Photo: Busson/Sanmartin

In this sort of terrain, it is paramount to stay focused and make the right call between subjective and objective factors. “There are countless reasons to turn around on climbs like this, but one must keep going, except for obvious reasons, such as an injury, a completely impassable section, or a really violent storm,” Sanmartin said.

“That is why it is important to consider all options and potential issues before starting the climb. You need to be sure you like the line and want to climb it.”

Asked whether they reached a point of no return during their climb, Busson admitted they did. “On reaching the ridge, we became aware that trying to downclimb the same route would be quite complex. However, we also saw the route upward was doable.”

The Spaniards did not count on a professional meteorologist. “We had our friends texting us the online, free forecasts over InReach,” they said. “One of the positive aspects of Huayhuash is that good weather periods tend to last for quite a while. Weather windows of five to six days are common.” During their climb, for example, they enjoyed acceptable weather, with some rain and snow but no high winds.

They warn that conditions in the Andes are changing quickly. “You need to observe the mountains and adapt,” Toralles said. “Some people aim for a route based on the information in a guidebook printed 50 years ago, and find a totally different scenario.” He pointed out that Siula Grande looked different in 2025 than it did when he climbed it just three years earlier.

Sanmartin noted that conditions have also changed on normal routes. They are now drier, more complex, and often more hazardous. “In some cases, climbers are abandoning the normal routes up the glaciers and opt for ridges which may be more difficult but in better conditions,” he said.

The climbers think the Andes will not get as crowded as the Himalaya, simply because the logistics are not as advanced — no refuges, no helicopters — and because they don’t have the sizzle of being the tallest peaks on Earth. “But they have incredible potential,” they said.
