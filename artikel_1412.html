Quantum resistance paradox of low-dimensional superfluids

Resistance in standard conductors decreases with increasing cross-section. Yet, in low-dimensional superconductors and superfluids, residual resistance arises from topological fluctuations of the order parameter, manifesting as phase slips in one-dimensional (1D) and vortices in two-dimensional (2D) systems. How resistance and dissipation evolve as geometry interpolates between these regimes remains an open question. This evolution is masked in solid-state experiments by disorder, impurities and geometric imperfections1,2,3, and poses theoretical challenges due to competing dissipative processes and pronounced finite-size effects. Here, we use a defect-free unitary Fermi gas in a digitally programmable transport geometry to isolate geometric effects on superfluid dissipation and discover a paradox: in the crossover from one dimension to two dimensions, the resistance reaches a minimum. There, widening a channel increases its resistance. Narrower, quasi-1D channels show dissipation described by Langer–Ambegaokar–McCumber–Halperin theory of phase slips4. In this regime, varying the channel width yields the predicted exponential scaling of the activation factor over many orders of magnitude. Wider, quasi-2D channels show dissipation consistent with phase-slip events described by a finite-size vortex model5. The minimal dissipation in the dimensional crossover reflects a transition in the dominant dissipative mechanism, with both 1D and 2D events simultaneously suppressed. Our measurements suggest a route to minimizing dissipation in superconducting devices and provide a benchmark for theoretical efforts aimed at describing the dimensional crossover.

The Landau model provides a powerful framework for understanding many phases of matter through the lens of spontaneous symmetry breaking6,7. In superfluids and superconductors, this breaking manifests in the emergence of a macroscopic condensate that exhibits long-range phase coherence. This coherence is ultimately responsible for properties such as dissipationless transport8,9, the Josephson effect10, and in the case of superconductivity the Meissner effect11. Although relatively robust in bulk systems, in lower-dimensional systems, the enhanced effect of fluctuations—and in particular topological defects—can suppress this long-range phase coherence and govern superfluid transport4,12.

For example, in a voltage-biased superconducting wire, the applied voltage continuously winds the superconducting phase, causing an increase of the phase gradient between the contacts. The supercurrent, proportional to this gradient, would grow without bound unless intermittent phase fluctuations relax the accumulated gradient. By relaxing the gradient, the fluctuations dissipate energy, thereby causing a resistance3,13. The characteristics of the fluctuations and their corresponding resistance depend on the dimensionality of the setup as follows.

In one-dimensional (1D) wires, discrete relaxation events known as phase slips dominate the dynamics. Microscopically, a phase slip occurs when the magnitude of the superconducting order parameter is transiently and locally suppressed over a healing length ξ. Where the amplitude vanishes, the phase is ill-defined, allowing for phase jumps of 2π. As the order parameter recovers, the wire returns to a state with a uniform amplitude but a reduced phase gradient4 (Fig. 1a, top row).

In a wide, quasi-2D channel, dissipation is governed by vortex–antivortex pairs traversing the channel (bottom row). b, A transport channel of programmable width connects two superfluid reservoirs (blue). In two-dimensional (2D) geometries, such as thin films, dissipation instead arises from the nucleation and motion of topological defects known as vortices. A vortex is a point-like singularity of the phase around which the phase winds by 2π, with a small core in which the amplitude is suppressed. Driven by a current, vortices move across the film and dissipate energy. Each time a vortex traverses the sample, the phase difference between the contacts relaxes by 2π. Repeated vortex crossings generate a finite voltage and hence an effective resistance14,15 (Fig. 1a, bottom row). In intermediate, thin-strip geometries, both vortex flow and phase slips may contribute to dissipation.

Previous studies included experiments on superconducting rings16; Dayem-bridge nanowires17,18,19,20; Bose–Einstein condensates in dumbbell21,22, cylindrical23 or toroidal geometries24; optical lattices25,26; and fermionic Josephson junctions27,28,29. These previous studies have explored various facets of phase-slip and vortex physics, including direct imaging of vortices28,30,31,32, the Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless phase transition33 and the recent observation of Shapiro steps in atomic Josephson junctions34,35. However, the experimental challenge of isolating and characterizing geometric or dimensional effects on dissipation remains unresolved.

With the rise of quantum simulation of devices based on highly controllable ultracold atomic gases36,37, it is now possible to shed new light on how these dissipative processes emerge in quantum systems.

In this work, we use ultracold, strongly interacting fermions in a two-terminal configuration to study nonlinear transport through a digitally programmable channel. The optically shaped channel can be reproducibly tuned from a quasi-1D to a quasi-2D geometry, providing a platform ideally suited for probing the influence of geometry on fluctuation-driven dissipation in the absence of defects or impurities.

In the narrow, 1D channel limit, we identify signatures of phase slips, in agreement with the Langer–Ambegaokar–McCumber–Halperin (LAMH) theory4,38,39. In particular, we observe the predicted exponential suppression of the LAMH activation factor over many orders of magnitude on widening the channel. For even larger widths, the system crosses over to a 2D regime in which dissipation is dominated by vortices, as indicated by the observed power-law dependence of the current–voltage characteristics predicted for vortex dynamics.

Beyond establishing these limiting behaviours, our measurements provide a direct window into the crossover between 1D and 2D transport, a regime in which analytical modelling is challenging. It is in this crossover that widening the channel paradoxically increases its resistance. More broadly, our results highlight the utility of ultracold atomic superfluids as a platform for quantum simulation of nonequilibrium transport.

We prepare a balanced mixture of the lowest and third-lowest hyperfine states of fermionic 6Li in the unitary regime. A total of 2N = 2.7 ± 0.1 × 105 atoms are cooled to T/TF = 0.144 ± 0.007, where N denotes the number of atom pairs, TF denotes the Fermi temperature, and the errors in both quantities denote the uncertainties due to shot-to-shot fluctuations. The temperature corresponds to T/TC ≈ 0.7, where TC ≈ 0.2TF denotes the critical temperature40. The atoms are confined in a cigar-shaped, harmonic trap with trapping frequencies (νx, νy, νz) = (222 ± 7, 28.1 ± 0.1, 195 ± 5) Hz.

As shown in Fig. 1a, a repulsive TEM0,1 beam, propagating in the x-direction, is aligned with the trap centre. It divides the atomic cloud into two reservoirs, separated by a quasi-2D transport region that is 51 ± 1 μm long and provides confinement of ν2D = 10.46 ± 0.09 kHz along z. Within the central 27 ± 0.5 μm of this plane, the atoms are further confined in the x-direction to a channel of variable width w. The channel is defined by a repulsive beam propagating along z, shaped by a digital micromirror device (DMD). It enables reproducible tunability of w from 0.9 ± 0.5 μm to 19.8 ± 0.5 μm, in which the uncertainty is dominated by and calculated from the resolution limit of our projection system (numerical aperture = 0.53, λ = 532 nm). Note that given the dimensions of our setup, we are not in the Josephson regime (see Methods, ‘No Josephson oscillations’). A third, attractive Gaussian beam (not shown) illuminates the transport channel and its connections to the reservoirs, locally increasing the degeneracy. A more detailed description of this effect as well as of the overall setup can be found in ref. 41.

At time t = 0, we prepare a relative atom-pair imbalance between the two reservoirs, ΔN/N = (Nl − Nr)/(Nl + Nr), where Nl,r denote the number of atom pairs in the left and right reservoirs. An initial imbalance of ΔN/N ≈ 0.1 relaxes through an approximately constant initial current, followed by damped oscillations of the imbalance. The discharge is distinctly non-exponential, indicating a nonlinear response. The damping, and in particular its dependence on the channel width w, carries information about the underlying microscopic mechanisms.

The key data of our measurement series are shown in Fig. 2a. We record transport traces for w between 0.9 ± 0.5 μm and 19.8 ± 0.5 μm. As w increases, the magnitude of the initial current increases, which manifests in a steeper initial slope. Concurrently, both the oscillation frequency and amplitude grow. The frequency rises from just below 10 Hz to approximately 18 Hz (see also Extended Data Fig. 1). For the narrowest channel (w = 0.9 ± 0.5 μm), the oscillation amplitude is comparable to the noise floor and barely resolved. By contrast, for the widest channel (w = 19.8 ± 0.5 μm), the amplitude reaches ΔN/N ≈ 0.06.

At longer transport times (for example, t = 0.3 s), the remaining oscillation amplitude reaches a maximum for intermediate channel widths (see also Fig. 4). These signals suggest distinct dissipative mechanisms in the narrow and wide channel limits, which we analyse quantitatively below.

The general characteristics of the dynamics can be understood as arising from the interplay between the potential energy stored in the reservoirs, the kinetic energy inside the channel and the dissipative processes we want to investigate. To study these dissipative processes, we model our system with an equivalent RLC circuit shown in Fig. 2b. We first characterize the capacitance and inductance of the circuit to subsequently infer the dissipative (resistive) processes by fitting the dynamics of the circuit to the measurements. The capacitances, inductance and resistance in the circuit model correspond to the compressibility of the atomic reservoirs, the inertia of the atoms and the dissipative processes in the channel, respectively. We explore these elements in the following starting with the capacitors. As the model circuit features two capacitors in series, each with a capacitance C, the effective capacitance of the circuit is Ceff = C/2. Phase slips and vortices dissipate the kinetic energy of the current into heat. So, they effectively act as a topological nonlinear resistor (TNR). With L and C determined, the resistance R remains the sole unknown component in our circuit. Owing to the nature of phase slips, R is topological and nonlinear, meaning it depends not only on the geometry but also on the current.

Showing how R depends on geometry, in particular in relation to the length scale given by ξ ≈ 1.2 μm (Methods, ‘Estimate of ξ’), reveals the nature of the underlying dissipative processes. To do so, we fit the width-dependent resistance R(w) in the equations of motion of an RLC circuit (derived in Methods, ‘RLC circuit modelling and fitting’)

The exponential suppression of R with w reflects the thermally activated phase-slip rate scaling with \(\varGamma \propto \exp (-\Delta F/({k}_{{\rm{B}}}T))\) (ref. 18), where the free energy barrier for phase slips is ΔF ∝ w. Assuming that the LAMH model can be used to describe our system, a simultaneous fit to multiple channel widths provides a robust extraction of the model parameters but enforces the predicted exponential suppression of RLAMH with w. We find R0/h = 102.3±0.2 and w0 = 49.7(6) nm, where the errors denote the uncertainties of the fit. Outside the validity range of the model, the quality of the fits degrades noticeably, failing to capture the phase of the oscillations and underestimating their damping.

In a second step, we validate the choice of the LAMH model by fitting RLAMH(w) to each curve individually. In contrast to the simultaneous fit, these individual fits do not enforce the predicted scaling RLAMH(w) and therefore provide an independent test of whether the scaling is present in the data. Within the validity regime of the model, the individual fits reproduce the predicted exponential scaling over many orders of magnitude, as shown in Fig. 3a.

This is the first main finding of this paper. Compared with RLAMH(w), the total resistance R(I, w) apparent in the measurements (Fig. 2a) varies much less. As we initialize a fixed potential bias in our system, the exponential geometric suppression (R ∝ exp(−w/w0)) approximately cancels the current-induced exponential enhancement (R ∝ sinh(I/I0) ≈ exp(I/I0)/2). This allows RLAMH to be inferred over a wide range—more than 10 decades—while only exploring a relatively modest dynamic range of R. Importantly, the observed scaling of RLAMH(w) is contained within the data itself and is not just an artefact of the choice of the model. This becomes evident when the model is applied outside its validity regime, where the scaling breaks down immediately (Methods, ‘Breakdown of scaling laws’).

When approaching the dimensional crossover w ≱ 4ξ, while still largely capturing the dynamics, the model begins to slightly but systematically underestimate the amplitude of the oscillations at long times. Presumably, because the approximation of sufficiently large w used in the derivation of R(J) ceases to hold (Methods, ‘Vortices in a finite width’). The eventual breakdown of the vortex model at w ≈ 4ξ marks the end of the 2D regime: the vortex model should be valid only when vortex–antivortex pairs are available as excitations. As each vortex has a radius of ξ, such an excitation requires w ≳ 4ξ. This leaves a gap between the validity ranges of the phase slip and the vortex model, in which neither model provides a microscopically satisfactory description. In this gap, there is a dimensional crossover, in which both phase slips and vortices compete.

The underdamped oscillations allow us to characterize dissipation in our system using only a few basic assumptions, while staying agnostic to the dissipation mechanism. This is particularly useful to analyse the dimensional crossover, in which neither the phase-slip nor the vortex model provide a satisfactory microscopical description. For each channel width w, we extract the times ti,w at which ΔN(t = ti, w) reaches an extremum and thus I ≈ 0. There, the system has minimal inductive energy (EL ≈ 0) and maximal capacitive energy. For a fixed width, the energy decays over time, reflecting dissipation. At any given time t > 0.15 s, Et(w)/E0 varies non-monotonically with the channel width, exhibiting a clear maximum near w ≈ 3ξ, corresponding to a minimal effective resistance. We call this deviation from the expected monotonicity the ‘quantum resistance paradox’ and consider it the third and last main finding of this paper. Note that the activation energy for phase slips increases exponentially with channel width, whereas vortex–antivortex pairs require sufficiently wide channels to form. The observed non-monotonicity thus suggests a transition in the underlying dissipation mechanism and a crossover from 1D to 2D physics.

We have investigated atom transport between two bulk superfluids through channels across different dimensional regimes and observed distinct behaviours. In narrow, quasi-1D channels, our data are in agreement with those of the LAMH model. We infer the geometric suppression of the phase slips by many orders of magnitude, providing the first experimental confirmation of the predicted geometric scaling. For wide, quasi-2D channels, dissipation is captured by a vortex flow model. The borders of the 1D and 2D regimes appear close to the theoretically expected length scale. A measurement of energy decay, agnostic to microscopic details, provides further evidence for a transition in the dissipation mechanism for channels of intermediate width, which paradoxically exhibit the least energy loss. These findings not only suggest routes for optimizing energy transport in mesoscopic devices but also provide a benchmark for theoretical efforts to describe the delicate phase-slip and vortex-flow mechanisms in the dimensional crossover in superfluids and superconductors42,43,44.

Finally, we discuss the fitting weights. Each configuration (t, w) has five measurements. Points with preparation errors (low atom number) are excluded, typically leaving four to five measurements per point and occasionally three. The experimental standard deviation is calculated for each configuration individually. We assume that for a given w, the uncertainty is independent of t. To prevent the fit from being dominated by points with unrealistically small uncertainties, both the fitting and the \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}\) calculations replace individual error bars with the median error bar of all the data sharing w.

The \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}\) analysis shown in Fig. 2c is performed as follows. The uncertainty of the measured points is assumed to be identical for all points belonging to a specific channel width. For each channel width, the model is fitted, either with 2.5 effective degrees of freedom (R0, w0, ΔN0, I0 or \({\widetilde{R}}_{0},\alpha ,\Delta {N}_{0},{I}_{0}\)), where the first two parameters are shared among four channels (2/4 = 0.5) if the channel participates in the global fit of the respective model, or two degrees of freedom (ΔN0, I0) otherwise. The reduced chi-squared is then calculated using \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}=\frac{1}{n-m}\sum \frac{{({m}_{i}-{f}_{i})}^{2}}{{\sigma }^{2}}\), where mi are the measured values, fi the corresponding values fitted by the model, σ the uncertainty shared by all measurement points of a specific width, n the number of measured points and m = 2.5 or m = 2 the number of effectively fitted degrees of freedom.

We discuss how the fitted models are affected by the various sources of systematic uncertainty. Specifically, we investigate how systematic uncertainties in the capacitance C, the channel width w and the temperature T affect the quality of the model fits and the inferred parameters RLAMH and α.

Assume that the true physical capacitance \(\widetilde{C}\) differs from the calculated value (see section ‘Derivation of the reservoir capacitance’) by a factor γ, such that \(C=\gamma \widetilde{C}\). In our analysis, the effective inductance L is determined (see section ‘Derivation of the inductance’) before fitting the resistance by analysing the underdamped, multi-period oscillations of the system. Owing to the observation of multiple periods, the frequency can be measured with good accuracy and precision. A systematic shift γ ≠ 1 would therefore result in an inverse scaling of the extracted inductance curve, \(L\approx \widetilde{L}/\gamma \), where \(\widetilde{L}\) represents the true physical inductance. Inspecting the underlying equation of motion of the circuit (equation (2)) shows that the extracted resistance scales identically with this factor (R ∝ 1/γ). This uniform rescaling by 1/α can be absorbed in the prefactor R0 or \({\widetilde{R}}_{0}\), leaving the quality of the fits, scalings, the inferred dynamic ranges and the geometric suppression constant w0 entirely unaffected.

The geometric potential landscape is projected onto the atoms using a DMD. Although physical pixel spacing and optical demagnification ratio are known accurately, the primary source of spatial uncertainty stems from the finite resolution of the imaging optics. The finite resolution effectively blurs the repulsive potential walls of the channel. Consequently, having to choose where to define the sharp boundaries of the model (spaced by w) within this blurred profile introduces a systematic uncertainty Δw.

Importantly, this geometric uncertainty of roughly 0.5 μm is expected to be shared across all channel configurations and does not affect each channel independently of the others. For channels in the 1D regime, the initial discharge through the narrowest channels is characterized by an extended period of nearly constant current in which inertial forces are negligible (∣L dI/dt∣ ≪ |RI|). In this regime, the system behaves predominantly as a nonlinear RC circuit, approximately decoupling the resistance fit from the precise value of L. Then, a uniform translation of w by Δw alters the resistances by a constant factor. For the LAMH model, \(R(w+\Delta w)\propto \exp (-\Delta w/{w}_{0})\). Similarly to the capacitance error, this effect is entirely absorbed by a re-normalization of the global prefactor R0, preserving both the dynamic range of RLAMH and its slope set by w0. The relatively high value of \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}\) for the LAMH model might be caused by the experiment that violates the assumption of box-like channel potentials. For the channels in the 2D regime, Δw/w ≪ 1, so we expect systematic effects caused by Δw ≠ 0 to be small and the approximation of modelling the channels as box-like to be safe.

Although we calibrated our measurements with the thermodynamic reference point of Bose–Einstein condensation, our measured atom-pair numbers and temperatures (see sections ‘High-saturation imaging’ and ‘Thermodynamic state extraction by 2D equation of state fitting’) might be subject to systematic errors. An error in the inferred pair-number \(N=\gamma \widetilde{N}\), where N is the inferred particle number and \(\widetilde{N}\) is the physical particle number, would force a corresponding shift in C, thereby rescaling L and R as discussed above. Moreover, it would rescale I. As the current enters the expression for the resistance in the vortex model only in the form of a power law, it would merely shift \(\widetilde{R}\) by a constant factor γ−α. Again, this constant factor would affect neither α, nor the quality of the vortex-model fits, nor the quality of its scaling law. As the vortex model does not contain an explicit temperature dependence, it is unaffected by potential systematic effects in T. For the LAMH model, the situation is different. The temperature enters the model explicitly through I0 = 2kBT/h. Variations in both N and T ultimately tie into the ratio I/I0. Because this ratio controls the degree of nonlinearity of the circuit, we can model their combined systematic impact by analysing variations in I0 alone.

The initial discharge of the narrowest channels is distinctly nonlinear (implying I ≫ I0), whereas the subsequent oscillations are approximately sinusoidal (implying I ≱ I0). Consequently, the ratio I/I0 is constrained by the geometric profile of the relaxation curves. However, systematic effects that affect the ratio I/I0 do also affect the quality of fit and the dynamic range of RLAMH. In general, if I/I0 is lowered (by decreasing I or increasing T), the circuit is less nonlinear and the dynamic range of RLAMH decreases, whereas an increase in the ratio has the opposite effect. Although the parameter I0 is constrained in the LAMH model, we performed additional fittings, where I0 was a global fit parameter, to effectively account for potential systematic errors in I or T. The best fits are achieved with I0,fit = 3.50(1)I0, reducing \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}\) averaged over the five narrowest channels, by more than a factor of 2. A possible cause for this could be that the attractive Gaussian beam illuminating the channel locally heats the channel, thereby increasing I0. Note that this beam also increases the local degeneracy such that we would still expect the channel to be deeply degenerate. Although in the case of fitted I0, the dynamic range of RLAMH is reduced, it continues to span more than four orders of magnitude. The values are still in good agreement with the predicted exponential scaling.

Given the method with which we extract the energy ratio, two main sources of error are to be considered. The first one is the statistical noise of the measurement points. This directly translates into an uncertainty of the inferred energy ratio but also causes a systematic bias towards higher values, as only the extrema of the signals are considered. The second contribution comes from the finite sampling rate of the oscillations. This causes an uncertainty and a bias towards lower values, as the measured points do not sample the peaks exactly. To quantitatively assess their influence for all our measurements, we performed Monte Carlo simulations. Specifically, we sampled sinusoidal signals with the frequencies, amplitudes and dampings observed in the signals used for Fig. 4. We sampled these artificial signals with the sampling rates and statistical noise that occurred in the physical experiment. This simulation yielded the following insight: For the data presented in Fig. 4, the inferred energies have a typical positive bias of 0.5% and a standard deviation smaller than 2%.

Although several studies have reported on Josephson oscillations with seemingly similar measurements in comparable settings27,28,29, our system operates far outside the Josephson regime. The Josephson effect requires the junction length to be much shorter than the superconducting coherence length, which for our system is close to the healing length4,12. In our case, the channel length ℓ = 27 μm is much larger than the estimated healing length ξ ≈ 1.2 μm (Methods, ‘Estimate of ξ’). Moreover, we observe neither a well-defined current-phase relation nor an oscillation amplitude consistent with the Josephson relation. The former statement is based on time-of-flight measurements of the relative phase of the two reservoirs with the same preparation performed in a slightly different geometry. Although for the data shown in this manuscript, we always observe the same initial dynamics (up to small noise), these additional measurements confirm that the prepared phase between the two reservoirs is not controlled, sampling values between 0 and 2π from a flat distribution. We therefore conclude that the oscillatory signal observed in our experiment cannot be attributed to JO.

Extended Data Fig. 2 provides additional data related to Fig. 3. The theoretical motivation and the \({\chi }_{\nu }^{2}\) analysis presented in Fig. 2 already justify the choice of models. The data shown here complements that analysis by demonstrating that the scaling laws reported in Fig. 3 are not numerical artefacts and are not imposed by the fitting procedure.

For both the LAMH and vortex models, the predicted scalings break down outside their respective ranges of validity. This behaviour indicates that the observed scaling is not a numerical consequence of the fitting procedure and is not artificially enforced by the functional form of the models.

The saturation of the LAMH-inferred resistance at large widths does not seem to be due to limited fitting precision, fitting boundaries or lack of convergence. Instead, it reflects a transition from the strong exponential width dependence characteristic of the LAMH model to a much weaker dependence, as predicted by the vortex model.

For the vortex model, the trend reverses in the crossover regime, showing a clear deviation from the scaling expected by that model.

The individually inferred inductances are well described by our fitted model. This model assumes a width dependent inductance of the channel in series with a width independent, residual inductance from the 2D region and the bulk superfluids. Inset: individually inferred oscillation frequencies are also well described by modelled frequencies relying on the same inductance model.

Inferred resistances from the LAMH model (top) and the vortex model (bottom) are shown across the full width range. In both cases, the expected scalings break down outside the respective range of model validity. For the LAMH model, the inferred resistance does not encounter fitting boundaries or precision limits. Instead, the data exhibit a weak width dependence, consistent with the vortex model prediction. Portions of these data are also shown in Fig. 3.
