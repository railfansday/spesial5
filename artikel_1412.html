Non-halogen aqueous electrolytes for four-electron iodine conversion

Accessing reversible four-electron iodine redox chemistry in aqueous metal–iodine batteries offers a promising route towards high-energy aqueous energy storage, but has conventionally relied on highly concentrated, corrosive halogen-containing electrolytes to stabilize reactive hypervalent iodine cations through interhalogen complexation1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9. Here we show a kosmotropic anion–polar anion–nucleophilic species (Nuc) electrolyte framework that harnesses anionic chemistry to coordinate the interfacial microenvironment and molecular interactions, enabling reversible four-electron iodine conversion in halogen-free aqueous electrolytes. Polar anions establish a water-deficient, anion-enriched interfacial microenvironment, whereas kosmotropic anions regulate water–Nuc interactions to increase the availability of Nuc for stabilizing hypervalent iodine cations through Nuc–I+–Nuc halogen bonds. This electrolyte framework is validated across diverse Nuc and in both aqueous Zn–I2 and Al–I2 batteries. Moving beyond conventional interhalogen stabilization, these findings establish a distinct electrolyte chemistry framework and represent a mechanistic shift for reversible multi-electron iodine chemistry in aqueous environments.

The increasing integration of renewable energy calls for sustainable electrochemical energy storage systems beyond lithium-ion batteries1. Aqueous metal–iodine batteries offer a promising platform for large-scale energy storage by combining earth-abundant electrode materials, aqueous electrolytes and conversion-type iodine chemistry2,3. Unlike intercalation-type positive electrodes, iodine positive electrodes store charge by reversible valence-state changes, offering access to multi-electron redox chemistry with increased capacity and redox potential4. Most studies have focused on the two-electron I−/I0 redox couple, with a theoretical capacity of 211 mAh g−1 at a redox potential of 0.54 V versus the standard hydrogen electrode10. Extending iodine conversion to the successive I−/I0/I+ redox couples doubles the theoretical capacity to 422 mAh g−1 while introducing the I0/I+ redox process at a higher redox potential of 1.07 V versus the standard hydrogen electrode11. However, despite this potential, achieving reversible four-electron iodine conversion in aqueous environments remains a fundamental challenge3,7.

The crux of iodine conversion chemistry lies in interfacial reactions, where electrolyte composition and interfacial microenvironment govern reaction pathways. Electron-deficient I+ is strongly electrophilic and susceptible to nucleophilic attack by water, resulting in hydrolysis and poor reversibility in aqueous environments9,12. Previous studies have achieved four-electron iodine conversion in aqueous Zn–I2 and Al–I2 batteries by stabilizing reactive I+ through interhalogen complexes (for example, ICl2 or IBr2) in highly concentrated chloride- or bromide-containing electrolytes5,6,13. In these systems, electrophilic I+ coordinates with two nucleophilic halide ions (Cl− or Br−) through non-covalent interactions8,14. However, at water-enriched interfaces, hydration of nucleophilic species (Nuc) competes with these interactions and destabilizes I+ complexes (Fig. 1a). Highly concentrated, halogen-containing electrolytes mitigate this competition by reducing water activity and increasing halide availability, but their high salt contents and corrosivity constrain broader implementation (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1), necessitating new electrolyte chemistry and a mechanistic framework that move beyond reliance on halogen-containing electrolytes and conventional interhalogen stabilization5,8.

Given the competition between I+ stabilization and hydration at water-enriched interfaces, overcoming this challenge requires simultaneous regulation of the interfacial environment and water–Nuc interactions. Anionic chemistry offers a powerful lever to coordinate these interfacial and molecular interactions15,16,17,18. Herein we establish a kosmotropic anion (KA)–polar anion (PA)–Nuc electrolyte framework for reversible I−/I0/I+ conversion in halogen-free aqueous electrolytes (Fig. 1c). Systematic experiments and theoretical calculations reveal how the synergistic effects of PAs and KAs coordinate the interfacial microenvironment and molecular interactions, thereby stabilizing interfacial electrochemical behaviour and promoting reversible four-electron iodine conversion. The applicability of this electrolyte framework is demonstrated across four halogen-free Nuc (urea, acetamide, pyridine and nicotinamide) and in both aqueous Zn–I2 and Al–I2 batteries (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Beyond establishing a halogen-free electrolyte chemistry, this work reveals how electrolyte chemistry governs iodine conversion across interfacial, molecular and electrochemical scales, providing a mechanistic basis for reversible multi-electron iodine chemistry in aqueous environments.

Stabilizing I+ against hydrolysis is essential for reversible four-electron iodine conversion in aqueous electrolytes. Previous studies have relied on highly concentrated, corrosive halogen-containing electrolytes for interhalogen stabilization19,20, whereas more recent studies have explored specific additive-halogen nucleophilic substitution reactions21,22. These approaches primarily address I+ stabilization through specific molecular interactions, whereas how electrolyte chemistry collectively regulates the interfacial microenvironment, molecular stabilization and the resulting iodine conversion pathways remains less understood. This work establishes a mechanistic framework that integrates anionic chemistry with halogen-bond stabilization in halogen-free aqueous electrolytes. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, strong PAs establish a water-deficient, anion-enriched interface, whereas KAs weaken Nuc–water hydrogen bonding to facilitate halogen-bond stabilization of I+. These complementary effects underpin the halogen-free KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte framework, whose composition was subsequently optimized (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4) and exhibits sufficient electrochemical stability (Supplementary Fig. 5) for four-electron iodine conversion.

The KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte framework was validated in aqueous Zn–I2 batteries across four different Nuc (Supplementary Fig. 6). Electron-deficient I+ can interact with two electron-rich Nuc molecules to form linear Nuc–I+–Nuc halogen bonds. These halogen bonds provide the molecular basis for I+ stabilization, as supported by Nuc-free controls, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and theoretical calculations23 (Supplementary Figs. 7–11). Using urea (U) as a representative Nuc, KA–U exhibits only the I−/I0 redox process, whereas PA–U enables further I0/I+ oxidation but with poor reversibility (Fig. 2b,e). The excessive capacity at the second charge plateau is attributed to irreversible iodine oxidation coupled with parasitic water oxidation (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Combining KA and PA in KA–PA–U produces two reversible redox peaks and corresponding charge–discharge plateaus, demonstrating their synergistic role in enabling reversible I−/I0/I+ conversion (Supplementary Fig. 14). Replacing U with acetamide (AE), nicotinamide (NA) or pyridine (PD) shows the same phenomenon, with reversible I−/I0/I+ conversion achieved only in the KA–PA–Nuc electrolytes, including under identical ionic strength conditions, indicating the broad applicability of this electrolyte framework (Fig. 2c,d,f,g and Supplementary Figs. 15–18).

To isolate the role of PAs in the KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte framework, SO42− was maintained as the KA, whereas structurally related CF3SO3−, CF3COO− or CH3COO− was introduced as the second anion. Density-functional-theory calculations reveal a polarity sequence of CH3COO− > CF3COO− > CF3SO3− (Supplementary Fig. 19), providing a basis for examining how anion polarity influences interfacial behaviours24,25. Molecular-dynamics simulations show similar water-enriched bulk environments across the three electrolytes (Supplementary Figs. 20–22), but distinct interfacial structures (Fig. 3d). CH3COO− reduces the interfacial water density while increasing anion enrichment, whereas CF3COO− and CF3SO3− retain high interfacial water intensity (Fig. 3e,f). In situ attenuated total reflection infrared (ATR-IR) spectroscopy further supports this interfacial redistribution. The δ-HOH vibration at 1,640 cm−1 remains pronounced with CF3SO3− or CF3COO− (Fig. 3g,h and Supplementary Fig. 23), but is suppressed with CH3COO− alongside pronounced anion-associated signals26,27,28,29 (Fig. 3i). Notably, highly concentrated ZnCl2 retains a water-enriched interface despite its water-deficient bulk environment (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). Differential capacitance measurements indicate stronger interfacial adsorption of CH3COO− than of CF3COO− and CF3SO3− (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27), linking preferential adsorption of strongly PAs to the formation of a water-deficient, anion-enriched interfacial microenvironment30.

These polarity-dependent interfacial structures lead to distinct iodine conversion behaviour. CF3SO3− shows a negligible I0/I+ process, whereas CF3COO− enables I0/I+ oxidation but with limited reversibility. By contrast, CH3COO− supports reversible I−/I0/I+ conversion with improved stability and capacity (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 28). In situ ultraviolet–visible spectra and micro-computed-tomography analyses further link these differences to iodine species distribution, showing that CH3COO− confines iodine species within the electrode and suppresses their migration into the bulk electrolyte (Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30). Together, these results establish a mechanistic relationship between anion polarity and the resulting water-deficient, anion-enriched interfacial microenvironment that governs the reversibility of four-electron iodine conversion.

Building on the understanding of PAs, the critical role of KAs in the KA–PA–Nuc framework was further explored. Nuc stabilize I+ through electron-rich nitrogen or oxygen centres, which also interact with water through hydrogen bonding and therefore compete with halogen-bond formation (Supplementary Fig. 31). Inspired by anion-specific effects on water–solute interactions, SO42−, CH3COO−, NO3−, CF3SO3− and ClO4− were compared in the same PA–Nuc baseline31,32,33,34 (Supplementary Fig. 32). Although these anions have little effect on the initial capacity, they produce different cycling stability (Supplementary Fig. 33). SO42− maintains nearly overlapping galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) profiles, whereas CH3COO− and CF3SO3− show intermediate cycling stability and ClO4− undergoes rapid deterioration (Fig. 4a–c and Supplementary Fig. 34), giving a stability sequence of SO42− > CH3COO− > CF3SO3− > ClO4−. NO3− deviates from this sequence owing to its limited electrochemical stability within the applied voltage range35 (Supplementary Fig. 35a,b).

NMR spectroscopy and molecular-dynamics simulations reveal the molecular origin of these electrochemical differences. The 2H chemical shift follows SO42− > CH3COO− > NO3− > CF3SO3− > ClO4− (Fig. 4d), indicating progressively stronger anion-induced water polarization and restructuring of the aqueous hydrogen-bond network36,37. These differences in water–anion coordination translate into distinct water–Nuc interactions. The corresponding 15N chemical shifts, together with the inverse trends observed in 1H and 13C chemical shifts, indicate that KAs such as SO42− polarize hydration water and thereby weaken Nuc–water hydrogen bonding, whereas chaotropic anions such as ClO4− interact directly with the Nuc backbone and facilitate Nuc hydration32 (Fig. 4e,f, and Supplementary Figs. 32 and 36–38). Nuc solubility measurements, two-dimensional 1H–15N heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) NMR spectra, and calculated Nuc–water distances and hydrogen-bond populations further support this interpretation (Fig. 4g–i, and Supplementary Figs. 35c, 39 and 40). By weakening Nuc–water interactions, SO42− increases the availability of Nuc for halogen-bond stabilization of I+, thereby promoting reversible iodine conversion. These results reveal how anionic chemistry regulates molecular water–Nuc interactions to govern the reversibility of four-electron iodine conversion.

Having established the complementary roles of PA and KA in interfacial and molecular regulation, we next examined how these effects manifest in the electrochemical processes governing iodine conversion. In situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) combined with distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was used to decouple the complex impedance response and identify individual electrochemical processes38,39,40 (Supplementary Figs. 41–43). In the absence of either KA or PA, the DRT responses evolve distinctly with additional competing interfacial processes, whereas their combined presence maintains stable interfacial electrochemical behaviour throughout iodine conversion41,42 (Extended Data Fig. 1). Rate-dependent analysis identifies charge transfer, rather than ion transport, as the dominant kinetic contribution to iodine conversion in the KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte (Supplementary Figs. 44–50). In situ Raman spectroscopy further links these electrochemical processes to iodine chemistry, revealing the reversible evolution of the Nuc–I+–Nuc species during cycling (Supplementary Figs. 51 and 52).

The KA–PA–Nuc framework supports four-electron iodine conversion across different operating conditions and cell configurations. Zn–I2 cells retain 96% of their capacity after 1,000 cycles at 1 A g−1 and 94% after 2,000 cycles at 2 A g−1, while sustaining four-electron conversion at iodine loadings up to 12.2 mg cm−2 (Supplementary Figs. 53–55, 58a and 59). This electrolyte framework is also compatible with different current collectors and pouch-cell configurations and extends to aqueous Al–I2 cells using Al–Zn alloy negative electrodes (Supplementary Figs. 56–58b,c, 60 and 61). A techno-economic analysis under standardized modelling assumptions further illustrates its theoretical cost-reduction potential by avoiding highly concentrated, halogen-containing electrolytes and costly current collectors (Supplementary Fig. 58d–f and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Mechanistic and post-mortem analyses further reveal that the apparent low Coulombic efficiency and capacity fading involve coupled reversible and irreversible iodine inventory loss, with the latter becoming more pronounced at increased iodine loading (Supplementary Figs. 62–67 and Extended Data Figs. 2–4). Together, these results clarify the iodine conversion pathways while defining the applicability and remaining limitations of the KA–PA–Nuc framework, showing that sustained reversible four-electron iodine conversion requires not only stabilization of hypervalent iodine species but also broader management of the entire iodine inventory.

Iodine-activated carbon (I2–AC) composites were prepared using an evaporation–adsorption method. In brief, 0.5 g iodine (>99.5%, Thermo Scientific Acros) and 0.5 g activated carbon (AC; YP-80F, purity not specified, Kuraray) were manually ground in an agate mortar with a pestle for 10 min under ambient conditions. The resulting mixture was sealed in a Teflon reactor and heated at 120 °C for 6 h. For the preparation of iodine positive electrodes using titanium (80 mesh, >99.5%, Hebei Conway Metal Products) or stainless steel mesh (80 mesh, 316L, Hebei Conway Metal Products) as the current collector, I2–AC, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, 60 wt% dispersion H2O, Sigma-Aldrich) and Super P (purity not specified, Canrd) were combined at a mass ratio of 8:1:1. For each batch, 40 mg I2–AC, 5 mg Super P and 5 mg PTFE (calculated on a dry-PTFE basis) were manually mixed with deionized water using an agate mortar and pestle under ambient conditions. The resulting mixture was rolled into a self-standing film using a roller press (Etelux) and subsequently dried in an oven at 60 °C for 3 h in air. The areal iodine loading was controlled by adjusting the thickness of the composite film. The dried film was then pressed onto the titanium or stainless steel mesh using the roller press. The resulting electrodes were punched into 12 mm disks using a manual disk punch (T07, ‌Hefei Kejing Material Technology).

For iodine positive electrodes using carbon paper (TGP-H-060, Toray) as the current collector, I2–AC, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, weight-average molecular weight, Mw ≈ 534,000 g mol−1 by gel permeation chromatography, powder, Sigma-Aldrich) and Super P were combined at a mass ratio of 8:1:1. For each batch, 160 mg I2–AC, 20 mg PVDF and 20 mg Super P were manually mixed with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (>99.5%, Thermo Scientific Acros) using an agate mortar and pestle in ambient conditions. The resulting slurry was manually and uniformly spread onto the carbon paper using a laboratory spatula. The coated electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 8 h and subsequently cut into 12-mm disks using the manual disk punch. Commercial Zn foils (70 µm, >99.99%, ‌Qinghe Haoxuan Metal Materials) were used as negative electrodes as received.

By adjusting the electrode thickness, the capacity of the AC electrode was controlled to be much higher than that of the iodine working electrode, ensuring its capability as the counter electrode for evaluating the electrochemical performance of the KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte. To further validate the applicability of the KA–PA–Nuc electrolyte framework in multi-electron aqueous Al–I2 batteries, Al–Zn alloys were fabricated as negative electrodes following a previous study43. In brief, symmetric cells were assembled using Zn foils (70 µm, 16 mm in diameter) as electrodes and 2 M Al (CF3SO3)3 (>99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) as the electrolyte. During charging, Al3+ in the electrolyte deposited on and reacted with the metal Zn substrate, forming the Zn-supported Al–Zn alloys.

Halogen-bond complexes were synthesized via a cation-exchange reaction using corresponding silver analogues, following a previously reported procedure with some modifications44. All operations were carried out under a standard Schlenk line. In a typical synthesis, 0.94 mmol of the corresponding silver salt (Ag2SO4, >99%, Sigma-Aldrich; AgCH3COO, >99%, Sigma-Aldrich; AgCF3COO, >98%, Sigma-Aldrich; AgCF3SO3, >99%, Sigma-Aldrich; AgNO3, >99%, Sigma-Aldrich; or AgClO4, >97%, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to a 20-ml vial equipped with a stir bar. The vial was sealed with a rubber septum and evacuated. Subsequently, 6.0 ml dry CH2Cl2 (purity not specified, VWR Chemicals) and 1.90 mmol pyridine (3.80 mmol for Ag2SO4) were added at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was stirred for 10 min to form the Ag(I)–pyridine bis-coordination complex. Next, 2.00 mmol of iodine (4.00 mmol for Ag2SO4) was added, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for an additional 20 min under continuous stirring. The supernatant was then carefully transferred to another vial via cannula filtration, followed by the addition of 15 ml dry n-hexane (>99%, Macron Fine Chemicals) to induce precipitation of the halogen-bond complex. The mixture was stored at −20 °C overnight to complete precipitation. Afterwards, the supernatant was removed using a syringe, and the resulting solid was washed with 20 ml dry n-hexane to remove any residual I2. Finally, the product was dried under vacuum on the Schlenk line.

All molecular-dynamics simulations were performed using the LAMMPS package45 to investigate the bulk solvation structure of various aqueous electrolytes. The initial configurations were constructed using Packmol46, which randomly packed electrolyte components into a periodic cubic box according to the target molar ratios, ensuring no initial atomic overlaps. The simulation cell was then relaxed by energy minimization before subsequent equilibration. Force-field parameters for organic molecules were taken from the OPLS-AA family47,48, whereas others were adopted from validated aqueous zinc salt parameter sets in the literature. To partially account for polarization effects in concentrated electrolytes, the atomic charges were uniformly scaled (scaling factor 0.8)49. Interaction of water was represented by TIP3P50, and all X–H bonds were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm51. Each system was first energy-minimized and equilibrated in the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (300 K, 1 atm) using a Nosé–Hoover thermostat until both the density and potential energy were stable. Subsequently, production runs were performed in the canonical ensemble for 5 ns (5,000 ps) with a 1-fs integration step to ensure complete equilibration while avoiding structural drift.

Radial distribution functions gij(r) were computed to analyse ion pairing and solvation structure. Coordination numbers were obtained by integrating gij(r) up to their first minimum. Both radial distribution functions and hydrogen bonds were identified using the hydrogen-bond analysis tools implemented in VMD52. Average hydrogen-bond numbers and distributions were determined over the equilibrated trajectories to characterize the reorganization of the hydrogen-bond network by different anions.

To probe field-induced ion and molecular redistribution, an additional set of simulations was conducted in the canonical ensemble with a uniform static electric field applied along the z axis. The field strength corresponded to the experimentally estimated potential gradient.

Density-functional-theory calculations were performed using the Gaussian16 program package, using the M06-2X exchange-correlation functional53. Geometry optimizations were conducted using a mixed basis-set approach: the 6-311+G(d,p) basis set was applied to all elements excluding iodine54. Iodine was described using an effective core potential from the Stuttgart/Dresden group55. Solvent effects were accounted for using the polarizable continuum model, with water selected as the solvent56.
