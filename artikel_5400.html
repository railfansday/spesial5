From Millerites to Y2K: 10 end-of-the-world dates that passed

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 failed doomsday predictions, from the Millerites' 1844 date to Y2K and viral blackout hoaxes, show how precise end-of-the-world dates came and went

Every end-of-the-world claim carries a built-in test: a date. The preacher William Miller's followers settled on Oct. 22, 1844. Harold Camping named May 21, 2011. Popular accounts of the Maya Long Count calendar pointed to Dec. 21, 2012. Each date arrived, and each one passed.

That pattern makes failed doomsday predictions easy to check. A vague warning about decline is hard to disprove. A specific date is not. Once the calendar moves on, the claim either holds or it does not. In every case on this list, it did not.

The sources of these predictions vary more than their outcomes. Some came from religious date-setting, built on readings of scripture such as the Book of Daniel. Others drew on astronomy and physics. In 1910, the detection of cyanogen gas in the spectrum of Halley's Comet fed fears that Earth's passage through its tail could poison the air. A 1974 book called The Jupiter Effect tied a 1982 planetary alignment to possible earthquakes. In 2008, critics warned that the Large Hadron Collider at CERN could create black holes.

Technology produced its own version. The Y2K bug was a genuine programming problem rooted in two-digit year fields. Governments and companies spent years fixing it. The collapse of power grids and banks that some predicted did not follow. More recent claims have spread mainly online, from the supposed hidden planet Nibiru to recurring hoaxes that say Earth will go dark for days.

This list is a selection of 10 cases, not a complete record or a ranking. The cases run in chronological order. Each is explained on its own terms: who made the prediction, what it rested on, what date it named and what happened next. Several share a second pattern. When the first date failed, the prediction was often revised rather than abandoned, with a new date set further out.

That history offers a few questions for apocalypse claims circulating now. Who is making the claim? What evidence does it cite? Has a scientific body such as NASA already addressed it? And what happened the last time someone set a date?

The Millerite movement predicted that Christ would return on Oct. 22, 1844. When the day passed without incident, followers called the letdown the Great Disappointment. Some of them went on to help build the Seventh-day Adventist Church.

The forecast began with William Miller, a farmer and Baptist preacher from Low Hampton, New York. He spent years studying the Bible. He concluded that the Book of Daniel contained a timetable for the Second Coming.

His key passage was Daniel 8:14, which speaks of 2,300 days before the sanctuary is cleansed. Miller read each prophetic day as a year. He started the count in 457 B.C., the date he assigned to a Persian decree to rebuild Jerusalem. That arithmetic pointed to roughly 1843.

Miller began preaching his conclusions publicly in the 1830s. Joshua V. Himes, a Boston minister, turned the message into an organized campaign. Himes launched the newspaper Signs of the Times. He also arranged camp meetings and lecture tours across the northeastern U.S.

Miller avoided a single date at first. He described a window that ran from about March 1843 to March 1844. When that period ended without the expected return, the movement needed a new answer.

Samuel S. Snow, a Millerite preacher, supplied one. He tied the prophecy to the Jewish Day of Atonement. By his reckoning, it fell on Oct. 22, 1844. The date spread quickly through the movement in the late summer and fall of that year. Some believers left crops unharvested or closed their businesses to prepare.

The day ended like any other. Many followers left the movement. Others faced ridicule from neighbors and the press.

A smaller group reinterpreted the prophecy instead of abandoning it. They argued that Oct. 22 marked an event in heaven rather than on Earth. From that group came figures such as Ellen G. White and James White. The Seventh-day Adventist Church was formally organized in 1863. Miller died in 1849, years before the church took formal shape.

Earth passed through the tail of Halley's Comet around May 18–19, 1910, and nothing happened to the planet or its people. The scare behind that date grew from a real observation. Astronomers had found cyanogen, a poisonous gas, in the comet's light.

Halley's Comet is a periodic comet that returns to the inner solar system roughly every 75 to 76 years. It is named for the English astronomer Edmond Halley. He calculated that comets recorded in 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object, and he predicted its return. The comet reappeared in 1758, years after Halley's death.

By 1910, astronomers had a tool Halley never used: spectroscopy. Splitting a comet's light into its component colors reveals the chemical signatures of the gases it contains. When observers studied Halley's Comet this way, cyanogen showed up in its spectrum.

Cyanogen is a compound of carbon and nitrogen, and it is toxic. Its presence gave a doomsday story something that sounded like scientific grounding.

The French astronomer Camille Flammarion pushed the finding further. He suggested that Earth's passage through the comet's tail might poison the atmosphere. Flammarion was a widely read author on astronomy, and his warning traveled well beyond scientific circles.

Newspapers covered the fear. Some sellers looked to profit from it, marketing so-called comet pills and gas masks to people anxious about the encounter.

The physics never supported the alarm. A comet's tail stretches across millions of kilometers but holds very little material. The gas in it is far too thin to alter the makeup of Earth's atmosphere.

Mark Twain was born in 1835, during the comet's previous appearance. He died in April 1910, as it came back. In 1909, he had remarked that he expected to go out with the comet.

Halley's Comet returned in 1986. That March, the European Space Agency's Giotto probe flew past its nucleus and sent back close-up images. Its next return is expected in 2061.

The Jupiter Effect, a 1974 book by John Gribbin and Stephen Plagemann, argued that a 1982 alignment of the planets could set off earthquakes, including along California's San Andreas fault. The planets arrived in position. The disaster never came.

The forecast stood apart from most end-of-the-world claims because it was framed as physics rather than prophecy. Gribbin trained as an astrophysicist. The book laid out a chain of physical causes and asked readers to follow each link to a year on the calendar.

The chain began with the Sun. The authors reasoned that when most of the planets gathered on one side of it, their combined gravity would raise tides on the Sun and stir up solar activity. A more turbulent Sun would then slightly alter the speed of Earth's rotation. That small change, they suggested, could release strain already built up in stressed faults.

Every step depended on the one before it, and the first step was the weakest. The planets are tiny next to the Sun. Their tidal pull on it is minute. On Earth, the Moon's tidal pull far exceeds that of any planet, Jupiter included. Grouping the planets together adds very little to forces Earth already feels every day.

The word "alignment" also promised more than the sky delivered. The planets did not form a straight line. They clustered within the same broad stretch of the solar system on one side of the Sun. That grouping carried little gravitational consequence for Earth.

The San Andreas fault gave the warning its local force. It marks the boundary where the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate slide past each other. Movement along it caused the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Californians already knew the fault could produce another large quake.

The year 1982 came and went without the predicted catastrophe. The San Andreas did not rupture on the planets' schedule. California's earthquake risk remained what it had been before the book appeared: real, ongoing and still not predictable to a specific date.

Edgar Whisenant, a former NASA engineer, predicted that the Rapture would come between Sept. 11 and Sept. 13, 1988. The three days passed without incident.

Whisenant set out his case in a booklet titled 88 Reasons Why the Rapture Will Be in 1988. Each numbered reason was meant to support the others and point to a single three-day window.

In the teaching of many evangelical Protestants, the Rapture is the moment when Christian believers are caught up to meet Christ in the air. The term comes from rapiemur, the Latin for "we shall be caught up" in the Vulgate translation of Paul's first letter to the Thessalonians.

In the dispensationalist reading popular among many of these believers, the Rapture comes before a seven-year period of tribulation. Whisenant attached that event to specific days on the calendar.

The September window overlapped with Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year, which fell on Sept. 12 and 13 in 1988. The holiday is also known as the Feast of Trumpets, a name drawn from the Hebrew Bible. Paul's letter also describes a trumpet sounding at the moment believers are caught up.

Another strand of the timing rested on a count of years. The modern state of Israel was founded in 1948. Whisenant's reasoning treated 40 years as one biblical generation and counted forward from 1948 to reach 1988. The idea drew on Matthew 24, where Jesus tells his disciples that "this generation" will not pass away until the events he describes take place.

Sept. 13, 1988, ended with no Rapture. Whisenant then published a follow-up, The Final Shout: Rapture Report 1989, which moved the prediction to 1989. That year also passed without a Rapture.

The same chapter of Matthew records Jesus saying that no one knows the day or hour of his return. Whisenant's first booklet named a three-day window, and his second named a full year.

The Y2K bug was a real software flaw. Many older computer systems stored years as two digits, which meant "00" could be read as 1900 instead of 2000. Years of repair work came first, and the predicted breakdown of power grids and banks did not come.

The name is shorthand. Y stands for year, the 2 for the number and K for kilo, the metric prefix for a thousand.

The two-digit habit dates to early computing, when memory and storage were expensive. Dropping the "19" from a year saved space on punch cards, magnetic tape and mainframe records. Programmers writing code in the 1960s and 1970s generally did not expect it to still be running decades later. Much of it was.

The risk lay in date arithmetic. A program that calculates a person's age, a loan's interest or a license's expiration subtracts one year from another. If 2000 is stored as "00," that math can return a negative number or a result that is off by a century. Systems that sort records by date can also place new entries ahead of old ones.

A second, smaller issue involved leap years. Years divisible by 100 are usually not leap years, but years divisible by 400 are. That made 2000 a leap year. Software that applied only the century rule could get Feb. 29 wrong.

Public warnings went further than the code itself. Some forecasts said failures would spread from computers to utilities, banks and other services at midnight.

The repair work involved auditing old code, then rewriting or replacing the parts that handled dates. In the U.S., the President's Council on Year 2000 Conversion coordinated the federal effort.

When midnight passed across the world's time zones, the large-scale failures did not arrive. Some minor glitches were reported. A number of websites displayed the year as "19100." The code behind them placed the digits "19" in front of a year counter that had reached 100.

The quiet rollover left a question that is hard to settle: whether the remediation prevented failures or the risk had been overstated. A failure that does not happen leaves little evidence behind.

A related date limit still lies ahead. Many systems count time as seconds elapsed since Jan. 1, 1970. A 32-bit signed version of that counter runs out on Jan. 19, 2038.

Nancy Lieder predicted that a hidden planet called Nibiru, or Planet X $TWTR, would approach Earth in May 2003 and cause a global catastrophe. The month passed without incident. NASA astronomers have said no such object exists.

Lieder spread the claim through ZetaTalk, a website she ran. She said the information came from aliens she described as beings from the Zeta Reticuli star system. That gave her prediction an unusual basis. It rested on neither scripture nor a published scientific paper. It rested on what Lieder presented as messages relayed to her.

The scenario she described was physical rather than spiritual. A large body would move through the inner solar system, and its close pass would bring disaster to Earth. Lieder tied the event to a specific month, which made the claim easy to check. When May 2003 arrived, no planet appeared.

The name Nibiru did not originate with Lieder. It comes from ancient Babylonian astronomy, where it referred to a celestial body associated with the god Marduk. The writer Zecharia Sitchin popularized a reworked version of the idea in his 1976 book The 12th Planet. Sitchin argued that an unseen planet orbits the sun on a very long cycle.

Planet X has a separate and legitimate history in astronomy. Percival Lowell used the term in the early 20th century for a planet he believed lay beyond Neptune. The search at Lowell Observatory in Arizona led Clyde Tombaugh to discover Pluto in 1930. The phrase later drifted from that research into fringe claims.

NASA astronomers have pointed to an observational problem with the claim. A planet large enough to threaten Earth within months would have been visible in the night sky long before it arrived. Astronomers around the world would have been tracking it. No such object was ever observed.

The missed date did not retire the prediction. The date was later pushed back. The claim kept circulating online for years, attaching itself to fresh deadlines as earlier ones passed. NASA astronomers have kept giving the same answer: no hidden planet is on a collision course with Earth.

Fears that the Large Hadron Collider would create a black hole and swallow Earth peaked when CERN started circulating beams in the machine on Sept. 10, 2008. The planet carried on. CERN's safety assessment had already concluded that the collisions posed no danger.

The collider is a 27-kilometer ring of superconducting magnets buried beneath the French-Swiss border near Geneva. It was built to smash protons together at energies no earlier accelerator had reached. Physicists designed it to probe the basic structure of matter, including the search for the Higgs boson.

Those record energies gave critics their opening. Some argued that the collisions could produce microscopic black holes. In their scenario, a tiny black hole would sink toward the center of the planet. It would then grow by absorbing the matter around it. Other warnings involved hypothetical particles called strangelets.

The concern moved beyond online forums. Before startup, two critics, Walter Wagner and Luis Sancho, filed a lawsuit in U.S. federal court in Hawaii seeking to delay the collider. The case was dismissed.

CERN answered with a formal safety assessment. Its central argument rested on nature rather than on theory alone. Cosmic rays — high-energy particles from space — have struck Earth and other bodies for billions of years. Many of those impacts produce collisions at energies similar to or higher than anything the collider could reach.

The reasoning followed directly. If such collisions could create dangerous black holes, the damage would already be visible. Earth, the sun and other astronomical objects have absorbed that bombardment and still exist. The collider was repeating, under controlled conditions, something that happens in nature all the time.

Physics offered a second line of reassurance. Under Hawking radiation, a theory proposed by Stephen Hawking, a microscopic black hole would be expected to evaporate almost instantly rather than grow.

The Sept. 10 startup passed without trouble. On Sept. 19, 2008, a faulty electrical connection between magnets caused a helium leak that damaged part of the machine. Operations stopped for more than a year. The fault was mechanical and unrelated to black holes.

The collider resumed work in late 2009. In July 2012, CERN announced the discovery of a particle consistent with the Higgs boson.

Harold Camping, the head of the Family Radio network, predicted that the Rapture would take place on May 21, 2011, with the end of the world following months later. The date passed without incident. So did the revised end date he set for Oct. 21, 2011.

Camping was not a trained theologian. He studied civil engineering and ran a construction business before turning to religious broadcasting. Family Radio, a Christian network based in Oakland, California, carried his teaching to listeners across the U.S. and abroad.

His forecast grew out of a numerical reading of the Bible. Camping treated dates, numbers and time spans in scripture as clues that could be combined to build a calendar of the end times. On his count, May 21, 2011, fell 7,000 years after Noah's flood.

The prediction reached far beyond his regular listeners. Supporters paid for billboards in cities across the U.S. announcing Judgment Day. Some followers left their jobs or spent their savings spreading the warning.

When May 21 passed, Camping reinterpreted it. He said the day had brought an invisible, spiritual judgment rather than a visible one. He then set Oct. 21, 2011, five months later, as the date for the physical end of the world.

He suffered a stroke in June 2011, between his two announced dates. He later stepped back from hosting his regular broadcast program.

In March 2012, after both dates had passed, Camping acknowledged in a statement that his prediction had been wrong. He also called the attempt to name a date sinful.

The 2011 forecast was not his first. Camping had earlier made a prediction centered on 1994, laid out in a book titled 1994? When that year ended without the return he expected, he went back to his calculations. Those calculations later produced the 2011 dates.

Dec. 21, 2012, marked the end of the 13th baktun cycle in the Maya Long Count calendar, and popular claims recast that date as the end of the world. Maya scholars said the calendar simply rolled into a new cycle. The day passed without incident.

The Long Count is a day-counting system the ancient Maya used to track long spans of time. It records dates in nested units, much as a modern calendar nests days inside months and months inside years. The baktun is the largest unit in everyday use. One baktun equals 144,000 days, or roughly 394 years.

In Long Count notation, the date reached in December 2012 was written as 13.0.0.0.0. The string of zeros looks like an odometer rolling over. To some readers, it suggested that time itself was running out.

A completed baktun closed one counting period. It did not end the count, any more than Dec. 31 ends time. Maya inscriptions also refer to dates well beyond 2012. One text at Palenque, in southern Mexico, refers to a date in A.D. 4772.

The doomsday version spread through books, websites and television documentaries in the years before the date. The 2009 disaster film 2012, directed by Roland Emmerich, used the date as the backdrop for a global catastrophe. The movie put the idea in front of a mass audience.

Popular accounts tied the date to scenarios unrelated to Maya timekeeping. These included a collision with a hidden planet, a sudden reversal of Earth's magnetic poles and a special alignment with the center of the Milky Way. None of these ideas came from the Long Count itself.

NASA published material ahead of the date rebutting the doomsday claims. The agency explained in plain terms that no planet was on a collision course with Earth and that a magnetic pole reversal does not happen suddenly. It also released a video, framed as if from the day after, explaining why the world had not ended.

Dec. 21, 2012, fell on a Friday. The next day, the count moved to 13.0.0.0.1, and the 14th baktun was underway.

Viral posts claiming that Earth will go dark for several days have circulated online for years, and the predicted darkness has never arrived. One version spread around 2015 and claimed that NASA had confirmed days of darkness. NASA denied it.

These claims tend to follow a set pattern. A post names an upcoming date, attaches the name of a space agency or a scientist, and describes days without sunlight. The details change from one version to the next. The core promise stays the same.

Attaching NASA's name gave the 2015 post an air of official backing it did not have. The sun kept rising and setting on its normal schedule.

Basic astronomy shows why the claim cannot work as described. Earth turns on its axis about once every 24 hours. At any moment, roughly half of the planet faces the sun and is in daylight. For the whole planet to stay dark for days, the sun would have to stop lighting it.

Natural darkness during the day is brief and local. A total solar eclipse happens when the moon blocks the sun. Totality at any one spot lasts no more than about seven and a half minutes. The moon's shadow covers only a narrow path across Earth's surface.

Longer darkness does occur, but only in some places. Above the Arctic Circle, the sun stays below the horizon for at least one day around the winter solstice. Near the North Pole, that stretch lasts for months. At the same time, land south of the Antarctic Circle has round-the-clock daylight.

A blackout hoax is not the same thing as a power blackout. On April 28, 2025, the Iberian blackout cut electricity across Spain and Portugal. It was a real failure of the power grid. It had nothing to do with predictions that Earth would go dark.

The two ideas can blur when a real outage makes headlines. A grid failure is a problem with the electricity supply on the ground. The hoax describes the planet itself losing its light.
