‘The whole internet screams at once’: Downdetector logged 6.4 million U.S. outage reports in a week as Microsoft, the FAA, Chase, and Apple went down

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Microsoft’s email, the FAA, Chase, and Apple all went down within a week. Downdetector, the site people check when an app or a website stops working, logged 6.4 million U.S. reports in the seven days ending on Tuesday, four times a typical week’s number, and 14 million worldwide, three times its usual level. The company that runs it thinks the timing is mostly coincidence.

That puts the outage tracker at odds with the explanation taking hold in Washington, where Sen. Mark Warner has blamed organizations racing to patch software flaws that AI tools now find faster than companies can fix them. Downdetector’s data shows big outages getting rarer, and it can’t see what caused any of them. For companies watching their own update queues, the question is whether this week is a blip or the start of something.

Microsoft’s Outlook and Teams went down last week on Nov. 12 and 13, taking out email in more than 70 countries for almost 24 hours. With nearly 3.9 million U.S. reports and 7.8 million worldwide, it was Downdetector’s largest outage since Amazon Web Services went down in October 2025. Then on Friday, the FAA grounded every flight in the country in response to what it called unstable legacy equipment. Its initial investigation points to two sites, including a radio site in El Dorado, Ark., where its network contractor, L3Harris, had been installing a new router.

And on Monday, a software bug at Chase meant Chase and Wells Fargo customers couldn’t get to their money. Chase drew about 90,000 U.S. reports, roughly 165 times normal and more than twice any other U.S. bank outage this year. And on Tuesday, Apple Pay, iCloud, the App Store, and Apple Support drew about 81,000 reports as a result of what Apple said was a faulty internal update. Downdetector flagged 67 U.S. services that day, as opposed to the 16 it would get on a typical day, the broadest results of any day so far this year.

“You always wait for a bus, and then they come all at once,” Mark Giles, director of Ookla Research (Downdetector’s parent company), told Fortune. “I think this looks like a bit of a coincidence. There’s nothing really linking the outages.”

Downdetector, which collects user complaints about apps and websites, is the closest thing to a public scoreboard for these failures. Companies disclose their own outages, if at all, one at a time, and no U.S. agency publishes a running count. That’s part of what drives visitors to the site.

“We’re more connected than ever,” Giles said. “There’s these foundational points of the internet, which when something happens there, then the whole internet screams at once.”

And despite the four outages in a week, they’re becoming a rarer occurrence. Once the pandemic years are set aside, outages in Downdetector’s U.S. and U.K. data have fallen since about 2022. He said on average, Downdetector sees one large outage—which it defines as more than 50,000 U.S. reports for one company in a day—a little more than once a week. In the past year, only Christmas week and one week in June had more than this week’s four. Over the past 30 days the count is typical, because October was quiet, and Downdetector calls this week a cluster so far.

“This is a big blip, but it’s kind of on par for the course for what we’re seeing in terms of those longer-term trends,” he said. “Most of the time they’re fairly self-contained and remedied quite quickly. It’s the ones where you can’t withdraw money or you can’t access your emails that really get the headlines.”

Giles described a cycle he has seen in banking over the past three to four years. A company rolls out something new and spends less on making its systems resilient, and outages creep up. Then customers complain, the company invests, and outages fall. “I’m not saying that’s the case for Chase here,” he said. “A lot of these are just one-offs, maybe software problems or updates.”

The site can also overstate an outage, because it counts complaints rather than confirmed failures. Monday’s bank problems were a Chase outage, Giles said, but customers at other banks whose payments to Chase users failed reported their own banks as down. “We tend to overrepresent in some cases the actual outage,” he said. Outage news also brings more people to the site, which pushes up reports for other services.

Updates are now at the center of the explanation in Washington. Sen. Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, told the Financial Times there was no sign of a cyberattack and that the outages most likely came from organizations racing to patch software. Part of it is new technology: AI tools are now finding flaws faster than companies can safely fix them, and Warner said the pressure to fix them right away is straining systems that were never designed for that speed.

On Wednesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said at the New York Times’ DealBook Summit that the company had already pushed at least three critical patches that day, the FT reported. Citigroup CEO Jane Fraser, whose bank reported no outages, said the volume of patches moving through the world at once is “five-, 10-fold what we’ve ever seen before.” Still, neither Microsoft nor JPMorgan Chase tied their respective outages to patching AI-found flaws.

It proves trickier for companies to stay up to speed with this new tech when there are also nefarious actors using AI. Companies are rushing because attackers have the same tools: Giles said the patch rush “has to be an increasing concern for security and IT teams,” and it “speaks to a concern that hackers with swarms of AI agents are better able to find and exploit these.”

However, none of that shows up on Downdetector’s charts. “[It’s] not something that Downdetector can pick up on, though, as a root cause,” Giles said.

Currently, Downdetector flags a service only when its reports stand out from its own normal level. It plans to build AI tools over the next year to tie related spikes together and show which company actually failed.
