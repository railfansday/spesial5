With Age Comes a Unique Ability to Harness the Power of AI

Co-authored by George S. Everly, Jr., PhD, MA, ABPP, and Barry McGovern, Solutions Engineer, JBS International

Today, November 19, 2026, is an important day in the world of artificial intelligence (AI). Anthropic, a major artificial intelligence company, is beginning to sell ownership shares of stock in the company to the public. At the same time, Anthropic’s founder, Dario Amodei, issued a warning. He cautioned that AI is developing more rapidly than expected, especially through the process of recursive self-improvement, which allows the programs to improve themselves without human guidance.

We often look to younger generations when it comes to guidance regarding AI. Perhaps surprisingly, older generations may have much to offer in managing and directing AI, especially at the level of personal use. Decades of lived experience, pattern recognition, and knowing how to ask the right questions allow older generations to harness the power of AI through the process of “prompt engineering” in a way that is more mature and experience-informed than it is for younger generations.

On average, older adults (age 50 or older) are more skeptical of artificial intelligence (AI), less trusting of its responses to questions, and less likely to use it than younger adults. While caution is warranted, older adults possess a hidden “superpower” in the form of prompt engineering based upon lived experience that, at least at this point, allows them to become active managers of AI rather than non-critical users, overly cautious passive recipients, or worse yet, non-participants. Ironically, this “superpower” is more prevalent in older adults than younger adults and may increase to some degree with age.

For adults aged 18-40, the use of AI is likely 70 percent or higher. However, survey research conducted by AARP (AARP, September 23, 2025) found that familiarity with and use of AI decrease with age. About 47 percent of adults in their 50s reported knowing about and using AI, compared with 25 percent of those over 70.

As described in a previous post, AI may encourage passive, non-critical consumption of information. The risks of such passive consumption have yet to be fully determined, but AI may lead to the inhibition of critical thinking and the creation of a functional dependence. A recent TV commercial showed a character gleefully stating that she has difficulty thinking without AI. It is this very passivity that increases the risk of an untethered AI.

Younger users may seem to treat AI like an answer machine because they don't yet have the foundational knowledge to critically assess the validity of the information provided. Older adults, by contrast, have a built-in "lie-detector” from decades of career and life experience—in short, they know what a valid answer is supposed to look like and may be better positioned than younger adults to ask follow-up questions that can challenge AI.

Francis Bacon once noted, “If a man will begin with certainties, he shall end in doubts; but if he will be content to begin with doubts, he shall end in certainties.” So far, the errors or “overzealousness” exhibited by AI appear to be related to the questions asked of it. The more vague or less structured the questions, and the more repetitive the questions posed to AI, the greater its tendency to try to anticipate the desired answer and to “please” the questioner by “hallucinating” and creating spurious evidence to support the desired conclusions even though incorrect. In addition, AI does not possess the ability to independently assess the validity of the data it retrieves nor its recombinant output. As such, it may provide misleading or erroneous information.

The aging process is often accompanied by the accumulation of insights, knowledge, and informative experiences. These attributes may reveal themselves as the “superpower” ability to formulate specific, targeted questions. Rather than fostering passive consumption, knowledge and experience can foster informed, active consumption. It fosters the potential for a complementary, if not synergistic, relationship with AI wherein the person formulates knowledge- and experience-informed questions that not only target the efforts of AI but can serve as a series of checks and balances through AI’s recombinant efforts to provide answers.

For instance, a younger, less experienced person might ask AI, "Write me a business plan" (passive, vague), while a 60-year-old might ask, "Construct a business plan that overcomes these three most common reasons new tech companies fail" (experience-driven, targeted). Or, the less experienced consultant might ask AI, “Write an independent contractor’s agreement” (vague, too general), as opposed to “Create an independent contractor’s agreement that ensures the ownership of my pre-existing intellectual property does not transfer as a work for hire” (very specific, anticipating potential confusion over ownership).

Socrates believed the highest form of wisdom arose from introspection and self-reflection. Just as he used questions to challenge his students to provide the best answers to the most pressing questions of his day, perhaps we can harness questions in the Socratic method to guide AI to provide us with the answers to the most pressing questions of our day.
