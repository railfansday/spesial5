Does AI Really Need a Data Center? Lowdown Labs Bets on Efficient AI for Ordinary CPUs

Tech companies are spending heavily on new data centers to keep up with demand for artificial intelligence, and new campuses are often described by how many gigawatts of power it will draw. For most people outside the industry, AI means a chatbot run by one of a few large companies on a server farm somewhere far away. That setup comes with a heavy energy bill, and the usual industry answer to rising demand is to build more of the same.

Lowdown Labs, a Seattle startup founded by researchers and engineers from Caltech and Georgia Tech, is looking for the answer in software. The company builds AI models that need serious computing power to train but can run day to day on ordinary computers without graphics chips. Its first commercial product, the FEVER Multimodal Database, is meant to show that this efficient AI approach can handle real business workloads at scale.

Every AI model goes through two stages. Training is when the model learns from large amounts of data, and it happens once or every so often. Inference is when the finished model does its job, such as recognizing an image or answering a question. Inference happens every time someone uses the model, so its energy cost keeps adding up long after training is over.

Lowdown Labs trains its models on high-performance machines, as the rest of the industry does. The difference starts at inference, which the company runs on commodity CPUs, the standard processors found in regular servers and laptops. Graphics processing units (GPUs), the chips that fill most AI data centers, are left out of this stage entirely.

To make that work, the team changed the math inside the model. The widely used Transformer architecture compares every part of the input with every other part, so its memory needs grow with the square of the input length. The company replaces this with Fourier encoders, which rely on a long-established technique called the Fast Fourier Transform, and with linear attention, whose memory needs grow in step with the input. In practice, doubling the length of the input roughly doubles the memory the model needs, where the standard approach would need about four times as much. The company sums up the result as AI that runs on watts instead of gigawatts.

FEVER is a computer vision model, meaning software that lets computers understand what is in a picture, combined with a database that stores and searches the results. According to the company, it indexes about 5 million photos a day. The same API also handles video and audio files alongside documents, and it removes duplicates as it goes. Teams can query the archive with keywords or SQL and send finished datasets to AI tools through the Model Context Protocol (MCP).

Many companies that need this kind of image and video dataset curation assume they have to build the system themselves. FEVER lets them skip that project, and the company says the total cost comes out 10 to 100 times lower than the alternatives.

Lowdown Labs sees FEVER as evidence that efficient AI can take on jobs usually left to the largest AI providers. Processing a steady stream of images and video is demanding work, and the company argues that doing it on modest hardware shows the same methods can go further.

The company's next step is a large language model (LLM), the type of model behind chatbots, that runs entirely without data centers. The company says it is close to good results and plans to release the model soon. A language model like that would let businesses and individuals run AI on hardware they already own and keep their data under their own control.

The company calls its goal responsible AI: models that people can manage and use safely, without the environmental cost of ever-larger server farms. For companies sitting on growing media archives, FEVER is the part of that plan they can put to work today.
