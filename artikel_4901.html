Life Is Not a Competitive Sport

We have all fallen into the comparison game at some point. Someone shares pictures from their exotic international trip. Someone else shares a professional achievement on LinkedIn. Yet another shares photos of their picture-perfect family. Whether these posts share the full story is a topic for another day, but the underlying commonality is how they can make us feel: like we will never be good enough or are somehow falling behind.

There is an excellent book by Rosamund and Benjamin Zander, The Art of Possibility, which touches on these themes. In it, the Zanders discuss what they call "the measurement world", describing how we all evaluate, compare, rank, and assign value to ourselves and others based on the variables that are most important to us. Their suggested antidote to this problem is to consider how one can uniquely contribute to the world around them without getting caught up in the comparison game.

They quote Martha Graham as told to Agnes DeMille to illustrate this idea:

“There is a vitality, a life force, a quickening that is translated through you into action, and because there is only one of you in all time. This expression is unique. And if you block it, it will never exist through any other medium and be lost. The world will not have it.

It is not your business to determine how good it is, nor how valuable it is, nor how it compares with other expressions. It is your business to keep it yours clearly and directly, to keep the channel open. You do not even have to believe in yourself or your work. You have to keep open and aware directly to the urges that motivate you. Keep the channel open" (as cited in Zander & Zander, 2000, p. 116).

The daily practice of humility can help us identify how we can be a unique and positive contribution to the world around us. Researchers have called humility "the just right view of the self," meaning you can identify both your strengths and weaknesses without feeling conceited or ashamed of them (Davis et al., 2016, p. 192). This prevents ego defensiveness and enables individuals to put their strengths to use and to recognize and encourage others' strengths without feeling threatened by them (Owens et al., 2013). Essentially, there is no need to compare ourselves to others, because we all have unique contributions to make.

While competition may be healthy in certain contexts, the continual ranking of ourselves against others is ultimately dysfunctional and detrimental to our own success. We can, however, learn from those we admire and aspire to be like. For example, replacing comparison with curiosity can prompt us to learn more about others in order to improve ourselves. Instead of feeling inferior or jealous, try coming up with a question you'd like to ask another person about their success, achievement, or good fortune. This also helps you see the context and effort behind their success and reduces the notion that their achievements can only be attributed to who they are.

It's difficult to avoid everyone's highlight reel on social media: a promotion, an accomplishment, a milestone, a once-in-a-lifetime trip, but start to notice whether their good fortune automatically becomes a judgment about your own progress. Humility prompts us to recognize that we don't know everything and that we see only part of the story. We can appreciate what others have accomplished while continuing to pursue a life grounded in our own values. Someone else’s success does not have to become the measure of our worth.
