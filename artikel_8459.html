Glorious! (Last Day Part I) — John Muir Trail Day 23

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

I apologize in advance for the ending to this post.  After some deliberation, I have decided to break the narrative of my last day on the John Muir Trail into two.  This narrative will cover the last day up to about one or two miles from the terminus at Happy Isles.  The next shall cover that ending as well as my departure from Yosemite.  The reason for this you will understand when you read about the very unfortunate incident that occurred just as I reached the end of my hike.

With the excitement of Last Day on the Trail, I woke up around 5:30 a.m. and knew I would not go back to sleep.  I had risen to pee several times in the night, and each time I was filled with awe to see the silent sentinels of granite towering all around me in the starlight.  With the glimmer of dawn, it was even better.

With that silhouette, it is easy to see how they became called “cathedrals”

Despite the weather report, it was not as cold as the night before, and there was no sign of frost on the ground.   I believe the temperature hovered just above freezing.

I wanted to start especially early today because I was catching the YARTS (Yosemite Area Regional Transportation System) bus to Merced where son Gabriel was to pick me up.  I thought there was a 4:30 bus that got to Merced at about 8:30 and I figured we could be back in Santa Rosa by midnight if we made that schedule.  There were earlier and later departures (up to 7:00 p.m.) but that seemed the most reasonable to aim for with a fast pace.

So, I did my morning chores and ablutions, including breaking down my tent, with its broken pole.  (The repair had held solidly all night.)

The shear on the broken pole was mild enough the repair was successful

As I did so, I watched the morning sunlight gradually fill the space between the mountains and start to land on their upper ridges.

This spot was just such a glorious location.

I understood why I was I alone up there.  The site had no water and was not even within a mile of a water source.  The night before I needed water to soak my dinner.  This morning I needed water to soak my breakfast, and to fill my bidet.  That’s a lot of water needed before we even consider basic hydration.  Even with all that, the location on this high little mesa of rock, with such fantastic views in all directions, made the water risk totally worth it.

As I started down, I realized my elevation profile for the day included 6,660 feet of descent to the valley floor, with only a bit over 700 feet of ascent.  Right away I knocked out a few of those 700 feet as the trail started by climbing a small hill.

Cresting the hill, I started down, encountering a group of older hikers coming up.  They spent the night at Sunrise High Sierra Camp and were day-hiking from there.  They asked me where I started from and were duly impressed when I replied, “Mount Whitney.”  I could have said “Cottonwood Portal” but that would not be a location they would recognize.  I will admit, I was wearing three weeks of aches and pains, of wind and snow, of grand views and disgusting food, as a badge of honor.  A little smug?  Guilty as charged.

Soon I arrived at the large, flat, marshy meadow that borders the Sunrise Camp.  There, on the flat, I could lift my poles and really open it up.  As I sped up, I was thinking, “I’ve got to make it to Curry Village before 4:30!”

It was inevitable; I started to go down very steeply.  I realized I had to  treat my knees gingerly, because descent is truly hard on the knees.  I used the poles as much as possible to arrest my momentum and cushion them.

Getting the water at the lake the day before was a wise decision.  As it was, there were no sources of water, and I was running low.  When I finally encountered a small stream, the upper reaches of Sunrise Creek, I filled up despite only having a trickle to draw from.  That was prudent because I was trying to go fast and was dehydrating myself in the process.

I enjoyed coming across a few John Muir Trail southbound hikers just starting off.  We met up at that stream, and I told them their big worry might be cold.  The last day of August is still summer down in the lowlands, but in the high Sierra, it was already Fall, and the temperatures were dropping.

Continuing downhill, I came to an extensive area of burned trees.  There must have been a big fire in the recent past.  One advantage of the open land was the first clear view of the Half Dome in the distance.

The rock looms across the meadow of burned trees

Another advantage of the open sunshine was to create a terrain hospitable to lizards, and they seemed to be all over the place.  I may have mentioned that I am fond of reptiles?

The disadvantage was the mountain whitethorn.  As my AI friend points out about this plant:  “Dormant seeds can lie in the soil for decades until intense wildfire heat cracks their hard coats, triggering mass germination after the first rains.”

See how the thorns encroach on the trail?

While an important part of the ecological succession after fire, this thorny thicket was so robust it grew all over the countryside, even edging onto the trail in places.  As I was wearing shorts, the thorns were scratching my legs.  Although the scratches were superficial, and did not draw blood, they were unpleasant.

I spotted a horse train coming across the open land.  As it approached, I realized it was led by the same horsewoman I had seen about ten days before.  I said, “Hi!  I recognize you!”  In a friendly voice she said she remembered me too.  I had taken a video of her ascending as I was coming down from Muir Pass.

I continued dropping down through the burned area.  Eventually, I passed another stock train going northbound this time, as I was.  I had an unpleasant encounter there but managed to pass these animals and the humans with them.  I accelerated and soon re-entered the forest.

The forest was cooler and I kept moving quickly, always aware that I needed to get to the YARTS by 4:30 p.m.  At length I saw a person on the trail ahead.  She looked familiar.  As I got closer, I realized – it was my friend Sally!  I had not seen her in so many days I was resigned to assuming that our interaction was over.  But here she was!  We had a brief and joyful reunion, took a few selfies, and then I was on my way again.

Sally and Papa George looking very happy to see each other — and to be so close to the end of the John Muir Trail!

Now as I moved down the trail the Half Dome got bigger and bigger.  At a distance it had a kind of pristine beauty, but closer up it was dramatic, almost ominous.

Still taken from the burnt forest — closer up you need a fish-eye to shoot it!

After I passed the turn-off for the summit, there were ever more people on the trail.  Many of them were descending from Half Dome.  As they spoke of their accomplishment, of the challenge they faced in ascending and descending the cables, I felt a twinge of regret that I had not been able to get a permit.

The trail flattened out and I began looking for a place to have lunch.  It was hard to believe, this would be my last lunch on the John Muir Trail!  I found a log overlooking the Merced River and settled down to fill my water bottles again and to eat.  Two people were there — an older man and a younger woman who was his daughter.  They dared each other to go swimming and eventually both went in.  I teased them, “Watch out for the piranhas in the river!”

They laughed and we bantered back and forth for a while until they packed up and left.  I ate the last of my tamales.  It was sinking in that this was really getting close to the end.

Where I was sitting, I saw a piece of trash and was about to pick it up to carry it out.  “Who just tossed this here?” I wondered.  Then I looked more closely and saw a lot more trash in the vicinity.  This was not just a random act of littering, this was a sign of a society in a kind of crisis.  Yosemite is one of our iconic jewels; how could we be sullying it?

I swear I did not “assemble” this picture; that is what I found (and more)

While I was eating, a large group of teenagers and young adults, whom I had passed earlier, now passed me again.  There were probably eight or ten of them.  When I resumed hiking, once again I went past them.  Just after that, the trail went down a steep rock.  As I descended the rock face, I realized it was suddenly much more technical than it had been.  Just as the trail disappeared entirely, I looked around to find the whole group of chattering young people had followed me down the rock!

It turned out we had missed an obvious turn right at the top of the rock face.  As we retreated, I said, “That will teach you to follow me!  The blind leading the blind!”  But we were all laughing about it and having fun.

Shortly after this, the whole group stopped because we passed a bathroom.  Yes, you read that correctly, we were so close to “silly-visation”  that there was an actual, no foolin’, bathroom on the trail!  I pressed on, worried about my deadline.

Up ahead I saw a lot of people hanging out by the river, with many more swimming in its cool pools.  This was just above the spot where the Merced River shoots over the top of the rock, to drop 594 feet.  Yes, the iconic Nevada Falls.

Folks enjoying the Merced River, just above Nevada Falls

I felt I was getting really close to the end and my spirit soared.  Yes, after weeks of relative solitude, there were far too many people here, but I did not resent them.  They had a right to be here too (just NOT to litter, please!).  I appreciated that there were lots of Americans, but also Europeans and Asians and Africans.  All were here to enjoy and appreciate the phenomenal natural wonder that we are blessed to have in the United States.  Amazing!

As I continued, there was a section carved out of the side of the rock to create a sort of channel into the granite hillside.  That must have taken some engineering skill!

Some intrepid team just cut that path right out of the granite!

Descending further, some views of Nevada Falls from the side highlighted just how long the drop was for the water rushing over the top.

The bottom was getting closer, and I could see some of the infrastructure at the bottom of the valley.

Suddenly I spotted a familiar couple ahead of me. An older man and a younger woman.  It was the father-daughter duo who had swum in the river where I ate lunch!  I snuck up behind them and whispered loudly, “Beware the piranhas!”  They turned, saw who it was, and burst out laughing.

We started to chat and they were very interested and somewhat impressed at my having done the John Muir Trail.  Falling into step with each other, the young woman talked about how she was planning to do the Mont Blanc Trail the next year.

They asked a lot of questions about thru-hiking, and it was good for all of us.  I induced them to pick up their pace a little, because I was still trying to make that YARTS departure and was not sure how long it might take to find the bus stop.

We were laughing and enjoying the easy downhill, when the trail suddenly ended at a tee.  If there was a sign there, we didn’t see it, so we were puzzled as to whether to turn left or right.  I stood in the middle of the intersection trying to figure it out, as they stood behind me, by the side of the trail we had come down.  Just then…
