New Sonos software and firmware updates are rolling out. Here's what you can expect.

Sonos’ promise to release its AI-upgraded voice assistant is nearing its “Fall 2026” deadline, and before fans can use the highly anticipated update, the company continues to make small improvements to its app and player features.

This time, Sonos added System Health, a new in-app page to access a more comprehensive view of your system(s), though it’s only available on iOS for now. However, a new blog post states that iOS and Android users can expect volume presets, more interaction with the app miniplayer, and spoken voice prompts for the Ace Ultra headphones.

I poked around in the app and tried some of the latest features. Here’s what to expect.

If your system consists of multiple speakers, a mix of modern and legacy speakers, or multiple systems, you’re bound to experience a hiccup and be unsure which speaker is causing the issue.

It usually takes me some time to Google my issue and scroll through the Sonos Community forum, Reddit, or the official Sonos help documents to find suggestions and troubleshoot it. Sonos’ new System Health feature provides an overview of your systems, showing your network status and name, as well as the number of speakers and rooms in each system.

According to Sonos’ blog post, System Health can detect a Double Network Address issue, which usually occurs when you introduce a new router to your home or plug a mesh system into your ISP-provided router. The result is two home networks, and your Sonos speakers will split their connection between them, flagging “System not found” errors, missing speakers, or connection dropouts.

System Health is in the Settings tab, under the Help Center menu.

Once you tap into your system, you can see all of its speakers, with unconnected speakers bearing a red “Not connected” badge, and wireless speakers showing a black “Powered off” badge when they’re off.

Then, you can see a speaker’s connection information, including status, Wi-Fi signal strength, signal/noise ratio, frequency, IP address, and more. You can also find its volume output, Trueplay setup status, line-in connection, voice control status, serial number, hardware model and version, and which Sonos platform it operates on.

System Health can also show you your account details, Sonos ID, Controller ID, and your current app build version. System Health comes after Sonos updated the System Updates tab, which now displays an overview of your app, system, and headphone update status. This page also links to the Sonos release notes.

Sonos’ blog post states that this feature is new and still open to change, especially if the Sonos community has a particularly overwhelming response to it. The blog post also disclaims that this feature is a first look, not a deep dive.

However, after a brief first use, I see it as a great way to quickly access all the details you need about your systems and speakers, especially if you’re doing some rewiring, moving a speaker to a new room, or general troubleshooting.

System Health is rolling out to iOS users first. I updated my Sonos app in the App Store and was able to access it, but Sonos’ blog post states that this update is a phased rollout.

Updates to the Sonos app and its player are a long time coming. After receiving several new ways to control your speakers’ volume (I think we’re up to three), Sonos is realizing that granular volume control is a high priority for many Sonos users, including me.

The in-app volume dial is cool, and the interactive volume scrubber is a nice touch, but there’s nothing more jarring than wanting to play some tunes on my kitchen counter speaker, only for it to blast at 80% from when my husband was watching football the night before.

Sonos’ latest big update to volume this time around is Presets, a way to start playback in a specific room at a set volume. I haven’t found this feature in my app’s update yet, but I think it’s because my hardware needs an update. Presets will be available on iOS and Android.

Fortunately, I did use another update to the Sonos miniplayer: swipe to skip. What a teeny, tiny but useful feature. It works just like the miniplayer in Apple Music and Spotify; when the app is open, you can swipe left to skip to the next song.

One major caveat: swiping right to replay a track doesn’t work — at least not yet. Swiping both ways seems like a given to me, so it feels odd that you can’t. Still, being able to swipe the miniplayer to the next track is better than always having the main player open to toggle tracks. This feature is also coming to iOS and Android.

Sonos’ miniplayer is getting so much better; you can swipe to the next track and easily control volume from it, even when you have the Ace or Ace Ultra connected (I tried). The in-app volume dial also works with Sonos headphones.

Speaking of the Ace Ultra, a firmware update is available. A new Vocal Guidance feature can announce the battery percentage when you power on the headphones, when the audio mode changes from ANC to Transparency, and which device your headphones are connected to via Bluetooth.

It feels like this update is the final stretch before Sonos 27voice arrives, as the release notes suggest. It appears Sonos is laying the groundwork for speakers to support the AI-powered voice assistant in Early Access. Once it’s here, I’ll test it as soon as I can.
