But Our Flag Was Still There

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Imagine a time when Americans could be arrested by soldiers and imprisoned without the ordinary opportunity to challenge their detention before a judge. When a former congressman could be tried by a military commission for a political speech, then banished from the country. When newspaper offices could be occupied by troops and publication stopped. Imagine an editor imprisoned in the very fortress his grandfather had immortalized in a song about American freedom.

O say, can you see By the dawn’s early light

I woke up this morning thinking about the question at the beginning of our national anthem.

After an election, we tend to look for answers. This morning, I find myself more interested in what we can still see.

I see a country in which people cast ballots and those ballots have consequences. A country in which officeholders can be challenged and voters can change the direction of government. I see people who care enough to argue, sometimes bitterly, about the future of a place they still believe is worth fighting for.

That is an exhausting way to govern ourselves, but what a remarkable inheritance!

What so proudly we hailed At the twilight’s last gleaming

The president in those opening examples was Abraham Lincoln.

His administration’s restrictions on civil liberties are part of our history, even if they sit uncomfortably alongside the achievements for which we rightly honor him.

Beginning in 1861, Lincoln authorized the suspension of habeas corpus, the means by which a detainee can ask a court to examine the legality of detention. The Constitution permits suspension in cases of rebellion or invasion when public safety requires it. Whether the president could exercise that authority without Congress was fiercely contested. The practical consequence was that people could be held by military authorities without the ordinary judicial protection against unlawful imprisonment.

In September 1862, his proclamation, circulated by the War Department as General Orders No. 141, subjected people discouraging enlistment or engaging in broadly described disloyal practices aiding the rebellion to military trial. It also suspended habeas corpus for those detained by military authority.

These were sweeping powers, with consequences far beyond the battlefield.

Whose broad stripes and bright stars Through the perilous fight

In 1863, a former Ohio congressman was arrested after delivering an antiwar speech, tried by a military commission, and convicted. Lincoln changed his punishment from imprisonment to banishment into Confederate territory.

Another critic, a Baltimore newspaper editor, had been arrested in 1861 and confined at Fort McHenry. His grandfather was Francis Scott Key. Think about that for a moment. The grandson of the man who wrote the words we sing about the land of the free became a political prisoner inside the fort associated with those words.

He was a Southern sympathizer. The former congressman’s politics were deeply objectionable in their defense of a slaveholding order. But we do not need to admire either man’s cause to take seriously the government’s treatment of him. Constitutional protections have little substance if they depend on our approval of the person invoking them.

O’er the ramparts we watched Were so gallantly streaming

General Ambrose Burnside ordered the suppression of the Chicago Times in 1863. Lincoln intervened to have that order revoked. That correction belongs in the account as much as the original abuse.

But in 1864, after the New York World and Journal of Commerce published a forged presidential proclamation, Lincoln himself ordered military action against the papers, including seizure of their printing establishments and the arrest of their editors and publishers.

The forgery was serious. So was the use of military power against newspapers.

All of this happened while the country was fighting a rebellion rooted in slavery, with its survival genuinely in doubt. That context is essential to understanding Lincoln’s decisions. Even so, it does not place those decisions beyond scrutiny and reminds us today there is nothing new under the sun.

I can regard Lincoln as a great president and still recoil at some of the powers exercised under his administration. An honest affection for America must leave room for that.

And the rockets’ red glare The bombs bursting in air

Our anthem was born of an actual bombardment. The danger was physical, and the outcome could not yet be known.

We sometimes speak of our own political moment as though we alone have discovered uncertainty. Every alarming development becomes unprecedented. Every election becomes the final opportunity to save the country.

Some warnings deserve our attention. Rights can erode, and institutions can fail. History offers plenty of evidence for the absolute necessity vigilance.

This country has been torn apart by civil war. It has tolerated profound injustice while congratulating itself on liberty. It has denied the protection of its promises to people who had every right to claim them.

And Americans have challenged those failures…often at great personal cost and against a majority quite comfortable with the way things were.

The work has never been easy, and progress has never been secure. The end of slavery did not prevent the injustices that followed. A constitutional promise could exist for generations before people received anything approaching its protection.

But the promise gave people something to demand. It still does.

Gave proof through the night That our flag was still there

I won’t comment on the outcome itself, but I have great hope after an election like this. The system is working. Imperfectly, unevenly, and with people understandably dissatisfied by its results. Yet democracy remains alive. Voters can register disapproval and impose consequences. Political power remains something that must answer to the public.

One election cannot certify the health of an entire republic. It can, however, demonstrate that the mechanisms of accountability still operate. I think we should allow ourselves to recognize that today, across political lines.

I spend much of my life traveling beyond our borders. That has made me more conscious of how much I value the ability to criticize my government openly and expect another opportunity to vote. Neither should be taken for granted.

We have built something very special here. Its failures are real, and so is its capacity for correction.

Repairing what is broken will require patient work long after the election coverage ends. Some damage will take years to undo. Some losses cannot be restored to the people who suffered them.

Still, Americans before us have undertaken that work under circumstances far more desperate than ours. We can honor them by continuing it.

This morning, I feel grateful that the country remains ours to shape. Grateful that disappointment can find expression at a ballot box. And I’m hopeful that we retain enough humility to recognize our mistakes and enough resolve to address them.

The anthem ends with a question. After all this time, it remains a question worth asking ourselves.

O say, does that star-spangled banner yet wave O’er the land of the free and the home of the brave
