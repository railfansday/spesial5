A Roundtable of Men in Their Nineties Taught Me the Secrets of Longevity

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

Every single thing I learned is something you can start doing right now.

Alan Patricof, 92, is one of my idols. The legendary venture capitalist was an early investor in enterprises as varied as AOL, Apple, and New York magazine. More important to me, aside from his incredible career and philanthropic work, he is a man who believes in always moving forward into the future. He co-launched a fund called Primetime Partners when he was 85. At 88, he wrote his first book, No Red Lights: Reflections on Life, 50 Years in Venture Capital and Never Driving Alone. He was the oldest participant—and finisher—in the New York City Marathon at 88 and got remarried at 89 to the globally recognized art advisor Barbara Guggenheim.

So when Patricof—who has also become one of my friends—called me with a request to join in on a lunch he was hosting for 12 men in their 90s, I was intrigued. I have been reporting on longevity for several years now and lead a company called ROARforward focused on the subject. Patricof asked me to come and talk about what I had learned about longevity and the possibility of the 100-year life. I agreed, but I immediately thought of it differently—as a master class in wisdom and experience where I stood to learn at least as much from these men as they could learn from me.

When I saw the list of guests, it was a bit intimidating. It included a former CEO of a Fortune 100 company, a vice chairman emeritus at one of the largest banks on the planet, a major real estate developer, and a man who has his name on one of the most prestigious health systems in New York. And those are just the ones who agreed to be identified—some asked to remain anonymous.

You get the idea: This was not an everyday group of 90-somethings but rather a gathering of highly accomplished, successful men who continue to thrive in a variety of industries and disciplines. That only made me more interested, though I imagined it might take some work to translate their habits and the ways they were spending their time into things more accessible to everyday people.

Luckily, I would turn out to be wrong about that.

As the men assembled in a private room at Patroon, a popular Manhattan restaurant, I saw that many of them already knew each other, some from as far back as grade school. As Patricof went over ground rules, including no talk of politics (both parties were represented) and no talk of sex (though Viagra did find its way into the conversation), they moved around the room, shaking hands, asking about wives and kids, and explaining what was keeping them busy.

That was my first clue that the behaviors that helped these men stay robust into their tenth decade had little to do with wealth. Research has shown that cultivating deep, healthy relationships is one of the best things you can do to increase your chances of having a long, healthy life. These men were living examples of what that looks like, and it’s free. (Though not if you have your lunches at Patroon.)

These men took care of three major aspects of their lives: health, head, and heart. First, they were committed to a fitness regimen and healthy eating habits. Second, they were highly engaged and curious individuals who valued a strong sense of purpose. Third, each of them had deep relationships with loved ones, whether a spouse, family, or friends.

You may notice that these are all universal virtues. They’re also things that can give you a sense of fulfillment if you’re only in your 80s, or 70s, or even younger. They aren’t solely investments in the future. You do them now, and they benefit you now. Which is a good feeling and precisely what makes longevity worth it in the first place. As one of the men said, “If today is the last day of my life, I’ve had a helluva run.”

“At 90, I tried boxing at a gym near my home in Harlem,” said George Hirsch, 92, before adding, proudly, that he went a full round. Hirsch was the longtime chairman of the New York Roadrunners running club. While he is no longer out doing road races, he is on a treadmill four or five days a week in a walk-run regimen. He supplements that with rowing, the elliptical machine, weights, and, apparently, the occasional prizefight, in a 60-minute routine.

For a gathering men who were 90 or older, this was a remarkably healthy group. It’s true they all have had access to the best health care, but none had problems with issues that come for everyone, like cognitive decline, diabetes, and heart disease. That word routine seemed to be the key. Routine, regimen—take your pick. These men make a point of committing to physical activity rather than just dabbling in it. One told me, “I think the secret to my life physically is that I’ve worked with the same trainer three days a week for 29 years,” adding that “even when I travel, I take my iPad and work out with him wherever I am.”

In some cases, these routines would be the envy of any younger man! The men had all played some type of sports when they were younger and carried that behavior deep into their adult lives. Since movement and exercise are the top natural medicines for a long, healthy life, these guys were on it. Activities included walking, weight training, stationary bicycling, swimming, pickleball, tennis, and downhill skiing. Two of them have a regular tennis game, and one of the oldest in the group is still an avid golfer. Most of them go to a gym or have a personal trainer. While a few admitted that they were slower as they got older—a sign of remarkable maturity in a man, to admit that—they were all active.

They are also conscious of what they eat. They agreed that they consume less food than in the past and drink very little, if at all. As Hirsch said, “I never drank much, maybe a beer or glass of wine with a meal. But I don’t drink anymore—it just doesn’t mean that much to me.”

Their attention to eating habits stood out. One thing that especially interested them was GLP-1’s, because of their impact on diet but also other potential benefits. As I shared what I’ve learned about longevity, including the frontiers of research on the topic, they asked question after question about the emergence of GLP-1’s and other classes of drugs that may enhance individual longevity. That kind of constant, engaged questioning was a hallmark of the group.

Only one of the men at the lunch defined himself as “retired.” The others said they were, at most, “semiretired.” While they are not necessarily working full throttle in what was their main career, to a man they all have very busy days—and that busyness, I found, is driven by a voracious curiosity, which is one of the best ways to strengthen and maintain brain health.

Ken Langone is a great example. He came from a working-class family in Queens and is still in awe of his good fortune. He’s taken it to mean that, as long as he’s able, he should be doing everything he can not to squander what he has. “I won big, and I can’t tell you how grateful I am,” he said, adding, “We should be giving, not taking.”

He is in his office three days a week and works from home two days a week, engaged in a public board, three private boards, his family office, and other investments. His commitment to public health is incredibly important to him; he serves as chair emeritus of NYU Langone Health.

“At 91,” Langone says, “my biggest issue is boredom, sitting around with nothing to do.”

Jim Freund, also 91, was a high-powered attorney into his early 60s, did a stint as a mediator after that, and now has a robust life as a writer, photographer, and musician.

He’s written 20 fiction and nonfiction books and has produced 25 albums of music featuring him at the piano. For six years, he has circulated a monthly blog for more than 500 friends and colleagues that includes the details of various projects he’s working on—an essay or short fiction, a piece of music, a portfolio of photos.

One expression of this curiosity is that all the men want to keep current. They follow the news, are dedicated readers, and have a deep curiosity about emerging trends. They don’t just read print; they’re on their phones, Kindles, Audible—anywhere there’s action. And AI—they’re on ChatGPT like everyone else. Some even have digital assistants.

But being current doesn’t just mean trendspotting. It can also mean having hobbies and projects that force you to engage with the world as it is. All of these men had ambitious new endeavors in the works.

Hirsch now has a bimonthly podcast with the running legend Amby Burfoot called “Running: State of the Sport.” Dan Rose, 97, former president and chairman of Rose Associates, the company that built Pentagon City and One Financial Center in Boston, is writing a new book. Patricof and his wife are taking Yiddish lessons. At least one man reported that he was finally learning the piano.

For all of them, it is important that their activities, whatever they may be, connect to deeper meaning and purpose.

Larry Leeds, 97, the former CEO and chairman of the apparel company Manhattan Industries, now works at Buckingham Capital Management five days a week buying and selling securities. It allows him to keep up with trends and investments. But it’s far from the most important thing to which he’s dedicated his energy.

Leeds hopes the leading line in his obituary is the $27 million he raised to launch the Cary Leeds Center for Tennis and Learning in the South Bronx in 2015. Dedicated to athletic instruction and academic enrichment for underprivileged youth—health and head—the center is also an expression of the most important thing in Leeds’s life: his family. It is named after his son, who competed in six US Opens and five Wimbledon championships before dying at the age of 45.

Sitting there in Patroon, I heard stories of how these men had committed to keeping themselves healthy and how their curiosity led them to continue to engage with the world at a point in their lives when they could just as easily have settled into insular routine. But it was the lunch itself that testified to their commitment to their hearts. Ninety years in, they were still making a point of getting together, speaking, laughing, and growing closer to other people.

Upon arriving, as we sat and introduced ourselves, Patricof explained that he wanted to do this lunch not only to learn from one another at this stage of life but also to simply enjoy socializing with peers—like many women do on a regular basis. Women live five to six more years than men; maybe it is their commitment to deep relationships, he suggested, that is one of the key factors of their greater longevity.

Women came up in another way too: Everyone attributed their health and longevity to having a loving partner.

The majority of the men have had incredibly long marriages. Nine of the 12 had been married for more than 60 years. Two had been married for 70 years. Then there was Freund, who joked that he, too, has been married for 60 years—just not all to the same woman. (He is married to his second wife, and they are at 41 years and counting.)

They all also have children and grandchildren, whom they are in touch with more than a few times a week—something any of us who are parents can do.

Rose has a unique ritual. “We have a family video conference every Sunday night with family from all parts of the country. It’s life enhancing and a time to connect and give everyone a chance to share accomplishments and disappointments with each other,” he explained.

At this stage of their lives, many have lost longtime friends, but they all strive to make new friends, some young, some old. In fact, there’s already a plan in place to replicate the lunch so that a bunch of other 90-somethings can be invited into the conversation. Here’s the thing I noticed about this: What got these guys excited about another lunch wasn’t simply that they enjoyed each other’s company or having a laugh with peers about Viagra. They were also inspired by one other. That must count for something.

I walked away inspired, too, and in awe of how these men live. They have to walk a line that most of us don’t yet, and maybe never will. Part of being alive—especially for this group—is thinking about the future, trying to figure out what’s next, planning another pursuit, but now they all admit that their horizon of looking out, say, 25 years is no longer a reality.

In the face of that tension, they’ve found a way to be optimistic about their future while at the same time savoring the present. None complained about getting older. None talked about their aches and pains. Yes, they faced growing limitations, but they all seemed to take them in stride.

“I do my best to love my life and try to have the same intensity and diligence that I had in my younger years,” said Leeds.

Langone said, “When I can no longer take care of myself and I’m totally dependent on someone else, then that’s the time to go,” adding that “God has his plan.”

And Patricof has his. He’s already publicly stated that he plans to live to 114, which would be 22 more years. I pointed out that the world’s oldest verified living person, Ethel Caterham in the UK, just turned 117, and I challenged Alan to break that record. He gladly told me that he accepts the challenge, and I don’t doubt he’ll make a real run at it. I have to admit I have an ulterior motive: I look forward to my lunch with centenarians. And supercentenarians.
