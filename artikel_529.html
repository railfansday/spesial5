The 8 Best Movies to Stream Right Now (November 2026)

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

November is here, the holidays are approaching, and it’s gotten cold outside—the perfect time of year to cozy up inside with some of the best films gracing streaming services.

This month, there’s plenty of Halloween hangover for horror fans, with viral sensation Backrooms making corridors and empty rooms unimaginably spooky on HBO Max, Thanksgiving carving out a spot for grindhouse connoisseurs on Hulu, and the transgressive Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma proving the genre can have brains and blood over on Mubi.

If you prefer your thrills giant-sized, look no further than Netflix, where the king of monsters can be seen stomping across Japan in the Academy Award–winning Godzilla Minus Zero.

Elsewhere, the Earth itself is in peril in a double bill of animated spectaculars. A warning from the future arrives in the stunning time-travel fantasy Arco, taking viewers on an imaginative journey across centuries on Hulu, while The Day the Earth Blew Up on HBO Max places Looney Tunes icons Daffy Duck and Porky Pig as the planet’s last line of defense. (We’re cooked.)

Here are WIRED’s picks of the best movies to watch right now.

Drawing inspiration from the works of Sylvain Chomet and Studio Ghibli in equal measure, director Ugo Bienvenu has created an absolutely gorgeous piece of animation. This French science fantasy epic follows 10-year-old Arco (Oscar Tresanini in French, Juliano Krue Valdi in English), who time-travels from his home in 2932 in hopes of seeing dinosaurs. Instead, he crashes in 2075, a point where humanity is on the brink of destruction, the planet wracked by brutal storms, its resources all but exhausted, and humanity living in protective bubbles.

Aided by schoolgirl Iris (Margot Ringard Oldra, Romy Fay) and her robot caretaker Mikki, Arco desperately tries to repair his damaged time-suit to return home. However, between environmental devastation, a strange trio hunting him, and his very presence risking the timeline, Arco’s time might be up. Beautiful but somber, Arco is one of the best yet sadly most overlooked films of the year, animated or otherwise.

What good is a kamikaze pilot who survives? That’s the question haunting Kōichi Shikishima (Ryunosuke Kamiki) at the end of WWII, outlasting both the conflict and an encounter with an impossible, dinosaur-like creature on an isolated island. Returning to a devastated Tokyo, the directionless, traumatized Kōichi forms a relationship with fellow survivor Noriko (Minami Hamabe), raising an orphaned baby together. Yet their ad hoc family life is put in the atomic-breath firing line of that strange monster he encountered years earlier, now mutated to colossal size by exposure to atomic tests and stomping angrily onto land. Without weapons, military, or even much of a functioning society, can a still-broken Japan possibly withstand the horror of Godzilla?

While Ichiro Honda’s original 1954 Godzilla was a towering reptilian metaphor for the unknown risks of unleashing nuclear weapons on the world, Godzilla Minus One sees director Takashi Yamazaki leap back almost a decade to explore themes of survivor’s guilt, national despair, and rebuilding community. It’s a kaiju movie with brains, but also a treat for the eyes; it won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects. With sequel Godzilla Minus Zero in cinemas now, it’s well worth catching up on before seeing Big G throwing down with arch-nemesis King Ghidorah.

When struggling furniture seller Clark (Chiwetel Ejiofor) finds a crack in reality in his store’s basement, he becomes obsessed with exploring the seemingly infinite liminal space he’s slipped into. The space goes on forever, yet appears to be entirely empty—apart from an unseen presence stalking him. Becoming obsessed with mapping the unmappable, Clark’s already-fraying life disintegrates further, until his fixation ends up dragging his concerned therapist Mary (Renate Reinsve) into the Backrooms herself—except she doesn’t find it quite so uninhabited. And all the while, someone outside is watching closely.

From internet folklore to cinematic sensation, director Kane Parsons’ Backrooms was the surprise horror hit of the summer. It’s easy to see why—the endless sprawl of impossible corridors and uncanny rooms that connect in ways that seem to defy physics and geometry alike is darkly mesmerizing for viewers, while the unexplained nature of this twisted reality keeps you guessing at every turn. A sequel is in the works, but you can get lost in the Backrooms right now.

When up-and-coming director Kris (Hannah Einbinder) is hired to reboot the floundering Camp Miasma horror franchise, she wants to bring back Billy Presley (Gillian Anderson), the series’ original “Final Girl.” Unsettlingly, Kris finds Billy now living on the abandoned campsite from the dated first flick, living a reclusive life and obsessed with Little Death, the iconic killer from the series. Pitch meetings soon turn into the pair bonding over their fascination with, and experience of, the series—before descending into … well, the hint is in the film’s title.

A festival darling, picking up awards at both Cannes Film Festival and San Sebastián International Film Festival, this smart, transgressive horror outing from director Jane Schoenbrun (We're All Going to the World's Fair, I Saw the TV Glow) cleverly deconstructs the slasher genre and reexamines it from a queer—and extremely meta—perspective.

Somehow, despite a near-century of Looney Tunes cartoons (counting from 1930’s “Sinkin’ in the Bathtub”), The Day the Earth Blew Up is the series’ very first feature-length, fully animated movie (no, Space Jam doesn’t count). It’s been worth the wait, though—this loving pastiche of sci-fi B-movies is a stellar showcase for its top-billed stars, Daffy Duck and Porky Pig (both phenomenally voiced by the sadly departed Eric Bauza).

With an asteroid hurtling toward Earth, UFOs flying around, a sinister scientist meddling with unknown substances, and a zombie plague spread by infected bubble gum, Earth’s last hope is a deranged duck and an anxious pig. We’re probably doomed, but at least we’ll die laughing—this hilarious outing is a reminder of everything great about these beloved characters.

When Yu Kobayashi (Yuzu Aoki) transfers to a new school in rural Japan, he finds the strict culture and rigid conformity of students and staff alike unusual. Classes stand and bow to teachers in unnaturally uniform movements, PE lessons are precise marching practices, and even the hierarchy of bullies and victims seems oddly organized. Initially, Yu writes it off as culture clash in a new environment, until students inexplicably start to form ever-larger human pyramids, with teachers brutally encouraging them. Only Yu and outsider Ai Sato (Anna Yamada) seem able to resist the compulsion to join the pyramid—but with the increasingly maddening adherence to peer pressure spreading across Japan, they might be the only ones.

Director and writer Yûta Shimotsu expands the almost surreal setup into an exploration of conformity and expectations, infusing it all with an uncomfortable dose of J-horror. It’s exceedingly weird in places and demands you make your own conclusions, but at a brisk 81 minutes, it’s a short, sharp, and frequently shocking slice of satire.

Every holiday deserves a related slasher flick—everything from April Fools’ to Christmas to, of course, Halloween. Thanksgiving had been the odd one out, until 2007’s Grindhouse (the anthology double feature coheaded by Robert Rodriguez and Quentin Tarantino), which featured a bunch of mock trailers, including one by Hostel director Eli Roth, centered on a killer Pilgrim. Fast forward to 2023, and Roth finally got the idea out of his head with this full-length version.

A year after a Black Friday riot ends up with a body count, Plymouth, Massachusetts, finds itself stalked by a masked serial killer soon dubbed “John Carver.” The victims: anyone connected with the retail massacre—but the twisted chain of connections between Carver and his kills keeps viewers on their toes throughout. Thanksgiving is unapologetically loyal to the genre, but manages to cram in some sledgehammer-subtle commentary on consumerism alongside the gloriously gratuitous schlock.

When Alma (Maren Eggert) meets Tom (Dan Stevens), he seems perfect. Intelligent, poetic, able to dance—a rare modern gentleman. That is, until he starts glitching, revealing himself as an advanced robot. Initially repulsed, Alma is drawn into a three-week trial of a repaired Tom, testing his capabilities as an assistant and his capacity for emotion. It’s a quietly disconcerting experiment to watch play out, with Alma drawing closer to Tom almost in spite of herself.

First released in 2021, when undetectable AIs and synthetic humanoids were still just about within the realm of fiction, this German sci-fi drama now feels ahead of its time. While I’m Your Man bears some similarities to Alex Garland’s Ex Machina, it’s not as pre-apocalyptic, with director Maria Schrader balancing quasi-romcom tropes with hard existential questions about the nature of intelligence, the quiet banality of AI and robotics infiltrating society, and how we as humans respond.
