Europe’s Solar Problem Is No Longer Solar

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Europe’s solar fleet is delivering. The rules, grids, and investment framework around it are struggling to keep up.

SolarPower Europe’s September mid-year assessment describes a familiar contradiction: solar is contributing more to energy security and affordability, yet weakening policy support, grid constraints, and insufficient flexibility threaten continued growth.

In its September newsletter, the association reported that solar became Europe’s largest electricity source in June and estimated that it avoided more than €30 billion in gas imports for power generation over six months of the Middle East conflict. At that point, it forecast a slight contraction in EU solar deployment in 2026.

Europe’s ambition to become an electrostate is stuck in a State of Confusion and will remain unconvincing unless its rules and networks catch up. The challenge is no longer simply building solar. It is connecting it, using it, and making participation easier.

Ukraine’s September auctions illustrate the shift.

Preliminary results showed that a 50 MW solar auction attracted three bids totalling 38.99 MW. A separate 100 MW solar-plus-storage auction attracted 26 bids totalling 385.9 MW.

The terms differed: price ceilings were €0.08/kWh for standalone solar and €0.12/kWh for solar-plus-storage. These were bids, not completed projects. The results demonstrate stronger participation in the hybrid auction—not proof that adding batteries is inherently more profitable.

Physical projects show the transition too. A September report described Romania’s 918 MWh battery under construction and a separate 120 MWh system commissioned at Teiuș. Bulgaria’s Devnya solar plant expanded from 5 MWp to 65 MWp, adding 60 MW/165 MWh of storage.

But batteries are only part of the equation. Schneider Electric Research Institute’s October system-cost analysis models 40,500 scenarios combining distributed solar and batteries with central generation and networks.

Under global-average cost assumptions, optimised scenarios put total electricity-system costs about 10% below the benchmark, rising to as much as 40% below under assumed 2030s technology costs. These are modelled outcomes, not forecasts of European household bills.

The lesson from this company-sponsored research is coordination: electrifying transport, heating, and industry can improve infrastructure utilisation alongside distributed generation.

Denmark is confronting another bottleneck: access to scarce connection capacity.

Denmark’s October connection-prioritisation reform replaces a simple first-come, first-served approach with categories reflecting societal functions, electrification, and other uses. Within categories, projects placing less strain on the grid can receive priority.

Battery treatment matters. Standalone storage falls in category 3, below renewable generation and electrification in category 2. Qualifying co-located batteries can also fall in category 2.

Storage can increase connection requirements or improve an existing connection’s utilisation. Rules should recognise operation, not merely technology labels.

Europe needs transparent queues, credible project-readiness tests and connection arrangements that reward flexibility—not just faster permitting.

Roofs, station canopies, car parks, and noise barriers already occupy space for another purpose. Adding PV can reduce additional land take, although it does not make generation footprint-free.

Belgium’s Solar Tunnel demonstrates the principle. A 2011 announcement records 16,000 modules installed on a covered section of the Paris–Amsterdam railway near Antwerp, designed to generate 3.3 GWh annually for railway infrastructure and trains.

At Uden in the Netherlands, bifacial PV forms part of an A50 motorway noise barrier. The EU LIFE project’s monitored results recorded 325.5 MWh over 18 months—approximately 220 MWh annually.

The assessment also reports lower-than-expected generation and higher-than-anticipated capital costs. Multifunctional infrastructure is promising, not automatically cheaper. Engineering, procurement, and maintenance determine whether combined benefits justify the premium.

The strongest projects preserve their original function while adding electricity: shade plus charging, noise protection plus generation, or water infrastructure plus power. They complement conventional rooftop and ground-mounted solar rather than replace them.

At the other end of the scale, plug-in solar challenges the assumption that participation requires an owned roof and a substantial installation budget.

Germany’s Federal Network Agency provides a simplified administrative route for qualifying systems, with module capacity up to 2,000 Wp and inverter output up to 800 VA.

Registration remains mandatory. Separate network-operator notification is waived under specified conditions, including the unpaid-export arrangement. These administrative thresholds do not override technical or installation-safety requirements.

Germany’s environment agency explains that qualifying devices within technical limits can be commissioned by non-specialists.

That matters because administrative and installation costs can be disproportionate for small systems. Balcony solar offers a route for people without access to a conventional rooftop installation, including some apartment residents.

It does not replace large-scale generation or remove mounting, electrical-safety, building-consent, and country-specific requirements. Its importance is accessibility: proportionate regulation makes small-scale participation possible.

Europe needs large projects, distributed generation, solar-equipped infrastructure, accessible household systems, batteries, and flexible demand.

Writing from Down Under, the parallel is clear. High rooftop-solar penetration is reshaping Australian daytime demand and electricity prices. The response is batteries, flexible demand, and market rules that reward them.

The choice is not between ambition and practicality. Practical rules are how ambition becomes deployment.
