How Le Sserafim Unlocked a New Kind of Fearlessness

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

B efore Kim Chaewon could be fearless, she had to convince herself that she was.

When Le Sserafim debuted in 2022, the group’s name was a declaration, an anagram of “I’m Fearless.” Their debut single of the same name was equally assured, a cool, minimalist statement of ambition from a group of young women determined to make their mark on K-pop. Chaewon, the group’s leader, and Miyawaki Sakura were already seasoned idols, having performed together in the project group IZ*ONE. Huh Yunjin, a Korean American singer-songwriter raised in New York, brought her own artistic aspirations. Nakamura Kazuha had spent years training as a classical ballerina before making the leap into K-pop, while Hong Eunchae, the youngest, was just 15 when she debuted. Together, they were building an identity around a word that seemed to leave little room for uncertainty.

But confidence isn’t easy to maintain, especially in an industry where every note, dance move, and perceived misstep can become fodder for public debate. For Chaewon, fearlessness was something she had to practice. Coming off IZ*ONE’s rapid success, she remembers being genuinely afraid in Le Sserafim’s early days, quietly encouraging herself to be brave, as though courage were something she could summon through sheer repetition.

“Personally, I think I was actually quite scared in our ‘fearless’ years,” Chaewon recalls, reflecting on those early days as she sits in the Rolling Stone studio in New York. “So I used to repeat ‘fearless, fearless’ to myself, like I was casting a spell.”

Chaewon can laugh about it now. The 26-year-old singer has spent much of her adult life onstage. She’s performed for tens of thousands of fans, traveled across continents, and weathered the kind of scrutiny that comes with growing up in the public eye. These days, she has a different relationship with fear. “That feeling of fearlessness used to be about guts and courage, but after all this time, my fearlessness comes from accepting some fear,” she explains. “When I’m a little scared, I tell myself something like, ‘Yeah, I’m scared. So what?'” There’s a certain freedom in that, in being able to set down some of the expectations she once carried and keep going. “By accepting that, I feel stronger,” she adds. Sakura, at 28, has been an idol for more than half her life. She debuted in Japan, where she grew up, at just 13, long before Le Sserafim, and she has spent much of her adolescence and adulthood navigating the expectations that come with public life. Earlier in her career, she struggled to acknowledge her own fears. “I used to feel the most scared when I couldn’t accept the imperfections within myself,” she admits. But age and experience have changed the way she sees herself. “I’ve learned to accept that I still have room to grow, and that’s allowed me to love myself more,” she says. That acceptance has given her a greater sense of freedom, making it easier to embrace new challenges head-on.

It also helps that Sakura, or Saki, as she’s affectionately known, has weathered the storm of public opinion long enough to develop a healthy dose of perspective. She’s learned that not every opinion about her deserves her attention.

For Yunjin, fear has become something worth examining rather than escaping. “I feel like fear is such a multidimensional emotion,” the 25-year-old singer says thoughtfully. “You’re scared of something because you love it so much, or you’re scared of something because you want to hold on to it that much, and it’s that meaningful to you.” Over the years, she’s come to recognize that being afraid doesn’t necessarily mean you’re doing something wrong. Sometimes, it means you’re doing something that matters. “I’ve really learned over the few years that we’ve been together that true strength and true empowerment is to do things scared and do it anyway, even when you’re in the midst of fear,” she says. That mentality is beginning to shape the group’s music, too. If the Le Sserafim of 2022 were determined to prove they had nothing to fear, the Le Sserafim of today seem more interested in having a little fun. There’s a mischievous streak running through their recent releases, from the deliciously absurd “SPAGHETTI,” featuring BTS’s exuberant rapper J-Hope, which likens their inescapable popularity to food stuck between someone’s teeth, to the carefree house of “Made My Night” and the gleefully defiant “Iconic by Mistake,” a collaboration with HYBE labelmates ILLIT and Katseye that turns the familiar sting of criticism into something worth celebrating. It’s pop music with a knowing wink, made by artists who have spent enough time in the spotlight to understand that not everyone is going to like them.

“I think it’s come to a point where we really just don’t care,” Yunjin says. She’s quick to clarify what that means. It’s not that they’ve stopped caring about their work — quite the opposite. “We’re more concerned about whether or not we resonate with it, or whether or not we really enjoy it and immerse ourselves in it,” she explains. “Whatever you want to say, you can say it. Because the internet isn’t real, and what we feel and what we resonate with is real.”

In the studio, that means spending less time worrying about how a song might be received and more time asking whether it’s something they actually want to make. You can hear it on Le Sserafim’s second studio album, PUREFLOW pt.1, released in May. Across 11 tracks, including the buoyant “Boompala,” which samples Los Del Río’s 1993 hit “Macarena” (yes, as in the Macarena), and the more introspective “Need Your Company,” the group explores what it means to embrace uncertainty instead of pretending it doesn’t exist. Even the album’s concept turns their original declaration of fearlessness on its head. As Yunjin explains, they once believed “I’m fearless, and therefore I am powerful,” but now they’ve come to embrace something more complicated: “‘I am not fearless, and therefore I am powerful.”

“It’s not being perfect and being invincible all the time,” Yunjin says. “It’s choosing to laugh still and have fun and enjoy what we do.”

For Sakura, that has meant learning to enjoy the work itself. She admits that she once became so consumed with how a project would turn out that she struggled to appreciate the process of creating it. “I used to be the kind of person who prioritizes results, so as we prepared for our comeback, I was very stressed about how it might turn out,” she recalls. “But nowadays, I feel that the process is more important than the result. What’s most important to me is how much of myself I put into the work, and whether I loved the process, so I’m able to have more fun in our work than I did before.” She’s found that letting go of those expectations has made room for the simple pleasure of making music.

The results have been hard to ignore. PUREFLOW pt.1 became the group’s fifth Top 10 album on the Billboard 200, while “SPAGHETTI” climbed to Number 50 on the Hot 100, their highest position on the chart to date. But for Yunjin, those milestones are less important than the work that goes into making music they believe in. “How much are we truly putting ourselves into the work and making the effort and getting in the rooms and giving opinions and talking amongst ourselves to do exactly what we truly want to do?” she says. She believes the real results will come with time. “It’s not something that you can directly chase. It’s something that will build and eventually come to you when you stick to what really matters to you.”

Their growing confidence comes at a pivotal moment. Having recently wrapped the North American leg of their second world tour, Le Sserafim are embarking on their first headline concerts in Europe. The members have spent years refining their performances, but lately, they’ve been discovering the pleasure of letting go a little. For 19-year-old Eunchae, that feeling has been especially pronounced. “Last year, I felt a lot of pressure to do well and to show everyone our best selves,” she recalls. This time, she was less concerned with getting everything right and more interested in connecting with the audience. “I just felt so happy sharing myself onstage, making eye contact with the fans and dancing together,” she says.

Fan-captured clips of Eunchae looking like she’s having the time of her life onstage have circulated widely online, radiating the kind of joy that’s impossible to choreograph. Even on the set of this Rolling Stone photo shoot, she can’t seem to resist dancing along to Katseye’s “Gnarly” between shots, seemingly just as happy to move when nobody’s asking her to.

And Eunchae feels it, too: “I watch a lot of those fancams as well, and I definitely look happy.”

The group’s growing global audience has made those moments of connection even more meaningful. Chaewon still marvels at the sight of fans singing along to entire songs, even when they don’t speak Korean. “I felt so thankful watching that,” she says. “More than anything, seeing how happy they were watching us perform made me feel like we actually got even more energy from them.” For Eunchae, the upcoming European concerts offer another chance to experience that connection firsthand. She’s especially excited to meet fans she’s only been able to interact with online.

Inside the rehearsal rooms and recording rooms of Seoul, global fame can feel surprisingly abstract. What does it even mean when an Instagram Reel of Chaewon covering Ariana Grande racks up 8 million views? Yunjin admits that she sometimes struggles to comprehend Le Sserafim’s reach. “It feels so intangible almost because it’s just such a big scale that it’s hard to comprehend with the human mind just how many people know us and perceive us,” she says. “But on tour, when we’re able to see these people face-to-face, see all the love and the energy and the support that they put forth, we are really able to sit and realize, like, ‘Wow, people know us and they understand and resonate with what we’re saying.'”

As Le Sserafim’s audience grows, so does the members’ awareness of what their music means to the people listening. Yunjin has noticed more young girls in the crowd lately, and she lights up when she talks about it. There’s something especially gratifying about seeing them embrace songs that encourage listeners to take up space, trust themselves, and refuse to be defined by other people’s opinions. “It brings me so much joy,” she says. Knowing those messages might help someone feel a little more confident makes her want to keep creating music that reflects those feelings.

Of course, learning not to care what people think is easier said than done. Kazuha knows that better than most. Before joining Le Sserafim, the 23-year-old spent years dancing ballet, a discipline built on precision and control. Becoming a K-pop idol meant adjusting to an entirely different kind of public attention, one where the audience’s opinions could reach her long after she’d left the stage. Early on, she struggled to make sense of the criticism. “At first, that confused me a bit, and sometimes I got my feelings hurt,” she admits. She couldn’t understand why strangers felt so comfortable judging someone they didn’t know.

Over time, Kazuha has learned to place less weight on those opinions. What matters more now, she explains, is how she sees herself. That doesn’t mean criticism has suddenly become painless, or that she’s stopped wanting to improve. Instead, she’s beginning to recognize the difference between listening to feedback and letting other people’s perceptions determine her sense of worth. “If I think I’ve made a mistake, or fell short, that’s probably correct, and I can try harder next time,” she says. “And if I don’t agree with how someone sees me or with what they have to say, I think it’s OK to trust how I feel about it.” It’s an ongoing process, one she admits she hasn’t entirely mastered. “It’s really hard, and I don’t always manage to do it, but I try to pay more attention to how I feel.”

Trusting each other has come with its own learning curve. The members arrived at Le Sserafim with different backgrounds, personalities, and ways of expressing themselves. The track “Need Your Company,” co-written and composed by Yunjin, explores the loneliness of feeling misunderstood, even by the people closest to you. Yunjin sees the song as a reflection of how their relationships have evolved. Early on, she worried that their differences might make it difficult to connect. She felt pressure to adapt to the others, and sometimes expected them to do the same.

“Two truths can exist at once,” she says. Over time, she’s learned that being close doesn’t mean agreeing on everything, or even wanting the same things at the same time. Take Yunjin’s sleeping habits. A self-described night owl who has been known to stay awake until 6 a.m., she jokes that she’s had to accept that the other members might not appreciate being summoned for a conversation in the middle of the night. “You can’t ask your members to get together at 2 a.m. because that’s not fair,” she says. “That would only be OK to me.”

More than anything, Yunjin has learned to approach their differences with curiosity. “All intimate relationships, whether it’s platonic friendship or romantic love, start with a curiosity about one another,” she says. “It starts with that feeling of wanting to know somebody, and I think that’s what has brought us close together.”

Eunchae has experienced that acceptance in a particularly personal way. She was a young teen when she debuted, still figuring out who she was and how to express herself. “In the past, I wasn’t very aware of my own feelings and didn’t quite know what I wanted to do,” she recalls. But growing up alongside the other members, whom she’s long considered family, has helped her understand herself better. “Now, as time has passed and I’ve experienced lots of things, I’ve learned more about myself and what I like.”

That self-discovery has come with the reassurance that she doesn’t have to figure everything out alone. “No matter what choices I make and what I say, I know that my members will trust me and listen to what I have to say,” she explains. Their support has given her the confidence to stay true to herself, even when things get difficult, and to express herself more freely both onstage and off. Watching her members interact with fans, she feels a sense of solidarity that goes beyond simply performing together. They’re five very different people, but those differences have become one of their greatest strengths.

Eunchae’s words visibly touch Yunjin. It’s the kind of relationship she hopes they’ll always have, one where each member feels free to be herself, knowing the others will be cheering her on.

That sisterhood now extends beyond Le Sserafim. When they joined forces with ILLIT and Katseye for “Iconic by Mistake,” they brought together three girl groups at different stages of their careers for a song about embracing the online hate. Much of the preparation happened separately, with all three groups coming together for just one rehearsal in Seoul before promoting the song on Korean music shows.

For Kazuha, the collaboration was initially a little overwhelming. So many people were involved, and they had little time to get used to performing alongside one another. But once they shared the stage, she found herself appreciating what made each group distinct. “When we were on stage together, I felt each group’s style was so distinct, and by working together, each group’s individual strengths were able to shine through even more,” she says. The experience left her feeling inspired by her fellow performers. “I felt so encouraged by collaborating with these hardworking artists, and felt like we should work even harder. They were a great influence.”

Le Sserafim were also the most senior group in the collaboration, a fact that still catches Yunjin off guard. “That’s crazy!” she exclaims. “Time is a crazy thing.”

And Eunchae, who’s spent years as the team’s youngest, found herself in the strange role of older sister. “I felt like I had new younger sisters,” she says. “It felt amazing.”

Yunjin has her own history with the other groups. She trained alongside some ILLIT members before debut, while Le Sserafim met Katseye during The Debut: Dream Academy. Performing together brought those relationships full circle. “To see her again was such a crazy thing,” Yunjin says of reuniting with a former trainee. “We [all] have history together. I think that’s what made it more meaningful.”

The song’s message was familiar territory for Le Sserafim. Like “SPAGHETTI,” “Iconic by Mistake” takes the criticism directed at female pop stars and turns it into something playful and defiant. “I don’t care what you say about me because my name is still in your mouth either way,” Yunjin says, describing the attitude behind the two releases. “Thank you for talking about me. Thank you for thinking about me.” She calls it a “bold” but “self-validating” message, one each group expressed in its own way.

Speaking openly about their experiences has always been central to Le Sserafim’s identity. “Our biggest pillar as a team was to speak about things that we feel, regardless of what the world will say, and regardless of what fingers will be pointed at us,” Yunjin says. Now, the difference is that the members have a clearer understanding of what they want to say and how they want to say it.

And they still have plenty more to say. Touring has given Yunjin new material to draw from as a songwriter. Asked whether she’s felt creatively invigorated by the experience, she answers immediately: “Oh, my gosh, 100 percent.” Meeting people, visiting unfamiliar cities, and experiencing the world beyond the rehearsal rooms and recording studios in Seoul — it’s all left her eager to create. “There’s just so many cities that we go to that we otherwise wouldn’t be able to go to,” she says. “So many people that we get to meet. So many places we get to go to.” And yes, she’s been writing, too. “I have, actually,” she confirms. “Maybe not full-on songs, but, like, there are ideas aboil.”

As Le Sserafim look toward their next chapter, the members are also thinking about what they’ll leave behind. For Eunchae, the group’s legacy has less to do with commercial milestones than with the feelings their music inspires. She hopes people get to know Le Sserafim as artists who are honest about their emotions, who love their fans, and who encourage others to find courage in their own lives. “As we’re touring right now and meeting lots of fans, I can feel that the stories we were trying to tell have resonated with so many people worldwide,” she says. “I hope to keep on doing that.”

Chaewon hopes their music reminds people that failure doesn’t have to be final. “Even if you’ve failed, even if you made a mistake, you can get back up and try again,” she says. “I think we’ve shown the courage to do that, and hope that we can embody that courage for others.”

Success, however, is something Chaewon is still learning to define for herself. “There is actually no objective measure of success,” she says. “And you can’t gauge it by numbers alone.” Rather than measuring her achievements against anyone else’s expectations, she’s become more interested in understanding what fulfillment looks like on her own terms. “I’ve been working to know myself more, by thinking more about what winning means to me and what success means to me,” she explains. “I really hope people don’t just determine success based on other people’s parameters.”

Sakura has lived enough versions of her career to know that success isn’t always linear. For her, there’s something gratifying about finding herself in places she once couldn’t have imagined reaching. “For me, success and happiness don’t necessarily go hand in hand, so success isn’t something I feel like I have to chase,” she says. “When this work takes me beyond what I thought was possible, to places I never imagined I’d be, that’s when I feel like I’ve succeeded.”

Four years after their debut, Le Sserafim have learned that being fearless doesn’t mean never being afraid. Setbacks will always happen, and expectations will always be out of their control. But there’s freedom in knowing the pendulum keeps swinging anyway.

And right now, Yunjin believes they’re on their way back up. “We are ready to raise our voices again.”
