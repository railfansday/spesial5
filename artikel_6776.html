Jesse Jo Stark on What It Means to Be a Muse

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

The singer’s new gothic country album, MUSE, releases on November 6

Jesse Jo Stark wrote her latest album MUSE in the wake of heartache. She left Los Angeles to find reprieve in the Colorado Rocky Mountains, where she found sanctuary among friends, a bottle of rosé, and a pink halter top and sweatpants combo. The melancholic album flowed out of her for three weeks, during which she transferred her emotions onto the page. “I am no longer holding on to a version of myself,” she tells Bazaar on a call from Los Angeles, on her way to a photoshoot. “There’s a freedom in moving forward.”

Stark keeps pressing on, no matter what. Outside of songwriting, she plays a key role in Chrome Hearts as the daughter of founder Richard Stark. She spent her formative years surrounded by musicians, artists, designers, and every kind of creative person imaginable, all of whom inspired her to pursue her own vision. That world endlessly inspires her to create, even when she feels low. “I’ve worn things that are absolutely horrible, and I’ve written songs that I don’t necessarily love,” she says, “but when you grow, you go back to a less constructed version of yourself.”

This more authentic version of herself fuels MUSE. She shed some of the macabre edge of her debut album 2022’s Doomed in favor of a softer reflection on her life, love, femininity, and the natural world that inspired these songs. But above all, Stark offers a reflection on her life being put on a pedestal, a narrative that deeply considers the women in her life, from friends to her mother, Laurie Lynn Stark. This 13-track gothic country album appropriately conveys Stark’s energy, with visuals described as “somewhat of a horror film, but I’m wearing a lace skirt.” She wrote on her Instagram, announcing the album, that the theme is “fragile but fierce femininity,” best to “enjoy with a glass of red.”

A few weeks before the release, Bazaar spoke to Stark about the women who inspired her, Napoleon’s poems to Josephine, writing songs in Colorado, and what it means to be a muse.

I was looking at a lot of mythology and history and the muses of rock and roll—women of rock and roll. If you break down the word itself, you get “used,” “amused,” “music.” I think women are admired and desired, but not always heard. For me, women have this pivotal point in their life—hopefully, at one point—where they regain their strength and voice, and this album was that for me. The muse, throughout history, could also have a negative connotation and feel lonely. But I felt like putting emphasis on the reciprocity of it—then owning that and allowing it to feel beautiful. It’s a powerful word. It felt fitting for this time in my life.

There’s so much serious crossover. Being able to develop the way I feel my best on stage and design what I wear enables me to embody that version of myself, to gather the strength to perform. Women like Daphne Guinness, Isabella Blow and Jane Birkin have really inspired me. I’ve always wanted to not just completely mirror something from the past, but recreate it as my own. I wear a loincloth on stage—it’s not that it hasn’t been done before, but the way I wear it or the way it’s designed, the accessories and tops that are paired with it... I do feel confident in me extending this already living thing, but making it my own.

When I was little, I always had an attitude about the two not going hand in hand. I think that was incorrect. They’re pretty much the same expression: one’s a vocal cord and playing guitar, and the other’s a boot that you walk in.

It’s weird. My last album was highly visual. There was a lot of fantasy involved, and this is very stripped back; [both] vocal production-wise and stylistically. I just did my first acoustic show, and I was wearing a vintage silk dress with a heel, and my hair was straight, and I had minimal makeup. Because there was such a rawness to it, especially where I recorded, which was amongst nature and very secluded. With this record, anytime I tried to put emphasis on creating a specific vibe, I rejected that.

The space and the quietness and the snow—I’ve never been one to be completely influenced by nature. I don’t love green or grass, but I love to sit in it and lie in it. I love being with animals and the snow and the sun. All these natural tones, I’m embracing the color brown like I haven’t before. It sounds so silly, but they represent a softness that I didn’t have for a while… The visuals have become somewhat of a horror film, but I’m wearing a lace skirt.

My main single from the record, “age of being honest,” has been visually stimulating and powerful. I can feel every emotion through that visual. It’s rawness—in the vocals and everything. “colorado” is the place that I literally went to and didn’t really know what was going to come from it. That set the tone for the rest of the record.

The shedding was happening as I was driving. The heartache was there, so it wasn’t difficult once I sat down to break through that human brick wall. I wanted to feel everything right away. I gotta be real, it was a white boa, a bottle of rosé, people I fuck with, the sun, and being in a really beautiful pink halter top and sweatpants half the time. I don’t think there was much going on except pain and not knowing where it was going and just sitting on the floor, living in a house with people for two weeks or a month. You can’t escape what you’re doing; I really leaned into that.

My dad had a lot to do with my love of music. I asked to play guitar at a young age. I was around a lot of rock and roll and glam—these really amazing artists who were way older than me. This desire to—and I was really shy—create something as beautiful as they had or stand as my parents could and make my own way. Music was always mine, so there was an independence to it. Finding yourself in writing, you don’t think you can write, and then you start writing poems. I loved Britney Spears. I loved the Spice Girls. But then my dad would play Merle Haggard, and he also let me listen to Christina Aguilera. I found punk rock when I was 13 because I fell in love with a punk boy. Then you somehow are, you’re mad at everything, and you found your voice in that way. It just started to become this whole thing, and then I started singing covers because I wasn’t ready to write. And then when you write, you really write your first song—to create something out of nothing is really funny because the fact that we think we get to do that is funny to me, but also so freeing and amazing. You never want to stop.
