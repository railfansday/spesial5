Monkeys that lived through a deadly outbreak showed surprising caution around food

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Japanese macaques from groups hit by a deadly outbreak were more likely to avoid food on feces, even if they had never tested positive.

In the first weeks of COVID-19, many households wiped down their groceries before putting them away.

Japanese macaques that lived through a deadly outbreak of their own also showed greater caution around food.

Macaques from groups where the virus had spread refused food placed on a soiled surface far more often than those from a group it never reached.

And the virus they had survived isn’t even known to spread through food.

Stephan Kaufhold and Federico Rossano of the University of California San Diego‘s Department of Cognitive Science ran the tests at a primate sanctuary in southern Texas.

The researchers tested whether monkeys would eat a grape placed on feces.

The experiment measured food avoidance, which does not necessarily indicate disgust.

The sanctuary’s Japanese macaques descend from a troop moved from Japan to Texas in 1972. Today they live in four separate fenced enclosures.

In January 2020, the sanctuary confirmed that a virus called simian retrovirus Type II was circulating in the Japanese macaques.

A long-tailed macaque that had retired from a research laboratory had carried it in without showing symptoms.

Late in 2019, the virus had likely changed enough to spread to the Japanese macaques.

Some infected monkeys died quickly of severe bleeding.

Others carried it with no symptoms, and some later lost weight and fell ill with other infections, much as people with AIDS do.

The virus passes through blood and saliva, mostly in bites and scratches during fights, and it has never been shown to spread through feces or food.

Three of the four groups went through the outbreak. The fourth, called Kyoto, didn’t.

Between October 2022 and March 2024, the team set two baited objects on the ground inside each enclosure and filmed what happened.

One was a plain piece of wood. The other was real macaque feces, collected from healthy animals and sealed under clear epoxy so it looked fresh but had no smell and couldn’t infect anyone.

A grape was placed on each object, and every monkey was free to take both.

Monkeys from the three outbreak groups ate the grape off the feces in 47% of 117 trials. Monkeys from Kyoto ate it in 79% of 80 trials.

So a monkey that had lived through the outbreak usually took the clean grape and left the other one lying there. A monkey from Kyoto usually ate both.

Within the outbreak groups, some monkeys had tested positive for the virus and some never had.

Kaufhold’s team compared the two, and the uninfected monkeys avoided the dirty grape about as much as the infected ones did. Both avoided it far more than the Kyoto monkeys.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kaufhold said that was the result he hadn’t expected.

“What surprised us was that animals within the exposed groups that had not been infected avoided the contaminated food just as much as those that had,” he said.

Kaufhold said the team doesn’t know why some monkeys escaped infection. Their immune systems may have resisted it, or they may have stayed out of the fights that spread it.

“Either way, the shift in behavior seems to follow having lived through the outbreak, for example witnessing others getting sick and dying, rather than being infected oneself,” he told Earth.com.

Only nine monkeys in the outbreak groups are confirmed uninfected, and the team treats that comparison as preliminary.

The outbreak groups showed greater caution around feces, even though the virus is not known to spread that way.

Kaufhold said the pattern “suggests a general increase in pathogen sensitivity rather than avoidance of the specific transmission route.”

In another experiment in the same paper, the team tested macaques from seven species, one at a time, with a sliding table that held the same two objects.

Among those tested, 14 were born at the sanctuary and raised by their mothers, while 15 were former pets raised by people.

The former pets had about twice the odds of taking food off the feces that the sanctuary-born monkeys did, even after years of living in monkey groups.

Kaufhold told Earth.com that one possibility is that the former pets “missed the chance to learn these behaviors from their mothers and other macaques early in life.”

Earlier studies found that young macaques watch their mothers and older groupmates forage, and that what they see others do shapes what they eat.

There is a cost, Kaufhold said. “Pathogen avoidance is always a trade-off.”

“Being too sensitive means leaving resources like food on the table, while being too indiscriminate increases exposure to pathogens and potentially disease.”

The outbreak monkeys were paying that cost in grapes.

The researchers can’t rule out that the four groups already differed before the outbreak, because nobody tested them then.

They also can’t yet say whether each monkey learned the caution on its own or picked it up from the group.

One precedent they cite is a troop of wild baboons in Kenya. A tuberculosis outbreak in the 1980s killed the troop’s most aggressive males, and the calmer culture that followed was passed on to males who joined years later.

Kaufhold said two questions come next: how long the heightened caution lasts, and whether monkeys that join these groups later pick it up.

“If newcomers picked up the increased caution, that would suggest social transmission, which could lead to a group-specific tradition,” he said.

Kaufhold said the macaque data “does not directly speak to how human behavior changed during and after COVID-19, but there are some interesting parallels.”

Studies of people found that disgust sensitivity rose during the pandemic, including toward things that had nothing to do with how the coronavirus spread.

The authors argue that human disgust was built on an older system for avoiding disease, one that changes with experience.

The team hasn’t yet measured whether that caution will still be there in the Texas enclosures a few years from now. They hope to go back and find out.

The full study was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.
