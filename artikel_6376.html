Why does sleep get worse with age? A common vitamin deficiency may offer a clue

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

A study of 460 adults found that people with vitamin D deficiency slept 23 minutes less each night than those with normal levels.

Sleep often becomes shorter and more fragmented as people age. A new study has examined whether the level of vitamin D in the blood is related to that change.

Among middle-aged and older adults, higher vitamin D concentrations were associated with longer sleep at night. Episodes of waking during the night were also found to be shorter.

The largest difference was observed in participants whose vitamin D was low enough to meet the definition of deficiency. These individuals slept about 23 minutes less per night than participants with normal levels.

The analysis was led by Diefei Chen, a PhD candidate in the Department of Epidemiology. This department forms part of the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

Researchers from the National Institute on Aging were also involved in the work. Because the design was observational, an association could be identified but a causal effect could not be established.

Most vitamin D in the human body is synthesized in the skin following exposure to sunlight. Additional amounts are supplied through diet and through supplements.

Receptors for the vitamin are present in the brain. They are found in high numbers within a small region that regulates the body’s daily rhythm of sleep and waking.

Deficiency is also common among older adults in the United States. According to the estimates cited by the authors, roughly half of this population has low levels.

Disturbed sleep is reported by more than half of older Americans. Such sleep problems have been associated with greater risks of illness and death.

In a 2015 study of 3,048 older men, low vitamin D was linked to shorter and less efficient sleep. Whether the same relationship would hold in women had not been determined.

Participants were drawn from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging, an ongoing project that follows healthy adults as they age.

Data collected between 2012 and 2019 were used. The final sample consisted of 460 adults aged 40 years or older with a mean age of about 71.

Women made up slightly more than half of the sample. Approximately one participant in five was Black.

Inclusion was limited to people whose memory and thinking were normal. Individuals with dementia or milder forms of cognitive impairment were excluded at the outset.

Such conditions can disturb sleep in their own right, and poor nutrition linked to them may lower vitamin levels.

A fasting blood sample was obtained from each participant and analyzed for vitamin D and vitamin B12.

Each participant was also asked to wear a small motion sensor on the non-dominant wrist for seven full days.

Over the same week, bedtimes, rising times and any daytime naps were logged by the participants in a sleep diary.

Sleep averaged approximately 6 hours and 40 minutes per night, with about 48 minutes awake after sleep onset.

For the main comparison, participants were sorted by vitamin D concentration into three groups of similar size.

Age, sex, race, education, body weight, depressive symptoms and other medical conditions were taken into account in the models.

Following these adjustments, the highest group was found to sleep about 19 minutes longer per night than the lowest group.

A slightly greater proportion of time in bed was spent asleep by this group. Their waking episodes during the night were also somewhat shorter.

Although 19 minutes may seem modest for one night, the difference amounts to more than two hours weekly.

The researchers then grouped participants using clinical thresholds. In this analysis, concentrations below 20 nanograms per milliliter were classified as deficient and those above 30 as normal.

Using these categories, deficient participants slept approximately 23 minutes less per night than those within the normal range.

Participants with concentrations between the two thresholds did not differ clearly from the normal group.

Vitamin D supplement use was reported by nearly half of the volunteers. Its inclusion in the analysis did not materially alter the results.

The association between vitamin D and sleep appeared stronger among Black participants than in the rest of the sample.

Black participants in the middle and highest vitamin D groups spent a greater proportion of their time in bed asleep. This proportion was about three percentage points higher than in the lowest group.

Among White participants and those of other races, the corresponding difference was smaller and may have arisen by chance.

This finding was described by the authors as exploratory. Confirmation in other populations will be required.

“Black adults in the United States have a higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and sleep disturbances compared to white adults, and may benefit more from increasing vitamin D levels,” Chen said.

Vitamin B12 was also assessed because of its role in the processes by which cells maintain DNA.

In the sample as a whole, no clear association was detected between B12 concentrations and any sleep measure.

A possible difference between the sexes was nevertheless noted. Women with higher B12 concentrations spent slightly less time awake during the night.

An opposite pattern was seen in men, although that estimate was too imprecise to be distinguished from chance.

Low B12 concentrations were identified in only 14 participants, so a modest effect may have gone undetected.

Most participants were assessed on a single occasion. Changes in sleep therefore could not be tracked as vitamin D concentrations rose or fell.

The direction of the association remains uncertain. Poor sleep could lead to lower vitamin D rather than the reverse.

Low vitamin D may also act as a general indicator of declining health. The sources of each participant’s vitamin D could not be distinguished.

The cohort was relatively healthy, and the results may not apply to less healthy populations.

Wrist sensors are also limited in what they record. The time spent in individual sleep stages cannot be measured with such devices.

Adam Spira, a professor in the Department of Mental Health at the Bloomberg School, served as senior author.

“These findings suggest that we should investigate more thoroughly whether boosting vitamin D levels can improve sleep health,” Spira said.

Earlier trials have reported that vitamin D supplements improved self-rated sleep. Those assessments were based on questionnaires rather than on sensor recordings.

The next question is whether raising vitamin D would lengthen sleep as recorded by a sensor. Future studies in older adults could test this directly.

The full study was published in The Journals of Gerontology: Series A.
