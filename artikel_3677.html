For The Love Of God, Stop Buying Canned Cranberry Sauce

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

We all know someone who insists on having this at Thanksgiving year after year.

What the hell even is this? Why are we tolerating it on our elaborately decorated and carefully curated Thanksgiving tables? It looks like a giant gummy bear took a dump on the table. It looks like it might start inching across the plate like a fat worm.

I was in a relationship for several years with someone who insisted on canned, jellied cranberry sauce at the table every year. He would slide it out of the can each year, still upsettingly can-shaped, slice it into neat and horrifying rounds, and put it on his plate. But then I’d take the dishes away and most of the cranberry jelly would still be there on his plate, which was otherwise clean of turkey and gravy and sundry. No one else would touch the canned stuff, either, so I’d end up with a weird leftover tub of goop that would sit in the fridge for weeks until someone finally threw it away.

Stop eating this garbage, folks. I’m here to save you. This year, you’re going to make and eat some real goddamn cranberry sauce, and it’s going to change your life. You’re going to do this whether you’re currently a can-cran lover, or a total cran-hater. Stick with me.

I was once like you. When I was a kid, the only food on the Thanksgiving table I wouldn’t touch was the cranberry sauce. It wasn’t for any particular reason. I don’t think I ever even tried it. I’d happily gobble up everything else: I’d munch at least a little on every vegetable on offer, green or otherwise, and even nibble at the plate of black olives my grandmother left out for hors d’oeuvres. But something about the cranberry sauce scared and repelled me. I really don’t know what it was, as I liked cranberry juice just fine at that age. I think it just seemed dissonant, you know? This weird, goopy red relish thing on a spread of savory things meant to be slathered in gravy? My ex’s love of the canned stuff only further cemented my repulsion.

It wasn’t until recently that I discovered the absolute joy and delight that is a good cranberry sauce. I have chef and author Samin Nosrat to thank for it. In her book Salt Fat Acid Heat, she opens the chapter on acid with a wonderful anecdote about her own experience of Thanksgiving as an immigrant. She described her first Thanksgiving meal, which she had in college, as “kind of a letdown” full of “soft, rich, bland foods” that “bored” her palate. It was only the cranberry sauce that made the dinner remotely edible thanks to its acidic components, and it wasn’t until she was older and eating Thanksgiving dinners with other chefs who incorporated acid into all of the meal components (sour cream in mashed potatoes, a splash of white wine in the gravy, etc.) that she understood the appeal of such a feast. I’m not here to tell you to start changing up all your other Thanksgiving recipes, though. For me, the understanding that cranberry was capable of brightening everything I already liked on the table was enough.

Canned cranberry is not capable of producing this magic. It is sickeningly sweet, usually full of corn syrup and high fructose corn syrup and probably somehow more corn syrup. There are cranberries, yes, and cranberries are tart, but with all the sugar added, that tartness is overruled to the point of utter inefficacy. It’s jello. You’re basically just eating jello.

You do need some sweetness to cranberry sauce, of course, but we can do so much better than this. We can make a lovely little dish of relish that will sit neatly on the plate, waiting to be dabbled onto basically any other savory item there to give it a welcome splash of delicious brightness, and which will taste pleasant on its own at the end of the meal when you still have a bit left you need to scrape up to clean your plate. What’s more, it will also serve well as a spread for a Thanksgiving leftovers sandwich in the following days.

A perfectly cromulent cranberry sauce is easy to make. You need, at minimum, a bag of berries, a cup of sugar, and ideally some orange juice, like a third of a cup. We can add other things, but let’s start simple. There’s a super basic recipe in my personal cooking bible, The Joy of Cooking, that just calls for those items plus a cup of water to all be tossed in a pot on medium heat until simmering. The cranberries will eventually start to “pop,” a deeply pleasant sound indicating they are bursting from their little berry skins and making lovely tart cranberry flavor happen. After about ten minutes of this, you have a fine cranberry sauce. Let it chill so it gels a bit. Serve. You’re already miles ahead of the canned stuff.

Now, from here, we can modify things. If it’s absolutely too tart for you, add a bit more sugar. Go on, I won’t judge. If you need a crunch, The Joy of Cooking recommends some slivered almonds at the end, or crushed pecans or walnuts. For a bit more citrus flavor, you can add orange zest during the cooking process, or you can add any number of spices if that’s your bag: black pepper, cinnamon, cloves, ginger, five-spice, thyme, or rosemary. You can mix in other types of fruit, too, like cherries, currants, raisins, apples, or even shallots. What gruhbology does here that I have never tried before is soaking his cranberries in a bath of water and baking soda before cooking. Googling it, this seems to just be a way of cleaning them before cooking with them, but I’m going to try it this year to see if it makes any kind of difference. I’ll report back. Now, the actual recipe I use year after year is from the World of Warcraft cookbook. Yes, really. I cook about half of my Thanksgiving spread annually from this book. It taught me how to cook turkey. I’ve never regretted it.

The basic recipe is still roughly the same, and will be no matter where you go for cranberry sauce.: no matter what, you need cranberries, water, and sugar, cooked until the cranberries pop. But in the WoW cookbook, it’s a little fancier. You start by cooking down a diced small onion, minced garlic, candied ginger, apple cider vinegar, honey, port, balsamic vinegar, a whole pressed clove, cinnamon, coriander, pepper, and pinches each of nutmeg, ground cloves, and salt in a sauce pan. Then you add diced pear and apple cooked until the fruit is soft,Â then the cranberries. The final product is still wonderfully tart and obviously cranberry sauce, but has a little more variety in each bite in both texture and flavor. This year, I’m too lazy to buy port (and the leftovers never get drunk anyway), so I’ll probably just dash some red wine in there and add a little extra honey to balance it out. I might also skip the candied ginger for the same reason. Live your truth.

Anyway, I don’t care how you do it. This year, I invite you to branch out of your comfort zone. If you’re going to the house of someone who does can-cran year after year, consider making your own cranberry sauce and bringing it, and inviting people to try. Act like it’s an insult to you personally if they don’t at least give it a little taste. If someone else is cooking and always makes their own and you never try it, for God’s sake, give it a go. And make sure you and everyone else tries a little bit of the sauce with something savory, so they can understand the power of the acid in bringing out the flavor of the other dishes.

I believe in you. Ditch the canned cranberry sauce and experience a real Thanksgiving for the first time in your life. You deserve better than cranberry goop that looks like it was dropped by a Dark Souls enemy. We don’t have to live like this.
