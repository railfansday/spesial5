Can AI actually help the climate? Nations want to harness the technology to cut emissions

Ten countries, including Germany and Brazil, at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, have pledged to do more to limit the greenhouse-gas emissions produced by the rapid development of artificial intelligence — and to use the technology to cut emissions elsewhere.

“The pledge brings together two priorities that must advance together: reducing the environmental footprint of AI and expanding its contribution to climate action,” said Mehmet Fatih Kacır, Turkey’s minister of industry and technology, on 16 November at a launch event for the initiative, called the Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge.

Some climate advocates welcomed the pledge as a necessary, if vague, step to address the growing emissions and environmental impacts caused by running AI data centres. And researchers say there are ways that the technology could be used to cut emissions, for instance by helping to integrate renewables onto power grids or by improving logistics.

But it remains unknown whether such emission reductions could offset AI’s consequences for the climate. “The trillion-dollar question is whether or not those savings will outweigh those impacts we’re seeing now,” says Eric Masanet, a mechanical engineer who studies decarbonization at the University of California, Santa Barbara.

The rapid building of AI data centres — which are used to train and operate AI systems — has drawn increasing ire from environmental quarters because of its insatiable demand for electricity. A report this month from BloombergNEF, which provides research on the transition to clean energy, estimated that electricity demand from data centres will almost quadruple over the next decade, making up 7% of global electricity demand in 2035, and accounting for at least 2% of the world’s emissions.

Most of that would come from the United States, where the building of data centres has been biggest and fastest, followed by China. In the race to train the most capable models, many big US technology firms are seeking to build gas-fired power plants to satisfy data centres’ energy demands, rather than waiting for permission to connect to the public power grid.

That’s the sort of approach the pledge aims to prevent. It calls on countries to take action to power AI infrastructure with “zero- and low-emission energy” and to improve the efficiency with which data centres use power and water for cooling. Nations that join will assess progress yearly.

The other G20 countries that signed on are South Korea and Turkey. The host country sponsored the launch of the pledge and said that it would aim to power its data centres with 60% low-carbon sources. They were joined by Andorra, Colombia, Luxembourg, Sierra Leone, Somalia and Uganda. More than 80 organizations endorsed the pledge, including a few large tech companies such as Nokia and Telefónica, according to a tracker from the United Nations’ International Telecommunications Union, one of the effort’s organizing bodies. However, none of the frontier AI firms has joined the pledge.

Given the absence of AI’s biggest emitters, some climate advocates dismissed the initiative. “The AI pledge risks giving Big Tech a green stamp without requiring it to address the real climate impacts of its infrastructure,” says Jill McArdle, a campaigner at Beyond Fossil Fuels, an advocacy group in Berlin.
