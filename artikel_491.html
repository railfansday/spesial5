Sunrise on the Reaping review: The biggest and boldest Hunger Games yet

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Are you looking for a movie to thoroughly wallop you? To leave you emotionally battered, physically exhausted, and full of despair and hope at the same time? Then allow me to prescribe a healthy dose of The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, the sixth film in the Hunger Games series.

Based on Suzanne Collins' second prequel novel following The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, Sunrise on the Reaping once again rewinds the clock on Panem to the 50th Hunger Games, otherwise known as the Second Quarter Quell. These are the biggest Hunger Games yet, with 48 tributes instead of the standard 24. Among them is Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada), future mentor to series protagonists Katniss Everdeen and Peeta Mellark.

The question here is not whether Haymitch will survive the games. We know, by virtue of the entire original trilogy, that he does. But before he was the cynical, heavy-drinking, lone wolf victor audiences met in The Hunger Games, he was an optimistic 16-year-old with a caring family, a loving relationship, and a solid community in District 12. So the real question of Sunrise on the Reaping becomes, what broke that young man down so thoroughly? The answer is, unsurprisingly, bleak as all hell. But director Francis Lawrence executes it in thrilling (if occasionally overstuffed) fashion, with a Hunger Games sequence that blows series-high Catching Fire's own games out of the water, and a dogged focus on the philosophical themes at the core of Collins' work.

This being a Hunger Games movie, there are certain key beats it needs to hit. First, tributes need to be reaped. In this case, District 12's motley crew is made up of Haymitch, Wyatt Callow (Ben Wang), Maysilee Donner (Mckenna Grace), and Louella McCoy (Molly McCann). From there, the tributes must go to the Capitol and witness its gaudy depravity for themselves. They get makeovers, train, give interviews, and eventually make their way into the Capitol's ultimate death trap.

Viewers have seen these beats before in The Hunger Games and Catching Fire. They also witnessed a deliberately scaled-back version in prequel The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, which established the Games' evolution from what was essentially a run-down cage match to a glossy propaganda spectacle.

After sending us through three very different Hunger Games, how can the franchise create a new variation on the same brutal theme? First, by making Sunrise on the Reaping a heist movie. Granted, Haymitch and his allies aren't actually trying to steal anything from the Capitol. Rather, they're trying to break the arena and send a clear message. For that, Haymitch gets roped into a scheme cooked up by former victor Beetee (Kelvin Harrison Jr.) and his son, District 3 tribute Ampert (Percy Daggs IV). With a complex series of moving parts, Ampert and Haymitch's plan captures the essence of a dangerous heist, and Lawrence delivers the suspense to match.

Then, there's the arena. If you thought Catching Fire's clock was a sadistic marvel of game-making, not to mention a visual feast thanks to its tropical jungles and beaches, get ready for a level up. The arena for the Second Quarter Quell is a technicolor nightmare. Lush flower beds and crystal clear streams hide poisonous underbellies. Swarms of beautiful birds and butterflies will kill at a moment's notice. Even an idyllic, snow-capped mountain hides explosive horrors. Thankfully, Sunrise on the Reaping is actually colorful to match, its eye-popping hues reminding blockbuster filmmakers everywhere that yes, vibrancy is still possible!

That vibrancy in Sunrise on the Reaping extends to the broader world of Panem itself, which has taken on a distinctive '70s look. The Capitol is awash in glitzy psychedelics, a far cry from the post-war dilapidation of The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes. As the Games have grown more extravagant, so, too have the Capitol citizens. Take District 12 escort Drusilla Sickle (Glenn Close, almost unrecognizable under prosthetics and an uncanny tape facelift). She is the pinnacle of excess, from her visible cosmetic work to her neon fashion. Close is chewing so much scenery she could probably devour the entire arena, but it's exactly what the role calls for: ridiculous pomp, circumstance, and venomous hatred for the Districts.

Drusilla is one of the new characters introduced in Sunrise on the Reaping. However, a big chunk of the film's supporting cast is made up of familiar figures from the original trilogy: Capitol insider Plutarch Heavensbee (Jesse Plemons); master of ceremonies Caesar Flickerman (Kieran Culkin); former victors Beetee, Mags (Lili Taylor), and Wiress (Maya Hawke); stylist Effie Trinket (Elle Fanning); and of course, President Coriolanus Snow (Ralph Fiennes). Their non-stop introductions give Sunrise on the Reaping an unfortunate case of prequel-itis, even if they play key roles in the story. Their presence also means the involvement of many new Capitol characters feels muted. Billy Porter's snake-loving stylist Magno Stift gets short shrift (and even shorter screen time), while his helpers Proserpina (Iris Apatow) and Vitus (Edvin Ryding) might as well be afterthoughts.

Thankfully, the District 12 newcomers fare far better. As Haymitch's great love Lenore Dove, Whitney Peak is a radiant beam of rebellious wisdom. Elsewhere, each of the tributes buck misconceptions about them to reveal their true mettle underneath. Maysilee may seem stuck-up and aloof at first, but Grace quickly unleashes the ferocious survivor in her. Wang brings kind courage to oddsmaker Wyatt, and McCann is steely sweet as Louella.

Leading it all is Zada, who does an admirable job making Haymitch his own, all while balancing Harrelson's mannerisms from the original trilogy. Put them saying "sweetheart" side by side and tell me that's not the same character, years apart.

Sunrise on the Reaping works hard to build out this captivating new version of Panem and the Games, but once it starts really playing in that sandbox, you're in for wave after wave of brutality. It's almost impressive how, after five books and six movies, the Hunger Games franchise can still find new ways to up the emotional pain of watching children fight to death under the thumb of an authoritarian regime. You'd think we'd already hit that peak with Rue's death in the first Hunger Games, and yet!

No spoilers, but let's just say I hope Zada's contract included a bonus for each time he had to cradle the body of a dear ally and tearfully beg them to stay with him. That his performance and the film never feel stale as they go through the motions of helpless grief time and time again is a testament to how distinct each of Haymitch's relationships feel.

But while Sunrise on the Reaping is arguably the Hunger Games at its most tragic, it's also the franchise at its angriest. Every instalment in the series has critiqued the Capitol, but Sunrise on the Reaping puts a laser focus on the mundane machinery that keeps these systems of power in place. As the film opens, we see Peacekeepers and Capitol staffers performing administrative tasks for the Reaping, like putting up barricades and placards dictating where children should stand. Elsewhere, the propaganda machine chugs along, with cameramen like Plutarch always finding the perfect shot to tell the Capitol's story. (And always knowing which context to leave out to make the Capitol look its absolute best.)

Throughout, though, Sunrise on the Reaping also turns its lens on Panem's citizens, asking why they allow themselves to be ruled by such a horrific regime. Collins opens Sunrise on the Reaping with several epigraphs, including two from Scottish philosopher David Hume, the first of which details his thoughts on implicit submission. The film takes that message to heart, repeatedly questioning what leads the many not to rise up against the few that govern them. Why do District 12's citizens stand mutely by while Drusilla and Plutarch re-stage a key moment from the Reaping ceremony? Why do armed tributes not turn on the unarmed Peacekeepers who oversee them at training? Sunrise on the Reaping doesn't take a subtle approach — at one point Plutarch straight-up says "no more implicit submission" — but then again, it's also vital to see a blockbuster not sand down the novel's overt messaging.

It's also vital for a film this heavy to have hope, and almost miraculously, Sunrise on the Reaping, does. The film emphasizes the fact of its title: that for Haymitch's whole life, the sun has always risen on the Reaping. But as Lenore tells him, just because that's the way things have been for so long doesn't mean they'll be that way forever. (Their conversation remixes the novel's second epigraph from Hume.)

Anyone who's watched or read the original Hunger Games series knows that Panem will one day see a world where the sun doesn't rise on the Reaping. It'll just take a lot of work and sacrifice to get there. In Sunrise on the Reaping, we get to witness the first sparks of that eventual reunion. Knowing where Haymitch's story ends turns his tragedy into a bittersweet bridge to the future, making Sunrise on the Reaping a perfect bookend to the Hunger Games franchise as it stands. (At least, until Collins decides to return to Panem.)

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping hits theaters Nov. 20.
