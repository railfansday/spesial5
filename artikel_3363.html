6 Books From the Year 2024 That Are 10/10 Masterpieces

There’s a bench near my house where I like to read in the evenings. It’s not comfortable, and the light fades fast, but you got to do what you got to do. Last year, I spent more evenings on that bench than I’d like to admit, usually telling myself “just one more chapter” until it got too dark to see the page.

2024 was a strong year for books I’ll be honest, and that made this list harder than I expected. I kept changing the order, moving one book up and another down, then changing it back again. In the end, I went with my gut. These are the six books I kept thinking about long after I closed them, the ones I pushed into my friends’ hands and said, “Just read it, trust me.” So, here they are.

Peter and Ivan Koubek are brothers who have just lost their father, and they don’t really know how to talk to each other. Peter is 32, a successful lawyer in Dublin, stuck between his first love, Sylvia, and Naomi, a much younger student. Ivan is 22, a shy chess player who falls for Margaret, an older woman he meets at a chess event in a small town. Intermezzo follows both brothers as grief quietly messes with every part of their lives.

Sally Rooney, who gave us Normal People, tries something new here. Peter’s chapters are messy and broken, like thoughts racing at 2 a.m., while Ivan’s are slow and careful, just like him. It takes a few pages to get used to, but then it clicks. Besides all the love triangles, this is really a book about two brothers who are trying to find their way back to each other.

It’s August 1975, and a girl goes missing from a summer camp in the Adirondack Mountains. Her name is Barbara Van Laar, she’s 13, and her rich family owns the camp. The scary part is that this has happened before. Fourteen years earlier, Barbara’s older brother, Bear, also disappeared in the same woods and was never found. The God of the Woods jumps between different people and different years as everyone tries to figure out what really happened.

Liz Moore juggles a lot of characters here, from a nervous camp counselor to a young female investigator nobody takes seriously. Somehow, she never drops a single thread. It’s a mystery, sure, but it’s also about money, power, and how rich families hide their secrets. The ending pulls everything together so neatly that I wanted to flip back to page one and start again.

A 45-year-old artist in Los Angeles plans a big road trip to New York, alone, to prove something to herself. But she only drives about 30 minutes before checking into a plain little motel. Then she never leaves. She spends a ridiculous amount of money redecorating her room and falls hard for Davey, a much younger man who works at a car rental place. Meanwhile, her husband and child think she’s halfway across the country.

All Fours is weird, funny, and sometimes so honest it makes you blush. Miranda July writes about getting older, especially what middle age does to a woman’s body and mind, in a way few writers dare to. The book was a finalist for the 2024 National Book Award, and it sparked a lot of loud group chats among women everywhere. You might cringe a little, but you won’t be bored for a second.

Most of us know The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn by Mark Twain, where a boy named Huck runs away down the Mississippi River with Jim, an enslaved man. James tells the same journey, but this time through Jim’s eyes. And the first big surprise is that Jim is clever, well-read, and only talks in a “simple” way around white people to keep himself safe. Among his own people, he speaks perfectly well.

Percival Everett, whose novel Erasure became the film American Fiction, turns a classic upside down and makes it feel brand new. The book is funny in places, which you won’t expect, but it’s also brutal and heartbreaking. It won the 2024 National Book Award for Fiction and later the 2025 Pulitzer Prize. Once you read it, you’ll never see the original the same way again, and honestly, that’s the whole point.

In 1965, Frankie McGrath was a sheltered 20-year-old nursing student from California. When her brother ships off to Vietnam, she makes a bold choice and joins the Army Nurse Corps. Nothing prepares her for what she finds there. Endless injuries, terrible losses, and friendships that keep her going. But the real shock comes later, when she returns home and finds that America doesn’t want to hear about women who served in the war.

Kristin Hannah, the author of The Nightingale, knows exactly how to break your heart, and she does it slowly. The Women is a big, emotional book, and I cried more than once (okay, a lot). What makes it special is that it shines a light on women who were pushed out of the war’s story for decades. Keep tissues nearby, because you’ll need them.

Phoebe Stone shows up at a fancy hotel in Newport, Rhode Island, in her best green dress. She’s sad, lonely, and has quietly planned for this to be her last trip. But there’s a problem. The whole hotel has been booked for a week-long wedding, and she’s the only guest who isn’t part of it. Soon, the bride, Lila, finds out why Phoebe is there, and the two strangers get tangled up in each other’s lives.

It sounds like a dark setup, but The Wedding People is one of the warmest, funniest books I read all year. Alison Espach handles Phoebe’s pain with care, and never makes fun of it. Instead, she shows how a bunch of noisy, annoying strangers can pull someone back toward life without even meaning to. I finished it with a smile, a few tears, and a strange urge to hug someone.

You belong to the fandom that is really about comfort. Hogwarts works on people because it is a place rather than a plot, and the pull is not the wizard war so much as the corridors, the food, the weather and the sense that somebody is expecting you.

You belong to the deepest fandom on this list, the one where somebody has always read further than you and is quietly delighted to say so. Middle-earth rewards patience more than any other world in popular culture.

You belong to the loudest and largest tent of the four, the one where three generations are all watching the same thing and none of them agree about any of it. That disagreement is not a flaw in this fandom, it is the main activity.
