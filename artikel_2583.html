How the Miami Heat saved the season after a terrible start

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

The Miami Heat ended last season in an awkward spot. For the fourth straight year, they found themselves in the NBA Play-In Tournament. Unfortunately, this was the first time they couldn't make it out. They have a S-Tier leading man calling the shots, a battle-tested defensive anchor, and a culture of winning in the ugliest ways possible.

Their roster, while nothing special, was far from the dregs of the association. It contained a good blend of young bucks and proven veterans. If the injury gods smiled in their favor and a couple of prospects took meaningful steps forward, the Heat could have run it back and flirted with 50 wins. And with their knack for upsets, maybe a postseason series victory would have followed.

However, the Heat, like the Minnesota Timberwolves, remembered that they didn't come to the table to nickel and dime their way through the nights. In Miami, they play for keeps. Win big, or go bankrupt trying. So, when Giannis Antetokounmpo finally became available, the Heat swung for the fences – trading the farm in the hopes that the singular excellence of the former two-time MVP, coupled with Bam Adebayo and the scrapes that remained would be enough to vault the team into true contention.

Through nine games, it looked like they would be filing for Chapter 7. The Heat were 3-6 and floundering, while their contemporaries (the Detroit Pistons, Boston Celtics, and Philadelphia 76ers) were thriving out the gate. But fast forward to today, and it looks like the big bet they placed may have been the right call.

After their 10-point win over the depleted Oklahoma City Thunder, the Heat are winners of their last seven games and now sit at No. 6 in the Eastern Conference – fully within striking distance of the conference's upper echelon.

In a team sport like basketball, it's foolish to point to one variable as the reason for positive change, as so many factors contribute to success, but it is hard not to watch this team and look at the ring leader as the catalyst: Erik Spoelstra.

Spoelstra may be the best coach in basketball. Heck, he may be one of the five best coaches that ever lived (we know he's at least one of the top 15). Spoelstra is innovative enough to turn oranges into lemonade. No, that's not a typo that was missed by my editor. The creative lens Spoelstra is able to analyze the game through literally defies everything we know about the human experience.

With an average age of 28.8 years old, the Heat have the second-oldest team in the NBA (behind only the Golden State Warriors). That is bad news bears in today's game – one that rewards athleticism, pace, and physicality. Spoelstra realized it was time to demote the veterans that most coaches would remain beholden too.

His biggest change was taking Andrew Wiggins (31 years old), and putting Pelle Larsson in his place (25). And when he's been hurt, instead of reverting back to Wiggins, Spoelstra simply plugs in Myron Gardner (25) in his place. A former second rounder and undrafted player in the place of a former first overall pick, NBA champion, and the third-highest paid player on the team's payroll.

Wiggins wasn't the only casualty. Bobby Portis' minutes are down, and Tim Hardaway Jr. is basically out of the rotation entirely. Along with Larsson and Gardner, those minutes are being eaten up by the youthful vigor of second round rookie Ryan Conwell (22). Despite hearing 36 names called before his on draft night, Conwell is seventh among rookies in minutes per game (26.2) in their last seven games.

Delegating hustle duties to fresher legs lessens the burdens on Antetokounmpo, Adebayo, Klay Thompson, and Davion Mitchell's shoulders, and enables them to focus more energy on scoring the ball. The Heat's effective field goal percentage is up 4.8% over the last seven games.

Spoelstra has also made a key strategic shift. To start the season, the Heat were doing a lot of hard hedging with ball screens involving their bigs. The idea is that since they start two center-sized players, they could afford for one of them to play these actions more aggressively, since the other one would be there to clean it up.

The idea wasn't a bad one, in theory, but playing this way asks the three guys not involved in the action to do a lot of flying around to cover up the gaps. Antetokounmpo, now in Year 14, has a lot of tread on his tires, and scrambling around possession after possession isn't necessarily high on his list of priorities.

So, instead of trying to fit a square peg into a round hole, Spoelstra scrapped the idea and started switching everything (except actions involving Portis, which the Heat continue to hedge). This asks Antetokounmpo and company to do a lot less rotating around, and it still lets the Heat benefit from their twin tower configuration (when one of them is switched onto the perimeter, the other can still lurk around the painted area).

As a result, the Heat are forcing fewer turnovers (the tradeoff of playing a less aggressive style). However, by switching everything, it is easier for them to keep the ball in front of them, and so the Heat's opponents are shooting far worse from the floor than they were before. And again, it conserves more energy, so the Heat have their legs under them on their own shots.

Due to the constraints of the written medium, we can't dissect ALL of Spoelstra's game-changing tweaks, but here are a couple of other developments you can see his fingerprint on: convincing Antetokounmpo into operating more off-the-ball, squeezing the last ounce of magic out of Thompson, and gradually helping mold Mitchell from bust to baller.

The good news for the Heat is that there are some aspects that will improve with time. Despite rostering Antetokounmpo and Adebayo – who have 11 All-Defensive Teams between the two of them – Miami is currently 24th in defensive rating.

The adjustments the Heat have made with minute allocation and scheme have helped bump them from 26th in this category in their first nine games to 17th in the last seven, but they are still being hurt by some bad luck.

This season, the Heat are surrendering a whopping 45.6% on wide open threes (per NBA.com). That is 2.7% than the next highest team, and 6.1% higher than the league average. The Heat under Spoelstra tend to give up more threes in order to prioritize protecting the rim, but defense has nothing to do with how well opponents shoot on open looks. That's just luck. If/when that number regresses to the mean (no team has allowed higher than 43% on these shots for an entire season), Miami's defensive rating will automatically improve.

Unfortunately, the Heat have also been the beneficiary of some good luck. According to Estimated Plus-Minus, they have had the third-easiest strength of schedule. They have also lost the fourth-fewest dollars to player injuries (per Spotrac). Antetokounmpo and Thompson have only missed one game each. Mitchell and Adebayo have been available for every game. What happens if one or two of those guys is out for an extended stretch? This isn't a super deep team.

More than likely, the conflicting noise between their negative shooting luck and positive schedule/injury history thus far is a wash. To me, the pace this Heat are currently on (51 wins) seems about right. They are a good team that, even with Spoelstra's magic touch, still has some glaring flaws (on-ball creation, offensive variety, depth) in a conference filled with them.

If they draw the first round opponent or injuries cause them to fall into the play-in tournament for the fifth straight year, another early exit could be in their future. But if Spoelstra keeps putting on a coaching clinic in the best league on the planet and Antetokounmpo can stay healthy enough to match him for a whole postseason, maybe, just maybe, the Heat can return to the NBA Finals for the first time since 2023.
