‘Exotic’ Director Sonja Orlewicz-Zakrzewska Talks About the Liberating Power of Pole-Dancing: ‘Do It Just for Yourself’

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Debuting Polish director Sonja Orlewicz-Zakrzewska destigmatizes pole dancing in Tallinn Black Nights premiere “Exotic.”

“It still shocks so many people,” she tells Variety.

“Most of the girls and boys from the pole dancing community have experienced some kind of ostracism. People associate it with men’s erotic pleasure. Orlewicz-Zakrzewska is a pole dancer too.

“Pole dancing blew my mind,” she admits. “I wanted to show this environment as a sporting environment. In every big city, there are a dozen or so pole dancing studios. But when I was pitching this project, people would hear ‘pole dancing’ and ask: ‘So it takes place in a nightclub?’ There’s nothing wrong with nightclubs or sex work, but these are very different things.”

“Exotic” is produced by Magdalena Sztorc and Anna Zajączkowska (Mozaika Films) and sold by Pluto Film.

In the film, the characters – played by Jaśmina Polak, Magdalena Koleśnik and Monika Frajczyk – head to a sleepy, unassuming beach resort to train. Just like Orlewicz-Zakrzewska. “Exotic” was shot during one of the Exo Stars Camp editions she also attended in the past.

“There are all these international pole dancing stars. People come from all over the world, and it’s all happening….There. I love absurdities and contrasts, so when I got out of the car and saw these two universes colliding, I knew I had to go back with a camera,” she laughs.

With the help of its founder Martyna Dominikowska, Orlewicz-Zakrzewska ended up shooting with real-life participants and instructors.

“It was all scripted, but we threw our actors into a documentary setup. These classes and showcases were actually taking place,” she recalls.

“One of the pole dancers, Gracjan, works as a mechanic on ships. He was supposed to be on his way to Gibraltar at the time of the shoot. He wanted to be a part of it so badly that he convinced his employer not only to find a replacement for the time she would be gone, but also to make sure he could disembark and fly to Poland. It’s my favorite story from the film.”

Orlewicz-Zakrzewska found pole dancing to be “very healing.” However, her protagonist, Mila, struggles with self-worth. Her inner voice repeatedly tells her: “You don’t belong here, girl.”

“I don’t want to speak for the rest of society, but I definitely have that internal voice. We are all each other’s worst critics. I’ve been training for four years now, but when I started, I was convinced I wouldn’t be able to identify with these dancers,” she says.

“I’ve always questioned my choices and I guess that’s reflected in the film. I’ve always asked myself: Why am I putting on makeup or dressing this way? I was afraid of my own femininity and I judged others. When I started my adventure with pole dancing and became someone unafraid of sky-high heels, I realized that my fear of being seen as ‘cheap’ came directly from patriarchy.”

Mila’s partner, played by “Corpus Christi” star Bartosz Bielenia, isn’t exactly supportive either.

“That character came from my conversations with girls in the pole dancing community. Their partners often pretend to be cool with it, but then their insecurities take over. They can see it liberates them, and that makes them feel threatened.”

Orlewicz-Zakrzewska wanted to be authentic in showing the bodies in her film.

“Just because you’re filming a woman’s body doesn’t mean it’s male gaze. It’s all about your intention,” she notes.

“My cinematographer Adam Suzin is also a sportsman and has trained acrobatics, so we thought about it together. My dream is to witness the destigmatization of bodies in public spaces, especially in Poland, where we’re still laced into a Catholic corset.”

“I tried to show what it’s like for me, a girl who is in this environment. You look at your body a lot, and the bodies of others. In the age of social media, we all have a very warped sense of how we think bodies should look. It was great to go to these sessions and see how different they are. Sometimes it’s so awkward, and funny, seeing someone try to strike a pose but end up looking like a cockroach,” she jokes.

“For me, a big lesson from pole dancing – which I tried to include in the film – is that attractiveness doesn’t come from your perfectly sculpted buttocks. It comes from how you feel about yourself and your attitude.”

“It’s about being able to express yourself freely through your body.”
