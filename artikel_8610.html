If fear keeps you from starting a business, make the worst case small enough to live with. Start on the side while you keep your job, and before you spend anything, set a budget you can afford to lose and a date to decide whether it’s working. Spend more only once customers are paying.

Plenty of owners already run a business alongside another job. Nearly four in five U.S. small businesses have no employees, a 2026 Federal Reserve report found, and for almost four in 10 of those, the business isn’t the main owner’s only job.

You don’t need a brand-new idea either. A better or more local version of something people already buy is enough. A service you know how to do can start with a laptop, a simple website, and some software. On the side, it takes only the hours you give it. And if you sell under your own legal name, you don’t have to register the business anywhere, the Small Business Administration says, though you’ll still need any license or permit your work requires before your first sale.

Keeping your job also improves the business’s odds. In a study published in the Academy of Management Journal, people who started a business while still employed and went full time later were 33% less likely to fail than those who quit a job to start one.

Picture a bookkeeper who caps her costs at $500 for software and a website, then does the books for three local shops on weekends. After a year, the clients pay enough that she cuts her job to part time.

Expect mistakes, like a price set too low or a client who pays late. On a small budget each one costs little, so fix it quickly instead of trying to prevent every one.

Write the three limits down today, before you buy anything for the business.
