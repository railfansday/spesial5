Buy a New Car Only for a 0% Loan or a Must-Have Feature

Buy a two- or three-year-old car rather than a new one, since a new car loses value fastest in its first years. Go new only when the automaker offers you 0% or a low-rate loan, or when you need a safety or fuel-saving feature no used car in your budget has.

The average new vehicle sold for a record $51,109 in October 2026, according to Kelley Blue Book (KBB). KBB estimates that a new car loses about 30% of its value in its first two years and 8% to 12% a year after that.

That early loss is why a two- or three-year-old car is the better buy for most people, and keeping a car for a decade doesn’t change that by itself. A 0% or low-rate loan from the automaker is what narrows the cost gap between new and used.

Take a $45,000 new car and its two-year-old twin at about $31,500, each financed in full for five years and kept for 10 years. The loans carry October’s average rates of 7.3% for new cars and 11% for used ones, according to Edmunds. Assume each car loses 10% of its remaining value a year once it’s two years old, the middle of KBB’s range. Counting interest and what each is worth at the end, the new car costs you about $40,300 over those 10 years and the twin about $30,100. Neither total counts insurance, fuel, or upkeep, the other parts of what a car costs to own.

With a 0% loan on the new car, its total falls to about $31,400, only about $1,300 more than the twin’s $30,100. That’s about $130 a year for a car two years newer with its full factory warranty, which makes new the better buy.

The other case for new is a safety or fuel-saving feature you need that no used car in your budget has. A new electric car no longer qualifies for the federal tax credit, which ended for those bought after Sept. 30, 2025.

Before you shop, get preapproved for a used-car loan and look up the automaker’s current financing offers on its website, then total both cars the same way over the years you plan to keep one.
