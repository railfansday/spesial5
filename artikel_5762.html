Impeach and Remove RFK Jr.

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

— The incoming Congress has several avenues to pursue Secretary Kennedy's impeachment

When the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, Secretary of HHS Robert F. Kennedy Jr. should be impeached by the House of Representatives and then removed by the Senate.

Can this be done? Yes. Although classically associated with presidents, impeachment applies equivalently to federal officials. The House votes to impeach by a simple majority, which triggers a Senate impeachment trial. Removal occurs if two-thirds of senators agree the official engaged in "...treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors."

Just two cabinet secretaries have been previously impeached, the most recent being Secretary of Homeland Security Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024. Also of note, articles of impeachment already were filed against Kennedy in 2025 by Rep. Haley Stevens (D-Mich.).

Impeachment can serve many purposes even without removal: the personal and professional embarrassment of the impeached official or to bring information to light through document discovery and the compelled testimony. Politicians can also grandstand during impeachments and drive points home for votes. For example, when the House impeached Secretary Mayorkas in 2024, his probability of removal was zero as the Senate was controlled by the other party, which opposed his impeachment. A trial of merits then never even occurred as the Senate voted to dismiss. As a result, the process was arguably performative.

The goal of Kennedy's impeachment would be removal from office because he is dangerous to public health -- because without it, more Americans will needlessly suffer and die. That is the only goal in my mind.

Which Arguments Could Kennedy's Impeachment be Based Upon?

Several options exist to pursue Kennedy's impeachment. All appear valid, and we don't have to select just one, though each also has strengths and weakness, pros and cons.

One avenue is looking at dereliction of duty, particularly in our ongoing measles outbreak. The number of American measles deaths should be zero. It was zero for years, and safe, effective vaccination exists. A single measles death is an unacceptable tragedy laid at the feet of pernicious leaders such as Kennedy. Now, the U.S. is on the brink of losing its measles elimination status, pending the outcome of the Pan American Health Organization meeting.

These arguments mirror those of Mayorkas' impeachment alleging incompetence at managing the southern border, therefore endangering Americans. It's also emotionally resonant as we are all watching the measles outbreak stack caskets in real time.

But "dereliction" and "competence" could prove qualitative rather than explicitly provable. Kennedy, for example, could claim that HHS is doing its best though measles is simply an intractable foe.

A stronger argument may thus lie in his cancellation of $8.9 billion in biomedical research grants. Unlike degrees of dereliction, grant cancellation is an unassailable fact that violates Congress' "power of the purse" as the funds had been earmarked. Regardless of party, if Congress approves funds for an expenditure then the expenditure should be discharged. For congressionally funded programs, the executive branch is executor, not executioner.

The administration's multi-front assault on research draws less attention than deserved given its long-term harm. A generation of medical scientists is being kneecapped, laboratories are shuttering, universities reeling. This magnitude of harm is incalculable because it is unknown -- what are the future cures that are now lost?

Even if impeachment proceeds, Kennedy's removal is a separate question. Removal in the Senate requires 67 votes; an admittedly high bar, never before achieved. The new Senate makeup is still pending narrow races. However, the final party distribution also shouldn't matter, as removal cannot be partisan in the first place. At 67 votes, removal will require both parties, so a politically centered impeachment would end with Kennedy still secretary. But questions of basic competence and our country's health should not be partisan.

Would a sufficient number of senators from both parties vote to remove? One lesson hopefully learned in this midterm election is that, regardless of party, politicians embrace unpopular figures at their peril, and Kennedy is deeply unpopular. The anti-vaccination movement is disproportionately loud, especially with conspiracists now in positions of power, though this fundamentally misrepresents voters' overwhelming support of vaccines. So, an electoral argument could resonate with a senator on either side of the aisle.

Kennedy's anemic support would likely further erode through the discovery process if impeachment proceeds. He's performed dismally during prior congressional testimony, as one would expect from someone with no formal training -- or even real-world work experience -- in healthcare, medicine, or science. The new Congress is on track for a record number of physicians, with doctors who support vaccination sitting on both sides of the aisle in both chambers. How many times have physicians, called before Congress, faced grilling from legislators who are lawyers? Now a lawyer, Kennedy, may face questions from leaders who are doctors.

So, is removal ultimately likely? To be frank, no, it remains definitively uphill. But it's worth pursuing.

Several counterarguments to impeachment exist, including the discretion given to the executive branch in managing agencies and the fact that senators previously voted for instatement while well aware of Kennedy's conspiratorial incompetence. There's also genuine concern for establishing precedent in which future secretaries become routinely impeached by opposing parties. Prior to 2024 there had only been one executive impeachment, and now there could be two in short order. (Meanwhile, the recent effort to impeach Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth was blocked.) And finally, the effort would be an uphill battle, with the risk of Kennedy emerging stronger should removal fail.

But Kennedy is a historically dangerous individual. How many deaths emanate from his secretaryship and how many more will die if his power is left unchecked? In our work every day, doctors bravely contemplate actions for which we balance risk against the possibility of lives saved if successful. This is no different. Impeachment is fraught, and there are risks, but we have, in the coming months, a window of opportunity with action demanded by the net calculus of consequences.
