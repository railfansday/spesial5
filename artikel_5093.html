The P vs NP question in computer science pops up in our feeds way more often than it did just a few days ago. No wonder—it's a problem mostly relevant and interesting to mathematicians and computer scientists, and it's also a Clay Millennium Prize problem. It asks a relatively simple question: whether every problem whose solution can be checked quickly can also be solved quickly.

Short answer: no, it can't. P vs NP is an efficiency question. NP is about whether a proposed solution can be checked efficiently, while P covers problems that computers can solve efficiently. For example, solving a complicated Sudoku certainly takes time, but checking the result is not as hard.

The question itself simply asks whether every checkable problem has an efficient solution. P ≠ NP says that some problems are harder to solve than to verify, and that is a core question: most scientists expect P ≠ NP, but proving it is the hard part.

Cryptography is purely mathematical, so issues relevant to computation apply here. ZK-proofs represent a more relevant crypto technology used every day and connected to NP: a statement (such as a transaction) is proven without revealing additional data (a zero-knowledge proof).

But the question is different: could it raise any problems, cause security concerns, or lead to exploits? To some extent—yes. Public-key cryptography is certainly something that can be affected right away—as can blockchains, wallets, and even HTTPS—but P=NP alone wouldn't break the whole industry.

Not really, because you can't change the fundamental math. Even if consensus mechanisms are changed and networks are upgraded, it would be similar to solving 2=2. It's fundamental. But instead of 'solving' it, cryptocurrency projects might adapt to such challenges. New systems might avoid relying on assumptions, so such a change would be a reaction to the consequences.

Quantum computing is a different story. Unlike P vs NP, quantum computing is not just a theoretical question about computational complexity. It's a technological development that could directly affect existing cryptographic systems.
