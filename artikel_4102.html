I Wish Had Spoken To Reid Duke Before I Started Playing Magic: The Gathering

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

We spoke with MTG Hall of Famer Reid Duke about how new players should approach Magic and how it's different now than when he started playing.

Few names are as iconic as Reid Duke in Magic: The Gathering. Picking up the game at just five years old, Reid Duke went on to win the Magic Online World Championship in 2011, six Grand Prix events, and one Pro Tour. Still active in the competitive scene and helping teach the next generation of Magic players, Reid Duke is currently part of team TCGplayer, and when they extended the opportunity to chat with one of the game's legends, it was the fastest I've ever responded to an email.

Fast-forward to our chat, and Reid was exactly as many have already stated: a gentleman. He is easily the nicest person I've ever had the pleasure of interviewing, but better still, he's a fount of knowledge when it comes to Magic, and specifically how new players should approach the game. During our chat, we focused heavily on new players and their progression from discovering the game to picking a preferred format, and everything in between. Here are the highlights from our chat, which includes some advice I desperately wish I'd had when I first discovered Magic over 15 years ago!

The first question I asked Reid was about new players finding their way into Magic. I used my own story as an example: when I first discovered Magic, I had access to cards from a local comic shop, but no LGS to play at, and finding that environment was tough. The only LGS I knew of basically rejected me because I was new, and everyone else was a long-time player who didn't want to teach me. Thankfully, most LGS communities are far more accepting of new players now, and I've always made sure that any store I play at treats all players the same regardless of how long they've played the game. Still, with paper, Online, and MTG Arena as routes into the game, here's what Reid had to say on new players finding their way into Magic:

"I think Arena is a great tool, there are so many ways to engage with Magic, and there's not necessarily a right or a wrong way, but for a new player who doesn't even know how they prefer to engage with Magic, the first thing I would recommend is Arena, because it is so quick, so easy, so accessible. You don't have to put yourself out on a limb in terms of travel, research, or feeling embarrassed to learn the rules of the game in front of experienced players, so there's no beating Arena in terms of accessibility, and it's a really great platform for learning the rules."

Reid would go on to point out the obvious, which is that when he first engaged with Magic, there was no Magic Online or MTG Arena; all he had were the physical cards and the more personal approach of learning the game through making connections with other players, and that route is still extremely valid today, but I completely agree with his point on Arena being far more accessible and, most importantly in the current economy, free.

Four Commander precons for Nauctis: The Sunken Realm have appeared online, and there are some very interesting archetypes in play.

The next question I put to Reid was about Wizards' new-player agenda, and whether it was doing enough to consistently bring new players in and keep them playing. His response was a little unexpected in that, for someone who has been playing for so long, to acknowledge the challenge of getting into Magic due to the core rules is something I figured would be second nature to him now. Here is his full response:

"I think Wizards do a great job of a few things: first, making the best game in the world, but also providing a variety of formats, products and opportunities for players of every level. The biggest obstacle to new player acquisition in Magic is that it's a very challenging game. The rules are hard, and even the most popular casual format, Commander, is very, very complicated, and you encounter a lot of complicated new cards very quickly. New players should be aware that you're going to encounter some challenging stuff as you're learning Magic, but try to be patient and don't get intimidated by that. However, if you manage to get your hands on a beginner box or Foundations kit, you're going to learn the basics a bit quicker, and you can get to the harder mechanics as and when you're ready for them."

I personally didn't consider Magic a complicated game until I started playing games like Lorcana and Riftbound long after I already had Magic pretty much sussed out, but to hear from a 30+ year pro that the game is hard is somewhat validating of those early struggles and should make every player feel better about where they are in their Magic journey.

Now that we've covered some of the routes into Magic and how Wizards is doing at getting new players into the game, I wanted to know where Reid believes new players should go next. Over his career, Reid has played pretty much every format out there at least once (I imagine), but obviously we'd focus on a few core formats as the next step. Here's the advice he has on where new players should look for their first big format commitment:

"Once someone gets their feet underneath them, they've experienced Magic and learned the rules, I would really encourage them to just feel it out and go where the game takes them, and go to the format that's the most fun for them. Now, I will say that my first recommendation for basically anyone is to try Limited. Sealed deck or booster draft, some of the advantages of Limited are that it's a totally even playing field, it's affordable as you don't need a huge collection to get into it, and it's really strong for emphasizing the fundamentals of the game and rules. I think anyone can have fun with Limited, and from there, it's a branching path where, if you have a competitive itch, you will probably go to Standard next, or if your preference leads you to casual Commander, at and LGS or even the kitchen table, that is a super popular way to engage with Magic as well."

This is, and I mean this with zero hyperbole, phenomenal advice. I started playing Limited and prerelease events only in the last few years, and I've become a far better Magic player because of it, but Limited felt intimidating at first. Opening packs and building a deck seemed daunting, but Reid is absolutely right: it's the best and most logical next step once you've grasped the basics.

I genuinely wish someone had given me this advice 15 years ago, because my whole relationship with the game would be completely different, but hopefully, in having this discussion with Reid, there's at least one player out there who will now feel confident enough to just tackle Limited as soon as they're ready, to see where the game takes them next.

When talking a little more about Limited specifically, Reid would expand further on his thoughts surrounding new players engaging with the format.

"Make no mistake, booster draft is played on the Pro Tour at the very highest level, so it's a challenging, deep, engaging format, but I do really strongly believe that it's fun for players at every level, it's a great way to build your collection, learn the rules, and make friends. I would love to highlight prerelease as a really great entry point for players, especially if it's their first time showing up to an LGS and playing with real opponents, because a prerelease is the definition of an even playing field."

As a father actively teaching his daughter to play Magic, I've found the game can be really good for critical and strategic thinking, but it's also a great way to teach reading comprehension and even math. I asked Reid, as someone who started playing at just five years old, whether there is, or should be, a barrier to entry for Magic directly linked to age.

"No barrier to entry. At this point I've taught hundreds of people magic in my lifetime and I have not met someone of any age or background who could not have something to enjoy or learn from Magic."

It's great to hear that Reid is giving back to the game in the form of teaching new players, but even better to hear that he is also of the belief that, if someone wants to learn how to play, there really is no limit that should be applied when it comes to age or background.

I believe there is a ton of value in Magic as a teaching tool. While I've already mentioned that I'm teaching my daughter to play, she has dyslexia, and that can make reading the cards quite tricky, so I try to use the game to aid her reading comprehension and support her already impressive math skills. Granted, this gets much harder when she starts sending an army of goblins to kill me via the Krenko deck she plays with, but that's a small price to pay for the potential upside of engaging her brain with the game.

One final question I had for Reid about new players was around the color pie, and again I pulled from my own experiences with Magic: when I first started playing, I always drifted toward Dimir (black-blue) decks. This was partly because it was the first deck I ever built, the first deck that got me to Mythic on Arena, and the first deck guide I ever wrote on a personal blog.

Over time, I've had to work hard to pull myself away from Dimir decks as a preference in Limited and Standard because it sometimes felt like I was forcing an archetype that wasn't powerful in the moment.

So, I asked Reid whether he has ever struggled with the same issue of color preference, and what his advice would be for new players entering the game.

"Everyone has a preference, one of the great things about Magic is that you can express yourself creatively in deckbuilding and the cards that you choose. My favorite colors are black and green. On the other hand, some of my biggest level up moments as a competitive player and some of my richest experiences in terms of having fun with the game, have been when my friends and teammates have encouraged me to go outside my comfort zone. As a competitive player, you want to go with whatever strategy you believe is best and will give you the chance to achieve your goals, but for anyone at any level, there's no wrong way to do it. Have your preferences, do what's fun for you, just be willing to challenge yourself and go outside of your comfort zone."

It is almost comforting to hear from such an accomplished player that they still need to be pushed outside their comfort zone, and it probably validates the idea that having a preference within the game is completely healthy, provided you're having fun.
