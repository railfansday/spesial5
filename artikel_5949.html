Hilton Diamond Guest Says He Gets Sent Next Door For Coffee—And Treated Like He Was Stealing Breakfast

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

I’ve written about people walking into hotels where they aren’t staying and helping themselves to a full breakfast. Somehow getting an actual guest a cup of coffee can be more complicated.

John Konrad’s account of the coffee dispute, November 21, 2026

In the second half of the thread, the manager talks up Homewood’s overnight oats. Konrad decides he’d like some after all. He’s refused because he’s registered next door. He raises his voice, objects to being told to calm down (no one in history has ever calmed down because they’ve been told to calm down), and eventually refuses an offered gym pass.

I AM A DIAMOND MEMBER BEING CROSS-EXAMINED OVER CHIA SEEDS.

The replies include plenty of people who think he’s being insufferable. One person who says they’ve stayed at both properties reports that guests routinely move between them. When a critic calls him a Karen, Konrad points back to his Curb Your Enthusiasm framing making himself part of the joke.

The two hotels have shared ownership by the Mian Companies. Signs direct Hilton Garden Inn guests to Homewood’s gym during construction. Homewood Suites Oxnard/Camarillo includes breakfast for its guests. Hilton Garden Inn has a different model. In the United States, eligible Gold and Diamond members who select the benefit receive a $10 daily food and beverage credit per person, for up to two registered guests. And asking for a room number when someone complains about a key is normal. A hotel employee can help with access. But a staff referral should make getting coffee easy. If the two hotels haven’t agreed on what guests can use, they need to sort that out between themselves. Meanwhile, People Really Do Walk Into Hotels For Free Breakfast I’ve covered people walking into a Drury Inn just to eat and people stopping at hotels for breakfast on their way to work. At many limited service properties, nobody checks. Looking comfortable in the lobby can be enough. At the aloft Dallas Love Field in 2022, I selected breakfast as my Marriott elite welcome amenity instead of 500 points. The Aloft and Element occupied the same building, with separate entrances. Breakfast meant walking over to the Element’s lobby buffet, which was included for guests staying there. Nobody asked my name, room number, or whether I’d selected breakfast. I could have taken the points and eaten anyway. Someone who wasn’t staying at either hotel could have walked in just as easily. This favors people who look like they belong. Konrad adds that he hadn’t shaved and his clothes were stained from boat work. He thinks that may explain some of the suspicion. There are real costs to feeding people who aren’t guests. There are also costs to policing breakfast: staff time, lines, and embarrassing the customers who are supposed to be there. A hotel can spend more trying to prevent a small loss than the food would have cost, especially if it chases away repeat business. Hotels Keep Getting Coffee Wrong I’ve been writing about the importance of hotel coffee for years. A business hotel ought to have drinkable coffee available around the clock, with real milk or cream. Guests arrive from other time zones, wake up early for flights, and get up before dawn to work. Their day have to start when the restaurant opens. At what was then the W San Diego, I was staying in the Extreme Wow suite. Still on East Coast time, I woke around 4:30 a.m. At about 5 I called the Whatever/Whenever line for coffee. They told me it wasn’t available until 6. They couldn’t provide whatever (coffee) whenever (in the morning). I wound up walking to Starbucks. I’ve also written about: The then-Andaz Wall Street removing its 24 hour lobby coffee, when rooms didn’t have coffee machines and room service wasn’t available around the clock. Hyatt Herald Square’s coffee stand opening 30 minutes late. Park Hyatt Washington offering 24 hour room service, but refusing overnight coffee. There was a Starbucks across the street there, too. And I don’t love Starbucks, they’re simply ubiquitous. And then there’s the time the Sheraton Macao investigated me for trying to steal coffee. I ordered six cups from room service. After pouring two, the pot was nearly empty. I called down, and they agreed to send four more. Instead, at 6:30 a.m. the delivery employee appeared at my door and announced: I am here to investigate. He insisted the machine was electronic, so it couldn’t have dispensed too little. Then he poured out what remained. Half a cup. There I was in my bathrobe, establishing that six cups and two and a half cups weren’t the same thing before he’d hand over the replacement coffee he’d already brought upstairs. Starwood apologized and offered 5,000 Starpoints. I declined. The hotel’s in-room dining manager apologized as well. They’d turned a short pot of coffee into a staff investigation, a corporate response, and an offer of points. Bringing the coffee would have been cheaper.
