Why The Future Of Materials May Be Biology-Enabled

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Kim Thompson, Founder and CEO, Kraig Biocraft Laboratories.

​For much of the past century, materials innovation has been driven by chemistry, engineering and increasingly sophisticated manufacturing. Those disciplines will continue to shape what we build. But another field is becoming an important part of the conversation: biology.

Advances in genetic engineering and biotechnology are giving us new ways to produce fibers, proteins and other materials using living systems. In some cases, the goal is to recreate properties already found in nature. In others, it is to take what biology does well and use it as the starting point for something entirely new.

This shift is becoming increasingly important as industries look for materials that deliver better performance while reducing dependence on petrochemical-heavy production. Several forces are driving that shift at once. Manufacturers are being asked to improve durability, strength, weight and functionality, while also considering supply chain resilience, resource use and the long-term environmental impact of the materials they choose.

Biology-enabled approaches will not solve every one of those challenges, but they give engineers and product developers a broader set of options than they have had in the past.

Nature has had millions of years to develop structures with unusual combinations of properties. Spider silk is an obvious example because of its combination of strength, toughness and flexibility. Wood provides strength with relatively low weight and low cost. Bone is both rigid and dynamic. Shells achieve remarkable durability through layered structures built from relatively simple components. These examples matter not because we should copy them, but because they show what biological systems can create.

Until recently, our ability to translate those capabilities into useful products was limited. A naturally occurring substance might be difficult to harvest, available only in small quantities or produced by an organism that cannot realistically become part of an industrial supply chain. Biotechnology gives us a different way to approach that challenge. Instead of asking how we can extract more of a material from nature, researchers can increasingly ask what genetic information gives the material its desired properties and whether that information can be adapted to a more practical production system.

The sustainability case for biology-enabled materials gets a great deal of attention, particularly as manufacturers look for alternatives to petroleum-derived fibers and other traditional inputs. That matters, but sustainability alone will not determine which technologies succeed. Economics and performance will.

A new fiber or advanced material has to do something useful. It may need to be lighter, stronger, more flexible, more durable or capable of performing under conditions where conventional alternatives fall short. It also needs to be made consistently enough that manufacturers and customers can rely on it. That creates possibilities well beyond consumer products.

Technical textiles, protective equipment, transportation, aerospace and industrial applications all place demanding requirements on the materials they use. Even relatively small improvements in weight, strength or durability can have a meaningful impact in those settings. “Authored biology” will give developers another way to pursue those improvements. Authored biology is an approach in biotechnology and genetic engineering that enhances existing, time-tested natural systems, proteins and genetic structures rather than building synthetic biological components entirely from scratch.

One of the most interesting possibilities is that biology may allow us to think about materials less as fixed substances and more as platforms that can be tuned. Traditional material development often begins with an existing substance and asks how its properties can be modified through processing, additives or changes in structure. Bioengineering adds another layer.

As the biological instructions responsible for a protein or fiber are understood, it can become possible to adjust characteristics much earlier in the process. Rather than accepting the properties nature provides, scientists may, and in some cases are able to, influence those properties at their source. We are still early in that evolution, and there is a considerable difference between what is theoretically possible and what can be currently manufactured economically at scale. Even so, the potential is significant. Advances in molecular biology can allow developers to design materials around a particular use case rather than search for an existing material that is close enough.

There is also an important reality that anyone working with emerging materials has to acknowledge: Novelty does not guarantee adoption.

Manufacturers operate within established supply chains, equipment, standards and cost structures. A material can be scientifically impressive and still struggle commercially if it requires customers to redesign too much of what they already do. The technologies most likely to gain early traction will be those that combine meaningful performance advantages with a realistic path into existing markets.

That means thinking beyond the material itself. How will it be processed? Can it work with existing manufacturing equipment? Is supply reliable? Can quality be maintained? Does the cost make sense for the intended application? Those questions may sound less exciting than the underlying science, but they are ultimately what turn a promising technology into something useful.

Biology-enabled materials do not need to replace conventional fibers, polymers or composites to have a meaningful impact. Their importance may come from expanding the range of choices available to engineers and product designers.

Chemistry gave us synthetic materials that transformed entire industries. Advances in composites created new combinations of weight and strength. Biology now adds another set of tools. The most exciting outcome may not be recreating what already exists in nature, but rather using biological systems to develop materials with properties and applications we have not yet considered.

While the future of materials will certainly still involve chemistry, physics and traditional manufacturing, a new door is opening. Authored biology will supply options and opportunities. Those options are just now beginning to open up.​
