Resigned vs. fired: 6 differences that decide a CEO's exit pay

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Free daily briefing on global business news.

CEO severance often hinges on whether a chief executive resigned or was fired, and these six differences shape cash pay, unvested equity, option deadlines and exit terms

A company's announcement of a CEO departure often uses neutral wording such as "stepping down." Behind that phrase sits a legal classification that can decide how much money changes hands. The same departure can be worth very different amounts. The result depends on whether the paperwork records a resignation, a termination without cause or a termination for cause.

The rules behind that classification are usually set long before anyone clears out an office. Boards and executives typically negotiate the terms when the CEO is hired or promoted. The employment agreement defines what counts as cause and what counts as good reason. The equity plan and individual grant agreements spell out what happens to stock awards at each kind of exit. By the time a departure arrives, much of the outcome is already written down.

That is why the definitions in a contract matter as much as the dollar figures when CEO severance is calculated. A resignation usually leaves an executive with less than a firing without cause. A firing for cause can leave even less. A resignation for good reason, as the contract defines it, is often treated much like a no-cause termination. Each label points to a different set of contract provisions.

The six differences below are a selection, not a complete list, and they reflect common U.S. practice. Every outcome depends on the specific employment agreement, plan documents and grant terms. A provision that appears in one executive's contract may be missing from another's.

The list starts with cash severance and the role of termination without cause. Next come good reason clauses, which can let an executive quit and still collect a package. A third difference is what a for-cause finding can erase. The list then turns to unvested stock options and restricted stock units, which often carry the most value. It covers the short windows for exercising options after a departure. It closes with the conditions attached to an exit. Those include the release of legal claims and restrictive covenants such as non-competes. They also include the Form 8-K disclosure a public company must file and the clawback rules that apply however a CEO leaves.

Together, these provisions explain how two departures that read alike in a headline can end on very different financial terms.

A CEO's employment agreement usually promises cash severance only if the board ends the job without cause. A chief executive who resigns voluntarily usually walks away with no severance payment at all.

That one distinction often decides whether a departing CEO leaves with a cash package or with only the pay already earned. Severance is a contractual benefit. U.S. federal law does not require employers to provide it. A CEO's right to severance comes from the employment agreement or from a company severance plan.

A termination without cause is a firing in which the board does not allege the kind of misconduct the contract defines as cause. A board might remove a CEO over results, strategy or a wish for new leadership. Under a typical agreement, that kind of exit triggers the severance promise.

The payment is often calculated as a multiple of the CEO's base salary and annual bonus. The multiple is negotiated, and it differs from one agreement to the next. Two CEOs with identical salaries can be owed very different sums when they are let go.

A voluntary resignation sits on the other side of the line. A CEO may leave to take another job, to retire or for personal reasons. The agreement typically treats that decision as the executive's own. The company usually owes salary through the final day of work and any benefits already earned, but no severance.

The structure gives the board room to act. It can replace a CEO without building a case for misconduct. The severance terms, set out in advance, establish what that decision will cost the company. For the executive, the same terms offer financial protection against being pushed out of the job.

Generous exit packages of this kind are often described with the informal label golden parachute. The amount a CEO dismissed without cause is actually owed depends on the wording of that executive's agreement, including how it defines cause and which payments it lists.

A CEO who resigns for good reason, as the employment agreement defines it, typically receives the same exit package as a chief executive the board terminated without cause. The clause turns a departure that looks voluntary into one that is paid like a firing.

The clause addresses a question of leverage. A board might want a CEO gone without paying severance. Without a good reason clause, it could make the job worse rather than end it. The executive would then have to choose between staying in a diminished role and quitting without severance. Good reason language closes that route by treating certain company actions as the equivalent of a dismissal.

Many agreements spell out what qualifies. Common examples include a material cut in pay, a material reduction in duties or a forced relocation. The word "material" carries weight in these clauses. The contract's terms, not the executive's view of the change, set where that line sits.

The triggers are negotiated one agreement at a time. No single list applies across companies. Two CEOs running similar businesses can hold very different protections. Some contracts define good reason broadly. Others limit it to a short set of specific events.

The clause usually comes with a procedure as well as a trigger. A CEO typically must give the company notice that identifies the condition said to amount to good reason. The company then gets a cure period — a window to reverse the pay cut, restore the duties or drop the relocation. Only if the company fails to fix the problem can the CEO resign and claim good reason treatment.

The cure period gives the board a chance to undo a change before it becomes a severance obligation. It also means a CEO who skips the notice step, or leaves before the window closes, risks being treated as having resigned voluntarily.

A single resignation letter can lead to a full severance package or to none. The result typically depends on whether a good reason condition in the contract was met. It also turns on whether the executive followed the notice and cure steps.

A CEO fired for cause typically leaves without severance, and the finding can also cost the executive other awards. Cause is a defined term in the employment agreement. Whether a firing qualifies depends on the contract's wording, not on how the board or the press describes the exit.

Cause definitions commonly cover fraud, a felony conviction, willful misconduct and a material breach of the agreement. The exact list is negotiated with each executive. The final version can be broader or narrower than that core set.

Small drafting choices matter. A clause that requires misconduct to be "willful" sets a higher bar than one that covers any misconduct. A clause tied to a felony conviction does not reach an indictment or an investigation that has not ended in a conviction. A company that wants more room to act typically pushes for broader language. A CEO candidate with bargaining power typically pushes for narrower language.

Weak performance is often left out of the definition. A board can still remove a CEO over poor results. That exit usually counts as a termination without cause. The executive then typically receives the severance the contract promises.

The label decides the money. A termination without cause typically triggers the package the agreement promises. A cause finding typically shuts that package off. Depending on the plan and award terms, it can also mean the loss of other compensation the executive would otherwise have kept.

The board's finding is not the last word. A CEO can dispute whether the conduct meets the contract's definition. Some executive agreements send such disputes to arbitration rather than court. The forum depends on the contract.

A dispute does not reopen every fact of the departure. It turns on a narrower question: whether the specific events fit the specific words the two sides agreed to. Because each definition is negotiated, the same conduct can count as cause under one CEO's contract and fall outside it under another's.

A CEO who resigns typically forfeits unvested stock options and restricted stock units, known as RSUs. A termination without cause often brings accelerated or continued vesting instead, depending on the award terms.

The gap comes from how equity grants are built. Boards usually award options or RSUs that vest over several years. Until an award vests, the executive does not own it outright. The grant works as a promise that depends on the executive staying with the company.

A stock option gives the holder the right to buy shares at a fixed price, called the exercise or strike price. An RSU is a promise to deliver shares, or sometimes their cash value, once vesting conditions are met. Both tie an executive's reward to staying with the company. Both can also disappear if the executive leaves before the vesting date.

Equity forfeiture on resignation follows from that design. When a CEO walks away voluntarily, the plan typically treats the unvested portion as unearned. Those awards go back to the company. Vested awards are a separate matter. They generally belong to the executive, subject to the plan's rules on exercising options.

A termination without cause often produces a different result. Grant agreements frequently soften the loss when the board, rather than the executive, ends the relationship. Accelerated vesting means some or all of the unvested awards vest at the moment of departure. Continued vesting means the awards keep vesting on their original schedule, as if the executive were still employed. Some agreements use one approach, some use the other and some combine them.

Performance-based awards add another layer. Many CEO grants vest only if the company hits set targets over a multi-year period. Agreements often spell out separately how those awards are handled after a no-cause exit.

The controlling terms usually sit in more than one document. The equity plan sets the general rules. Each grant agreement can set terms for that specific award. An employment agreement may also address what happens to equity on departure.

Grants made in different years can carry different terms. A single CEO can therefore face several outcomes at once, with some awards accelerating, others continuing and others lapsing. For a CEO whose pay leans heavily on stock, those award-by-award terms can determine how much unvested value survives a departure.

A departing CEO typically has a short, fixed period to exercise vested stock options before they lapse. Under U.S. tax law, an incentive stock option must be exercised within three months after employment ends to keep its incentive stock option treatment.

That three-month limit comes from the Internal Revenue Code's rules for incentive stock options, often called ISOs. Missing it does not by itself cancel the option. The option instead loses its favored status. If the plan still allows a later exercise, the option is taxed as a nonqualified stock option.

That change affects how the gain is taxed. With a nonqualified option, the spread between the exercise price and the stock's market value at exercise is taxed as ordinary income. With an ISO, exercise does not create ordinary income at that point. The spread can still count toward the alternative minimum tax.

Many company plans set a post-termination exercise window of similar length, whatever the tax label on the option. Once that window closes, unexercised vested options are typically forfeited. The clock usually starts on the date employment ends, not the date the departure is announced.

How the CEO left can change the length of that window. A for-cause termination can cut it to nothing. In that case, vested options can lapse when employment ends. Some employment or grant agreements allow a longer exercise period after a termination without cause. That extension is contract-specific, and there is no standard length across companies.

A longer window cannot normally run past the option's original expiration date. ISOs generally carry a maximum term of 10 years under the tax code. Plans typically state that a post-departure window ends on the earlier of its own deadline or the option's expiration.

The window also raises a cash question. Exercising an option means paying the exercise price, plus any tax due. Public companies often let executives use a broker-assisted cashless exercise, selling some of the shares to cover those costs. Whether that route is available depends on the plan.

Disability is handled differently. The tax code extends the ISO deadline to one year when employment ends because of permanent and total disability.

A departing CEO's severance is typically paid only after the executive signs a release of legal claims against the company. Several other obligations outlast the job however it ends, including restrictive covenants, a public disclosure filing and federal clawback rules.

The release is the condition attached to the money. By signing it, the CEO usually gives up the right to sue the company over the departure and the events leading up to it. In return, the company gets assurance that the payout settles the matter. A CEO who declines to sign typically forfeits the severance the agreement would otherwise provide.

Restrictive covenants work differently. Non-compete, non-solicit and confidentiality obligations are often written into the employment agreement or the equity award documents. They can continue to bind a CEO who resigned in the same way they bind one who was fired. A non-compete restricts where the former executive can work. A non-solicit limits recruiting former colleagues or approaching the company's customers. Confidentiality terms protect internal information after the CEO has left.

The Federal Trade Commission issued a rule in 2024 that targeted non-compete agreements. A federal court in Texas set that rule aside in August 2024. The covenant language in a CEO's own contract remains the document that defines what the executive can and cannot do after leaving.

Public companies also have to tell investors. A public company must report its CEO's departure on Form 8-K under Item 5.02, generally within four business days. That filing becomes part of the public record. The text of the announcement is often negotiated as part of the exit. One common point of negotiation is whether the company says the CEO is stepping down or was terminated.

Clawbacks are one area where the resigned-or-fired label makes no difference. SEC Rule 10D-1 and the matching NYSE and Nasdaq $NDAQ listing standards took effect on October 2, 2023. They require listed companies to recover incentive pay that was erroneously awarded, as shown by an accounting restatement. The rules reach both current and former executive officers. They apply regardless of fault. A CEO who left voluntarily and one who was dismissed face the same recovery obligation.
