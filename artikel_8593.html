Paid work after you retire can pay off in better health, a sharper memory, and more people to confide in, not just a paycheck. Choose part-time or seasonal work you enjoy at hours you set, and check how the job affects your Social Security, taxes, and Medicare before you start.

Work after retirement usually comes up as a fix for thin savings, so it’s easy to rule out once your savings are enough.

Retirees who took part-time, temporary, or self-employed work had fewer major diseases and fewer limits on daily activities than those who stopped completely, in a Journal of Occupational Health Psychology study that tracked 12,189 Americans. In the Whitehall II study of 3,433 British civil servants, memory for words declined 38% faster after people retired than while they still worked. And the circle of people older adults confide in grew while they kept working and shrank once they retired, in research published by Boston College’s Center for Retirement Research.

The paycheck covers costs your savings would otherwise fund, so more of your savings stay invested. It can also let you wait to claim Social Security, which raises your monthly check for each year you wait, up to age 70.

Pick from the jobs that suit retirees by what you’d enjoy, whether that’s part-time hours with your former employer, consulting in your field, a seasonal job, work in a new field, or a small business.
