I am glad I moved my servers to AlmaLinux. It has been working out really well over the last few years. They were also fast to offer mitigation information and updated packages for each of the high-profile vulnerabilities this year.

If you are worried about what level your processor supports just run this:

$ /lib64/ld-linux-x86-64.so.2 --help | grep -A4 'glibc-hwcaps directories'

Look for the word "supported" on each level. Mine shows this on my large Xeon 6254 (Alma) servers:

x86-64-v4 (supported, searched)     x86-64-v3 (supported, searched)     x86-64-v2 (supported, searched)

At home on my AMD Ryzen 7 3700X desktop (Mint) I get:

x86-64-v4     x86-64-v3 (supported, searched)     x86-64-v2 (supported, searched)

So I have v3 support, but not v4. Hopefully that will be good enough for a long time coming...

RHEL is not open-source anymore? How can that even happen?

It is, but basically only in the barest most minimally technically legal sense.

Only paying customers have access, and of course they can choose who gets to be a paying customer. Once you get access though, it still is open source, you can still publish derivative works and all. This is technically still GPL compliant and has at least held up for three years now.

In real short, the goal was to cut off Oracle, but they went pretty scorched-earth about it.

This was discussed extensively over the last few years.

Disclaimer: lengthy mansplaning post follows for the one or two slashdotters interested but who haven't followed this long-running controversy.

Until a few years ago, RH had a public git repository of their rpm spec files, and probably also their patch sets (can't quite remember). Fairly abruptly they shut down that public repository saying that the repo and folks using it to build RHEL clones didn't provide anything of value to the community (really passive aggressive). This caught a lot of people by surprise and it really was a poke in the eye of the community.

But going back even further, Red Hat's support contracts have always caused heartburn for free software supporters, particularly with regards to the GPL. A RH support contract grants access to SRPM files for the entire distro to their customers, along with of course the actual RPMS. But the wording of the support contract says you cannot use those files on non-supported installations. Meaning they don't want you to pay for one server and then use the downloaded files to update your whole cluster, even though the source code license, particularly in the case of the GPL, explicitly grants such permission. Many free software advocates have complained that although RH abides by the GPL technically, they are using contract law to end run around it.

So far as I know, no actual customers of RH have ever been threatened with contract cancellations over this, and to this day Rocky Linux and Oracle both use these RH-downloaded SRPMs to build their distros. I'm not sure to what extend RH actively pursues the accounts used to download by Rocky and Oracle.

RH's defense is that the upstream CentOS Stream system is public and free to use by the community to build on. This is what Alma Linux does and it works very well while maintaining compatibility with RHEL. The only downside is it's harder convert an existing install between RHEL and AlmaLinux. Back in the old CentOS days it was easy. I'd bring up a server on CentOS, test it in beta for a few months/year, then before bringing it into production I'd finally roll it over into a supported RHEL instance.

I recall an interview with a RH manager on a podcast a couple of years ago when the public RPM spec git repo controversy blew up. He maintained that the RH clones offered nothing of any value to the community. He said that the upstream CentOS Stream system was the public community and that if people want to take from CentOS Stream and create something truly unique and valuable they were free to do so. Alma Linux called his bluff and did just that, and it's worked out great for them. RH is still smarting over it I think.

While it's true RH offers hobbyists free-with-sign-up access to RHEL and its packages (probably how Rocky Linux and Oracle build their distros), for those who want an RHEL-type distro in their home lab, there's zero reason not to use Alma Linux.

Since 2023 I've seen AlmaLinux release patches, well ahead of RHEL itself, when several news-making exploits reared their ugly heads. They're inarguably offering significant added value to the community.

Yes RH engineers do great work and they deserve to get paid. Of course they also benefiting from the development and testing of the upstream projects, most of whom are doing it for no compensation at all. And RH does support a lot of upstream projects. Meanwhile Alma Linux is not competition for RHEL at all, and they are providing their own value to the community. In fact they contribute back to stream (also with testing) which RH benefits from.
