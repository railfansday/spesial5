The glymphatic system contains glial cells, non-electrical cells in the lymphatic system that protect and cleanse. In 2012, a team of researchers (Maiken Nedergaard and others) proposed that this system primarily clears waste and is most active during deep sleep. (also called delta-wave sleep, a type of non-REM sleep). The glymphatic system is mostly made up of cerebrospinal fluid that clears waste such as amyloid-beta plaques and tau proteins. These waste particles are commonly found in clusters in the brains and other organs of people with neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Huntington’s disease, as well as mood disorders (Zhang and colleagues, 2022).

Plaque clearance happens twice as fast at night when you sleep well. That's the good news. The bad news is that if your sleep is disturbed, waste is not cleared as efficiently. The natural ebb and flow of fluid is interrupted, and more waste particles accumulate.

Since this clearing occurs primarily in deep (delta-wave) sleep and not the other sleep stages, quality sleep is more important than quantity for the brain's night cleaning—more sleep does not mean better, and less sleep is not always harmful. The type of brain waves during sleep matters more than the eight hours you’ve heard about. The timing also matters. It is better to have deep (non-dreaming) sleep in several chunks toward the first half of the night. During a 90-minute sleep cycle, your brain dips into shallow sleep, then deep, then to shallow again.

Stop alcohol use. Dr. Qinghua Liu and his team found that even moderate alcohol use reduces cerebrospinal fluid from entering and clearing plaques and proteins (Brain, Behavior, and Immunity, 2020). They also found that chronic moderate alcohol use led to increased activation of astrocytes—star-shaped cells that reactively try to address the extra inflammation from alcohol, thus reducing glymphatic function.

There is also a widespread loss of proteins that can polarize (shift across membranes) to move fluid and waste out. Over time, this loss is very difficult to reverse. Alcohol can clean a lot of things outside of our body, but it does not help cell function inside the brain or body. Stopping alcohol may be the single most important thing to begin cleaning your brain. Other depressant medications, like opioids, when used chronically, may also inhibit the flow and cleaning process of cerebrospinal fluid.
