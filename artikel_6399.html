A farmer in Mexico saw the ground split open in his cornfield, then watched a volcano grow to 1,391 feet tall

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

In 1943, Mexico's Parícutin volcano erupted from a farmer's cornfield, growing rapidly and burying two villages during nine years of activity.

On February 20, 1943, farmer Dionisio Pulido watched a fissure open in his field near the village of Parícutin, in the Mexican state of Michoacán. Smoke rose from the crack, along with a hissing sound and a smell of sulfur.

Within a week, the new cone stood more than 490 feet (150 meters) tall. When the eruption ended in 1952, it had risen 1,391 feet (424 meters) above the cornfield where it began.

Lava buried two villages along the way. Parícutin was the first volcano whose eruption modern scientists observed in detail from its birth, according to the International Union of Geological Sciences (IUGS).

Starting around February 5 to 7, 1943, local witnesses felt about two weeks of earthquake activity before the eruption.

Felipe Cuara Amezcua, then municipal president of San Juan Parangaricutiro, later reported that the tremors grew until more than 200 shook the area in a single day.

On February 20, Pulido went to his farm to prepare the fields for the spring sowing. He had owned the land for 31 years.

Smithsonian mineralogist William F. Foshag and Mexican geologist Jenaro González R. later collected his account and his wife’s. A 1956 U.S. Geological Survey bulletin printed them in English translation.

At about 4 p.m., Pulido left his wife, Paula, and went to burn a pile of branches. She had been hearing underground noise and thunder.

“About 4 p. m., after talking to my husband, I heard a kind of loud whistle,” Paula Pulido recalled.

“I saw that it was a kind of fissure that had only a depth of half a meter,” Pulido said.

He then saw the ground swell by about 6.6 to 8 feet (2 to 2.5 meters), while gray smoke or dust rose from the crack.

“Immediately more smoke began to rise, with a hiss or whistle, loud and continuous,” he recounted.

Frightened, he mounted his mare and galloped to the village where his family was waiting.

Accounts from Pulido and other witnesses gave “the first adequate account of the birth of a new volcano,” Foshag and González wrote.

One of the first photographs of the eruption, taken at 6 p.m. on February 20, shows an ash plume rising from the new vent. By midnight, the cone was about 20 feet (6 meters) high.

On the morning of February 21, a strong earthquake caused panic. People from Parícutin fled to San Juan Parangaricutiro, and many from San Juan went on to Angahuan or Uruapan.

A photograph from February 21 shows the cone about 100 feet (30 meters) tall, just rising above the treetops. After six days of activity, it reached 548 feet (167 meters).

The first lava flow, called the Quitzocho flow, moved north over cornfields prepared for planting. Mexican geologist Ezequiel Ordóñez estimated that the early lava advanced about 10 feet (3 meters) an hour.

Ash fell too. It piled up deeper than 6 inches (15 centimeters) over an area of 116 square miles (300 square kilometers) around the volcano.

When the first phase of activity ended on October 19, 1943, the original vent had built a cone about 1,200 feet (365 meters) high.

Pulido’s home village lay only 1.9 miles (3 kilometers) northwest of the new cone. Its 733 residents had to abandon it for good four months after the eruption began, and lava later flowed over the site.

Starting in January 1944, a new phase sent out the Taquí lava flows, which spread over about 7 square miles (18 square kilometers) by 1945.

In July 1944, lava surrounded the church of San Juan Parangaricutiro. Its tower still stands in the middle of a lava flow, the IUGS notes.

In all, the volcano’s lavas reached up to 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) from the vent and formed a field of about 9.7 square miles (25 square kilometers).

The Mexican government gave displaced residents new land in Caltzontzín, 17 miles (27 kilometers) to the southeast.

Parícutin was “the first well-documented example of an eruption of a monogenetic volcanic cone in historical time,” according to a 1993 USGS film report.

A monogenetic cone forms during a single eruptive episode. Parícutin, active for about nine years, gave scientists an unprecedented chance to follow such a volcano through its full life cycle.

An international group documented the activity in real time. Celedonio Gutiérrez, a resident of San Juan who kept a diary from the first day, worked with U.S. scientists throughout the eruption.

In 1990, volcanologists I. Yokoyama and S. de la Cruz-Reyna analyzed the precursory earthquakes of the 1943 eruption, a type of signal that comes before eruptions.

In 1985, decades after the eruption stopped, a fumarole at Parícutin still measured 883°F (473°C). The cone is one of more than 1,400 vents in the Michoacán-Guanajuato volcanic field.

The information was obtained from the Smithsonian Institution’s Global Volcanism Program.
