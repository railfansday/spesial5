A last minute skeleton discovery finally proved that a Norse church once stood right near Erik the Red’s Greenland settlement

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A skeleton found at Qinngua confirms the first Norse church found in Greenland in over 60 years.

In August 2026, archaeologists at Qinngua, near Narsarsuaq in southern Greenland, reached the second-to-last day of their planned excavation timeline.

The National Museum of Denmark first dug at this particular ruin in the 2000s, and none of the work since then had turned up a burial site. Until that fateful day.

Their skeleton discovery confirms that the stone ruin was a Norse church, the first found in Greenland in more than 60 years. Its date is still provisional, and researchers are analyzing samples from the site.

Qinngua lies close to Brattahlíð, the main settlement of Erik the Red. Researchers from Copenhagen’s National Museum worked there with Bergen’s University Museum, Greenland’s National Museum and Archive and UNESCO Kujataa.

Michael Nielsen of the Greenland museum photographed the dig, and Kujataa site manager Aqqaluaq Frederiksen shot the ruin from above.

The ruin consists of a rectangular stone wall with a stone building in the middle. Researchers had suspected a church there for some time because of that layout, which the University of Bergen calls a typical mark of a medieval church.

According to the National Museum’s history pages, Greenland’s oldest churches were small, stood close to farmhouses and had circular churchyard walls. Farmers built them and owned them.

Proof needed a cemetery with graves. Museum teams dug at Qinngua in the 2000s and found none.

This summer the team located a skeleton placed with its head toward the east, which the museum describes as customary for Christian burials. The grave confirmed a cemetery, and the cemetery confirmed the church.

Christian Edelfelt Koch Madsen coordinates research on the project for the National Museum. He called the grave “the smoking gun.” He said, “Now there is no doubt that this is a church.”

Inuit were buried in stone-covered chambers and Norse people in cemeteries, Madsen said, and the written sources point the same way.

Konrad Smiarowski of the University Museum of Bergen co-leads the project. He said in Bergen’s statement that the Norse dead lay in consecrated ground beside churches.

The end of the dig drew many curious locals from the village of Qassiarsuk, according to a caption in the museum’s press pack.

Medieval texts name several Norse churches and say roughly where they stood. Smiarowski said researchers have placed most of them using those texts, especially Flateyjarbók and Ívar Bárðarson’s description of Greenland, along with landscape analysis and known Norse settlements.

One account mentions Kirkja undir Sólarfjöllum, the Church under the Sunny Mountains. The National Museum said this description fits Qinngua rather well.

The mountains behind the site stand right by the ice cap, under a local high-pressure system that often brings sunny weather. The site is in the part of Tunulliarfik Fjord that the Norse knew as Eiriksfjord.

Madsen expects the match to guide future fieldwork. Because this location agrees with the sources, he said, “it would make sense to try to locate other churches also mentioned in writing.”

Researchers have found six churches in the relatively small area around Brattahlíð, and only three remained in use into the Middle Ages. The ruins of two or three farms stand near the new church, which may suggest the beginnings of a village.

The National Museum’s research page on the Norse says farms with a church were central places in a hierarchic society. Other farmers were probably tied to church owners through kinship, religion or tenancy.

Madsen said the cluster near Erik the Red’s seat looks like “a veritable race to build churches and thus demonstrate power and position.” Some builders may have lost that race and given up on their churches, he said, and Qinngua could be one of them.

Smiarowski described churches as centers of power as well as places of worship. Studying them, he said, can show how Norse society handled climatic and social pressures.

Churchyards have been informative in Denmark too, where 77 Viking graves clustered around the lost St. Oluf’s Church in Aarhus.

The Norse held on in Greenland from about 980 until the 1400s. Written sources say Icelanders led by Erik the Red settled the southwest around 985, and that the Western Settlement emptied in the middle of the 14th century.

The last written evidence of life in the Eastern Settlement dates to 1408. Archaeological finds and radiocarbon dates suggest people stayed there until at least the middle of the 15th century.

A Norwegian king appointed a bishop for Greenland in the early 1100s, the National Museum says, but bishops probably did not reach their post at Gardar until the early 1200s. None lived there after Bishop Alf died in 1378.

The churches found 60 years ago dated to the Viking Age and were soon given up. The Qinngua church is bigger and was likely used through the Middle Ages, a period when knowledge is “still full of holes,” the National Museum said.

Lake sediment work has tied the arrival and departure of the Greenland Norse to shifting temperatures. Hemlock and jack pine from five Norse sites in Greenland also point to timber trips to North America.

The institutions have not published a laboratory date. The range of 1100 to 1300 comes from the church’s large size and its many hewn stones, and the arms laid along the sides suggest the early Middle Ages.

The statements describe one skeleton and give no age or sex. They also give no measurements for the building. The announcement came as a press release, without a peer-reviewed paper.

Neither statement reports an inscription or object that names the church, so the link to Kirkja undir Sólarfjöllum rests on the landscape.

The two possible histories of the building also remain open, since the museum describes likely use through the Middle Ages while Madsen suggests its owners may have abandoned it. “More research will show,” he said.

The grave confirmed on that second-to-last day is the the first burial reported at Qinngua at Qinngua. Samples from the find are still being analyzed.
