Tropical forests are changing in profound ways that are overlooked when simply studying average temperatures

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Decades of weather records reveal more extreme heat in two tropical forests, while rainfall patterns are shifting in different directions.

Tropical rainforests experience relatively little temperature variation through the year.

At two well-studied forests in the Caribbean basin, the average temperature has risen only slowly over the past few decades.

The hottest days, though, have become far more common, and the rain has turned more extreme in a different way at each site.

The plants and animals in these forests are adapted to steady temperatures, the researchers wrote.

So a jump in extreme heat can matter even when the averages hardly move, and at these two forests, the extremes are where the change showed up most clearly.

Alonso Ramírez, a professor in the Department of Applied Ecology at North Carolina State University, led the work. He has a long history with both forests.

Ramírez worked as a field technician at one of the forests before graduate school, and he directed the other for 15 years.

The team pulled decades of daily weather records from two stations. The La Selva Research Station is in a lowland rainforest in Costa Rica.

The El Verde Field Station is a mountain forest site in Puerto Rico and the main base of the Luquillo Long-Term Ecological Research program.

Temperature records ran from 1992 at La Selva and from 2000 at El Verde, and the rain records went back even further.

The average day is about 77°F (25.1°C) at La Selva and about 74°F (23.6°C) at El Verde.

At both sites, the gap between the coolest month and the warmest is less than about 7°F (4°C). In other words, temperatures vary little between seasons.

To find extreme heat, the researchers ranked every day on record at each site and flagged the hottest 5% as hot days.

“In La Selva prior to 2024, you had an average of eight hot days – days where the temperature exceeded the 95th percentile of temperatures at that site – per year,” Ramírez said.

“Now that number is up to 30. These spikes in temperature are becoming more severe and more common.”

El Verde had the same rise. The team got the same result with stricter cutoffs, the hottest 3% and the hottest 1% of days. The hot days themselves also got hotter over time.

Much of the jump came from 2023 and 2024, when temperatures ran high at both forests.

The team estimated that average temperatures at both sites rose by about 0.52°F (0.29°C) per decade.

That’s close to the warming earlier studies have reported across the wider Caribbean.

That estimate depends heavily on the regional figure the researchers gave their model as a starting point, as the authors made clear.

A rise in hot days, they wrote, may not be visible in average temperature records at all.

Total annual rainfall showed no significant trend at either site.

Both forests are very wet, with La Selva averaging about 164 inches (4,166 millimeters) a year and El Verde about 143 inches (3,636 millimeters).

At La Selva, days with more than about 3.7 inches (93 millimeters) of rain rose from around one a year in 1983 through 1985 to six in 2024. Days with no rain at all dropped from 128 in 1983 to 70 in 2024.

More frequent heavy downpours can disturb a forest even when the yearly total stays the same, the authors wrote.

El Verde moved the other way. Its count of heavy rainfall days didn’t change. But days with almost no rain, less than about a twentieth of an inch (1.4 millimeters), rose from 152 to 170 a year.

Marcelo Ardón, a co-author of the study, is a professor in the Department of Forestry and Environmental Resources and the Center for Geospatial Analytics at NC State.

“That was surprising, that one is getting wetter and one is getting drier,” Ardón said.

“Given how close they are to each other, we expected to find similar trends across the two sites. This shows how climate change is happening right now, and we can demonstrate that with data that doesn’t stretch that far back.”

The researchers wrote that the El Verde result supports projections of a drier Caribbean, including Puerto Rico.

Rainforests are adapted to steady rain, they added, and are especially vulnerable to drought.

The team also checked small forest streams. Water in a La Selva stream warmed by about 0.4°F (0.2°C) between 2007 and 2024.

A stream at El Verde warmed by about 1.6°F (0.9°C) between 2006 and 2024.

The La Selva stream is fed partly by groundwater estimated at about 2,500 years old, which the authors suggested may help explain its smaller rise.

The El Verde stream depends less on groundwater and runs through a forest that hurricanes often damage.

Earlier field and lab studies suggest that even small changes in water temperature can change which insects live in a stream and how fast their young develop.

At La Selva, rising temperatures have already been linked to lower forest productivity and to a drop in the number of insect species.

“A lot of people tend to think of the tropics as being stable and unchanging, especially in protected areas like the ones we’re studying,” said Ramírez.

“Conventional wisdom would say that these places should not show much of a change from anthropogenic impacts,” he added.

“Instead, these two sites that are hotspots for research are showing clear changes in climate and in the environment, which then affects the functioning of the ecosystem.”

Two forests are a small sample of a big region. The authors see La Selva and El Verde as typical of Caribbean settings, so similar shifts may be happening across the region, but that hasn’t been tested.

Streams at both sites also spent more time at high water, with the yearly hours at those levels roughly doubling.

The statistics behind that trend were weak, so it’s possible the pattern came up by chance.

The researchers haven’t yet checked whether the warmer temperatures and changing rainfall patterns are changing life in these streams.

The next step is to find out whether the insects and other animals in the streams at both forests are already responding.

The full study was published in the journal PLOS Climate.
