A graduate student’s team found a faint galaxy candidate in Rubin Observatory test data, and hundreds more may still be hiding

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

A Milky Way companion galaxy named Aquarius IV lies about 355,000 light-years away. A Yale-led team found it in Rubin test data covering about 7 percent of the night sky.

On July 27, 2026, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory on Cerro Pachón in Chile released its second batch of test data. Seven days later, Yale graduate student William Cerny and his colleagues posted a paper about the discovery of a Milky Way companion.

They named it Aquarius IV. It sits roughly 355,000 light-years from Earth, an estimate that rests on a single star, and the data behind the find covered about 7 percent of the night sky.

According to Yale University, it is a galaxy. The authors call that classification tentative and do not rule out a globular cluster, because Rubin has so far picked out only a small number of its stars.

The release is called Early Data Preview 2 and covers about 3,000 square degrees. Rubin took those images between April 2025 and January 2026, before its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time started in June 2026.

Cerny worked with 18 co-authors at places including the University of Chicago, Fermilab and the University of Washington.

They pulled stars from the release and kept the blue ones in parts of the sky with little dust. Then they ran an algorithm called simple, which looks for small clumps of stars with the colors of a very old population.

The team assumed an age of 13 billion years and scanned distances in steps. They repeated the search with two pairs of color filters and compared the lists to weed out false alarms.

Aquarius IV ranked highest among the new detections, at 8.0 sigma in one filter pair and 7.6 sigma in the other. Sigma measures how far a signal stands above random noise.

The same run also recovered every known ultra-faint satellite in that area, all three known ultra-faint satellites in that area: Aquarius II, Aquarius III and Virgo III.

Peter Ferguson, a postdoctoral scholar in astronomy at the University of Washington and a co-author, credited the data work behind the find. “Finding something as faint as Aquarius IV comes down to how clean your stellar catalog is,” he said.

After the algorithm flagged the spot, the team opened the stacked Rubin images. They also pulled archival pictures from the Dark Energy Camera. Both sets showed an excess of faint blue stars at the same position.

The researchers then checked candidates from an earlier search of Dark Energy Camera data. Aquarius IV was on that list too, at about 6 sigma.

Yale describes the object as only barely detectable by eye in the Rubin image. The automated search found it at high confidence anyway.

The fit gives Aquarius IV an absolute magnitude of about minus 1.9. Half of its light falls within a radius of about 19 parsecs, or roughly 62 light-years.

Its stars match a population about 13 billion years old and very poor in heavy elements. The paper places it among the faintest known Milky Way satellites beyond about 326,000 light-years.

Ultra-faint dwarf galaxies are the dimmest galaxies known, with a total glow of up to around 100,000 suns. Astronomers study these tiny, ancient systems for clues about the early universe. Yale says objects like Aquarius IV consist almost entirely of dark matter.

Size drives the tentative label. The authors note that its radius exceeds that of nearly all Milky Way globular clusters, the dense balls of old stars that also orbit the galaxy. A similar debate surrounded a very faint star system called UMa3/U1, found orbiting the Milky Way.

A second study from the same preview data reported a star cluster named Rubin-GC1. It lies on the far side of the Milky Way, inside a region thick with cosmic dust.

Aashay Pai of the University of Chicago led that work, with Cerny as second author, and it appeared in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. Marla Geha, a professor of astronomy and physics at Yale, co-authored both papers.

“Finding new Milky Way satellites in Rubin Observatory data has been an exciting future prospect for most of my career,” Geha said.

Yale counts about 65 known satellite galaxies of the Milky Way. Some researchers suspect hundreds more remain undetected, and simulations have pointed to dozens of unseen ghost galaxies around our own.

Rubin’s Simonyi Survey Telescope has a 27.6-foot (8.4-meter) mirror and a 3.2-gigapixel camera, which Yale calls the largest digital camera ever built. Ferguson said the test data covered 15 percent of the area the full survey will map.

The paper expects the survey to catch systems like Aquarius IV with better than 85 percent efficiency. Cerny said the full survey “will undoubtedly provide us with many more Milky Way satellite discoveries.”

The 355,000 light-year figure leans heavily on one star. It is a likely blue horizontal branch star, a type of aging star with a predictable brightness, located 0.8 arcminutes from the object’s center.

A footnote in the paper covers the other case. If the star does not belong to Aquarius IV, distances of about 490,000 to 650,000 light-years may fit better. The team already rejected one bright red giant because Gaia data showed it moving too fast across the sky.

The authors call their measurements preliminary. They say deeper imaging and spectroscopy must settle its properties and its classification.

The paper ran as a four-page note in a journal that the American Astronomical Society says is moderated and not peer-reviewed.

Those follow-up observations will go back to the spot in Aquarius that Cerny’s team reported seven days after the test data went public. Rubin’s main survey has more than nine years left to run.

The full study was published in the journal Research Notes of the AAS.
