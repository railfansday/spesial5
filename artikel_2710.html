Cash Rules Everything: How Vegas Events And Streaming Deals Are Funding $20 Million College Basketball Rosters

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

LAS VEGAS – In today's modern world of college basketball, it's expensive to fund a roster capable of going deep into the NCAA Tournament.

Schools like Duke, Florida, Kentucky, North Carolina, Louisville, Michigan and St. John's are reportedly spending more than $20 million annually in NIL for their basketball teams.

Programs like UConn, Kansas, Indiana and Arkansas are reportedly just under the $20 million mark.

Michigan, Florida, UConn (twice) and Kansas have won the last five national championships.

And one big revenue stream for these programs are the games themselves.

No longer are many of the elite programs flying to November multi-team events (MTEs) in Maui or the Bahamas or Florida on their own dime and paying for everything.

Instead, events here in Sin City like the Players Era and the Duke-UConn game that aired on Thanksgiving eve on Amazon Prime are providing schools with huge amounts of money that they, in turn, use to fund their rosters.

"Look, the old days of playing in an MTE where you basically got a good event, good location, you paid for everything – no one gave you a dime – it's changed significantly for us and we've been able to leverage that," UConn athletic director David Benedict told me while sitting courtside at T-Mobile Arena ahead of the Duke-UConn game, won by the Blue Devils, 77-63, in front of a non-sellout crowd of 8,507.

"And so whether it's playing in an event like this with Duke, or some events that are currently being put together that will be very beneficial for us, we're looking for great opponents in good environments that support our program financially."

Under the terms of Duke's landmark deal with Prime, the school is reportedly getting north of $5 million annually. This year, the Blue Devils will play three games on Prime in three different cities: vs. UConn in Las Vegas, vs. Texas Tech in New York City (Dec. 21) and vs. Gonzaga in Detroit (Feb. 20).

"Well, for us, the primary thing is always about finding unique ways we can do games that maybe other programs can't," Duke coach Jon Scheyer said when I asked how much the Prime agreement benefited his program. "I think that's where it stems from, and obviously in today's day and age, NIL is a part of that, and there's no question."

Benedict confirmed a report from CT Insider that Duke is paying UConn $333,333.33 directly as part of its Prime deal. The two schools share the ticket revenue, with an additional portion going to bdG Sports LLC, the third-party management company that helped facilitate the event.

UConn, which has been to three of the last four national championship games and won back-to-back titles in 2023' and '24 under head coach Dan Hurley, played a game against Michigan on Nov. 6 at TD Garden in Boston as part of the Naismith Hall of Fame series that was a rematch of the NCAA championship game. The game, won by UConn 85-77, drew more than 18,000 fans.

"We own that game," Benedict said of how ticket sales were split. "The Hall of Fame just paid us a certain amount and Michigan a certain amount."

He added: "We've got a $135 million budget and [income from these games] is a part of what we generate. What we spend on basketball is not necessarily just based on our revenues that we create from basketball. There's a lot of different ways that you can generate money that is both directly and indirectly tied to whether it's basketball or other sports.

"The better off we can do at generating money, the better off we're going to be in this new world."

Benedict pointed out that as a member of the Big East Conference, UConn benefits from Fox's TV deal with the league, which runs from the 2025-26 season through the 2030-31 season and is reportedly worth around $80 million. NBC and TNT are new partners in the deal as well.

"I think it comes down to money now," Benedict said. "I think the old days of you wanted to be on this network and that was a big deal….It's more about what people are paying you today and obviously playing great opponents in great venues in good locations."

As for the venue, Hurley wasn't a huge fan simply because the game wasn't sold out and the crowd appeared to be at last 65/35 in favor of Duke.

"It's great for us to be able to play Duke in the regular season," Hurley said. "It would probably be better for it to be home-and-home situations…I promise you, if we played at MSG, it would have been sold out. There would have been 20,000 people, there wouldn't have been sections that were empty.

"Anytime that Duke and UConn could play, it's a big deal. I just think it would be better if we came to Cameron [Indoor Stadium] and they came to Storrs, or if we did it in a place where it would have been sold out, like it deserved to be."

As for the Players Era, it hosted two events this month on the Las Vegas Strip among the bright lights of the casinos where acts like Carrot Top and Journey are playing and not too far from The Sphere, where The Wizard of Oz and the Eagles are playing to sold-out crowds.

The Players Era 8 (won by current AP No. 1 Houston) concluded last week while the Players Era 16 runs Thanksgiving week.

Kansas got "at least $3.1 million in NIL opportunities, travel support and other expenses" in exchange for playing three games at the Players Era 8, according to Sportico.

Per the report, the event provided "$2.75 million to Kansas in NIL money, a $200,000 travel stipend, and $150,000 for lodging, meals and other travel expenses. Additionally, Players Era would provide 150 complimentary tickets and the chance to purchase additional tickets that the school could sell for a profit, minus a 20% commission."

Tournament organizer Seth Berger declined to disclose how much teams in the Players Era 16 were making but CBS Sports reported teams are receiving an average of more than $1 million in NIL compensation, with exact payouts varying by school.

St. John's coach Rick Pitino, a Naismith Hall of Famer who has won two NCAA championships, believes the combination of NIL money and the influx of foreign players has raised the level of college basketball to a point where it hasn't been in decades.

St. John's roster features a number of international players, including two who make more than $10 million combined in Baylor transfer wing Tounde Yessoufou and British point guard Quinn Ellis.

"First, the foreign influence is with the NBA as well as college," Pitino said on The Herd. "I coached in Greece at Panathinaikos in the Euro League, and if you're a basketball player aspiring to go to the Euro League because the NBA is not right in your reach as a foreign player, you'd rather go to college. It almost pays twice more than the Euro League.

"Unless you are the first or second-best player on a Euro League team, the rest of the roster, you can go to college and get paid a lot more money. So college basketball has elevated to a level of great play right now."

Ironically, Pitino will face his old school Louisville on Thanksgiving night for the first time since the school fired him in 2017 after the program was implicated in an FBI investigation into college basketball recruiting bribery and fraud.

In November 2022, Pitino was exonerated by the Independent Accountability Resolution Process (IARP) in the Louisville infractions case. That cleared the way for him to take the job at St. John's, a Catholic school with deep religious convictions, in 2023.

"I would bring St. John's to the elite level not seen since Louie [Carnesecca] roamed the sidelines," Pitino told me by text from Greece in 2019.

Pitino's firing came after several NCAA Division I assistant basketball coaches were caught on hidden FBI surveillance video accepting cash bribes in a Las Vegas hotel suite in July 2017. One of those coaches, Book Richardson of Arizona, served 90 days in federal prison.

Now, seven years after Richardson was released from prison, paying players is completely legal and is part and parcel of the high-stakes college basketball world on display this week in Las Vegas.

"Whether it's right, wrong or indifferent," Benedict said, "…you can't create the money unless you're playing in these big venues in big locations."
