10 Best GameCube JRPGs That You Need to Play in Fall 2026

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Contributor at DualShockers, former Esports Insider DACH content lead, former Escapist Magazine Germany content lead, software engineering background.

There’s always a specific moment every year where I realise that autumn has properly arrived. The evenings start drawing in before I’ve even had the chance to notice, the air gets crisper, and my coffee suddenly needs to be hot enough to warm both hands around the mug. I don’t think of myself as someone who hibernates, but I’d be lying if I said I didn’t naturally start staying in a little more this time of year.

And honestly? I love it. Being cozied up on my sofa with a blanket, a hot drink and the rain doing its thing outside is one of my favourite feelings, and it always seems to pull me towards games that tug on my nostalgia heartstrings. More often than not, that means the GameCube. Its JRPG library was smaller than what you’d find on its competitors, but it was stranger, braver and far more charming than it ever got credit for.

So many GameCube games have managed to escape the console in some way, but there are some JRPGs still waiting to abandon ship.

Some of these games have since found their way onto modern hardware, whilst others are still very much living in 2004, waiting for you to dig out an old memory card. A couple of them are certified classics, a few are delightfully weird, and at least one asks you to keep a close eye on the expiry date of your bananas. So grab a blanket, put the kettle on, and let’s take a look at the ten GameCube JRPGs that deserve a spot in your autumn rotation.

Tales of Symphonia was a big deal for GameCube owners. Released in Japan in August 2003, before reaching North America in July 2004 and Europe that November, it was the first Tales game to make the jump into 3D, complete with gorgeous cel-shaded visuals and character designs by Kosuke Fujishima. You follow Lloyd Irving and his childhood friend Colette, the Chosen One, on a journey to regenerate the dying world of Sylvarant. Naturally, things turn out to be a lot more complicated than that.

What made it really special, though, was the multiplayer. Up to four people could plug in a controller and take over a party member in the real-time battles, even if the camera stubbornly stuck to player one, leaving everyone else to fend for themselves off-screen. Add in the optional skits you could trigger with the Z button and a soundtrack by Motoi Sakuraba, and you have a game that will happily eat 80 hours of your autumn. If digging out the original hardware isn’t for you, the 2023 remaster on Switch, PS4 and Xbox One is the easier way in.

Taking a franchise famous for its tight, side-scrolling action and turning it into a turn-based RPG sounds like a recipe for disaster. Capcom did it anyway in 2004, and the result is a wonderfully fun little oddity that also found its way onto the PlayStation 2. Set in the 23rd century, X, Zero and newcomer Shadow are sent to Giga City to put an end to a Reploid rebellion led by Epsilon, with everything revolving around a powerful new substance called Force Metal.

Battles are snappy, and Hyper Modes, which let your characters transform mid-fight for a temporary power boost, keep things from ever getting stale. Axl joins the fold along the way, as does a slick bounty hunter called Spider, and the cast as a whole has a lot more personality than I expected going in. It received a fairly average critical response at the time, but it sold well, and I think it has aged far better than its reputation suggests.

Baten Kaitos was developed by Monolith Soft and tri-Crescendo, published by Namco, and released in Japan in December 2003, before making its way west across 2004 and 2005. Its world is made up of islands floating high in the sky, with the oceans below reduced to nothing more than legend. Fittingly, the name comes from the Arabic for “belly of the whale”, and it’s also the name of a star in the constellation Cetus. You don’t play as the protagonist, Kalas, but as a Guardian Spirit bonded to him, and he will very happily talk to you directly.

Everything revolves around Magnus, cards that capture the essence of objects, and with over 1,000 of them, deck building gets intricate fast. The fun part? Many Magnus change in real time. Grapes go rotten, then turn into wine, then vinegar, and a perfectly good banana will eventually turn on you if you leave it sitting in your deck. The 2023 HD Remaster on Switch bundles it with its prequel, Baten Kaitos Origins, which is a lovely excuse to finally experience both.

Path of Radiance launched in 2005 as the ninth main entry in the series, but only the third to make it to the West. It was the first Fire Emblem on a home console since Thracia 776, and it brought a few firsts along with it: 3D maps, voice acting and full motion cutscenes. You take control of Ike, a young mercenary who ends up at the centre of a war on the continent of Tellius, where the human beorc and the half-human laguz share a long, complicated history of mistrust.

Ike went on to become one of the series’ most beloved characters, joining the roster of Super Smash Bros. Brawl and later topping the men’s side of the very first Choose Your Legends poll in Fire Emblem Heroes. Permadeath is, of course, still very much a thing, so be prepared for heartbreak if you get careless. Players with save data from the Game Boy Advance Fire Emblem games could even link up to unlock concept art and special maps, which is the kind of connectivity I often find myself missing nowadays.

For years, a physical copy cost an arm and a leg. Thankfully, it joined the GameCube library on Nintendo Switch Online + Expansion Pack in January 2026, so if you own a Switch 2, there has never been an easier time to sign up with the Greil Mercenaries. And should you get hooked, Radiant Dawn on the Wii picks up the story afterwards.

Skies of Arcadia first set sail on the Dreamcast in 2000, but for many of us, Legends on the GameCube was where we first met Vyse, Aika and Fina. You play as a Blue Rogue, essentially a sky pirate with a moral compass, in a world of floating continents orbited by six moons. The Valuan Empire is hunting down the Moon Crystals, and it falls to you and your crew to stop them, mostly by being extremely charming about it.

Exploration is at the heart of everything. Sailing into the unknown, stumbling across Discoveries and selling the information for a reward is ridiculously satisfying, and Legends bumped the total up to 89. The port also added Moonfish to hunt down and a bounty hunter named Piastol, whose side story finally explains how Vyse got that scar on his face. Crucially, it also toned down the random encounter rate, which anyone who survived the Dreamcast version will deeply appreciate.

If you're looking for some rough times on the Gamecube, here are 8 hard to beat Gamecube JRPGs.

Long before Demon’s Souls, FromSoftware made a card-based action RPG for the GameCube. Known as Rune in Japan, Lost Kingdoms released there in April 2002, with Activision publishing it in North America and Europe later that year. It was one of the very first JRPGs on the GameCube, and it shows in the best possible way: it’s a little rough, a little ambitious, and absolutely its own thing.

You play as Princess Katia, tasked with saving the five kingdoms of Argwyll from a mysterious fog that is swallowing the land. Instead of swinging a sword, you build a deck of cards, each one summoning a creature or weapon in real time, which makes every encounter feel like a frantic little puzzle. There’s even a two-player mode where you can pit your deck against a friend’s.

Reviews were average at the time, but it did well enough to get a sequel, Lost Kingdoms II, set 200 years later. Looking back now, it’s fascinating to see the studio already leaning into melancholy, fog-drenched fantasy a whole seven years before it would change everything. It’s not the most polished game on this list, but it might just be the most interesting.

The Thousand-Year Door released in 2004 and is still, for many, the high point of the Paper Mario series. After Princess Peach sends Mario a treasure map and promptly goes missing, he ends up in Rogueport, a rather grimy town sitting on top of a legendary door, and sets out to find the seven Crystal Stars. Along the way, you’ll pick up a wonderful cast of partners, from know-it-all Goombella to Admiral Bobbery, a retired sailor who just so happens to be a Bob-omb.

Battles take place on a literal stage in front of an audience, who can cheer you on, throw items at you or bring the set crashing down. Every chapter feels like a different genre, whether that’s climbing the ranks in the Glitz Pit wrestling arena or solving a whodunnit aboard the Excess Express. Meanwhile, Peach is on her own little adventure with a computer called TEC-XX, and the interludes where you take control of Bowser are pure comedic gold. If you’d rather not hunt down a GameCube copy, the 2024 Switch remake is a gorgeous way to play.

Pokémon Colosseum turned the formula on its head. There are no wild Pokémon to catch, no gyms to conquer, and instead of a wide-eyed kid, you play as Wes, a former member of Team Snagem who kicks off the game by blowing up their hideout. Set in the desert region of Orre, you use a Snag Machine to steal Shadow Pokémon, whose hearts have been closed off by Team Cipher, straight from other trainers.

Every battle is a double battle, you start with Espeon and Umbreon, and slowly purifying your Shadow Pokémon by fighting alongside them is a surprisingly heartfelt gameplay loop. In North America, pre-ordering the game got you a bonus disc that let you send a Jirachi to your copy of Ruby or Sapphire, which also fixed the infamous berry glitch. Europe, sadly, didn’t get one.

It sold 2.41 million copies worldwide, and its sequel, Pokémon XD: Gale of Darkness, joined the Switch 2’s GameCube library earlier this year. Colosseum was announced for the same service back in April 2025, and as of writing, we are still waiting for a release date. Patience is a virtue, or so they keep telling me.

Evolution Worlds is a curious one. Developed by Sting and published by Ubisoft outside of Japan, it arrived in 2002 as a compilation of two Dreamcast games, packing an abbreviated version of Evolution: The World of Sacred Device and the full Evolution 2: Far Off Promise onto one GameCube disc. Both games also received newly recorded English voice acting.

You play as Mag Launcher, a young adventurer exploring ancient ruins with a Cyframe, a powerful tool left behind by a long-lost civilisation. At its core, this is a turn-based dungeon crawler, and a genuinely charming one at that. It also has some of the most gloriously unhinged character names I’ve ever come across: Mag is joined by Linear Cannon, Chain Gun, Pepper Spray and his ever-loyal butler, Gre Nade.

I won’t pretend it’s the deepest game on this list. It really isn’t. But sometimes all you want on a rainy evening is a straightforward dungeon crawl with a lot of heart, and Evolution Worlds delivers exactly that. It’s comfort food in JRPG form, and there’s absolutely nothing wrong with that.

Final Fantasy Crystal Chronicles was a big moment, being the first Final Fantasy on a Nintendo home console since Final Fantasy VI back in 1994. Its world is covered in a poisonous miasma, and every village relies on a crystal to keep it at bay. Once a year, each village sends out a caravan to collect myrrh from distant trees, carrying a crystal chalice to protect them along the way, which many fans affectionately call the bucket.

The real hook was its multiplayer, and the price tag that came with it. Up to four players could join in, but every single one of them needed a Game Boy Advance and a link cable to act as their controller and personal screen. It was ambitious, a little bit impractical, and an absolute blast if you managed to pull it off. Playing solo, a Moogle carries the chalice for you instead.

You create your own caravanner from one of four tribes, the Clavat, Lilty, Yuke or Selkie, and set off into a world that rewards slow, patient journeys. If wrangling four GBAs sounds like a bit much, the 2020 Remastered Edition added online multiplayer, so your friends can join from the comfort of their own sofas. The myrrh only needs collecting once a year, after all.

Even though the GameCube didn't have a massive library, it did feature several fun and ambitious JRPG adventures.
