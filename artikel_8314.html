The Best Rifle Scopes for Deer Hunting, Tested & Reviewed

I grew up deer hunting in the woods of north-central Wisconsin where a 100-yard shot would be a long one. We needed only a simple 3-9X riflescope that gathered enough light to let us shoot a half-hour after sunset. In other words, any streamlined riflescope with decent glass would work just fine.

A lot of deer hunters still hunt this same way. But riflescope offerings have grown exponentially. So the best riflescope for deer hunting really depends on where, and how, you intend to hunt.

With this roster of scopes I’ve attempted to select a good variety of styles that will work in the northwoods, the open country of the West, and everywhere in between. All of my picks are based on data from in-depth testing conducted by OL’s Optics Editor Andrew McKean and our Gear Editor Scott Einsmann. I focus mostly on affordable options, but there are a couple high-end picks included, too.

The Burris Fullfield is a classic deer scope, built on a 1-inch tube with capped turrets and a second-plane duplex reticle. It’s a simple, capable scope that’s offered for under $300.

​This scope has some nice features, including distinctive red accents on the power-changing ring and turret dials and re-zeroable turrets. But it’s not perfect either. It has a distracting greenish coating that’s especially noticeable in low-light conditions. Its turret indexing is so tiny that it’s hard to confirm when you’re zeroed.

But for a good image, plus simple and repeatable controls, the Burris Fullfield 3-12×42 checks every box for the workaday deer hunter.

Nightforce is known for building battle-ready riflescopes for long-range precision shooting. But their new NX6 product line is designed specifically for hunters. I used the 3-18X50 F1 version with a MIL reticle on an elk hunt this fall and found it to be an excellent open-country hunting scope. It had strong image quality and low-light performance. It’s a hefty scope, but not absurdly so, like some other precision optics.

One of the key features of the NX6 line is the new FieldSet turret system. Once you’ve established your zero, any simple tool — a coin, knife, or flat rock — will loosen the exposed turret cap, allowing you to reset your zero and set the zero stop. The stop can be set a full revolution above zero, or 2 MILs.

One of the many cool things about this system is that any NX6 model except the 1-6x can be converted from capped to exposed turret, and the standard exposed turret can be traded for a custom BDC turret. It’s another indication that the NX6 is positioned to be the ultimate versatile scope family.

The scope comes with a versatile illumination system that lights up the entire reticle. This is a relatively advanced and expensive scope, but for spun-up riflemen who are looking to shoot deer at longer ranges, it’s an excellent option.

The latest incarnation of Trijicon’s legendary AccuPoint line, this durable LPVO is designed to withstand the recoil of big-bucking magnums. Its always-on illumination doesn’t require a battery to fire. Its balance, style, and grit befit a bespoke rifle that’s imagined as an heirloom but used as your ride-or-die hunting gun.

Instead of relying on a battery, the Trijicon’s illumination is activated by sunlight that hits the windows of a fiber-optic receptor and translates photons into intensity of a fine 0.25 MOA green dot at the intersection of a bold second focal-plane duplex reticle. In low-light situations, the element tritium does the work of the sun and supplies the energy to light up that aiming point.

It’s an elegant and durable system that’s optimized for this 1-8×24 build, which neatly fits every AR and straight-wall cartridge carbine made. The scope’s Dangerous Game BDC reticle in MOA is built for close-in and medium-range target work. The tough detail is its price, which is steep.

The excellent glass, tight but understated controls and tasteful lines elevate the Trijicon to a level of sophistication that most grubby LPVOs can’t reach.

The TANGO-MSR Compact brings plenty of versatility in a package that measures 8.4 inches and comes pre-mounted on a very good cantilever unimount for the screaming price of $399.

The glass in this scope isn’t great, but everything else about this little marvel is spot-on. The second-plane arrangement in the TANGO-MSR is simple and useful. The inverted-horseshoe center aiming arrangement blazes bright in daylight conditions, and the BDC grid is useful for placing bullets with precision on targets out to 500 yards. The only real tradeoff is that the scope must be set at its highest magnification for the bullet-drop subtensions to be accurate.

We put the scope through a battery of close-range fast-fire drills, and atop an AR carbine, then we slowed things down and rang steel out to a quarter-mile with the scope. It performed admirably in all cases.

The TANGO-MSR is optimized for AR, but it would be great on a straight-wall cartridge carbine or muzzleloader.

The Crossfire HD line, slowly replacing the Crossfire 2 line, has a scope for every hunter, from a handy LPVO to a big 6-18×50 model to this mid-mag optic that’s optimized for America’s deer hunters.

The 4-12×44 model is priced at only $300, and is an excellent budget option. It’s built on the second focal-plane arrangement and with MOA-based aiming points. There’s the classic Dead-Hold BDC that’s a non-illuminated plex design with three bullet-drop references and windage hashed. Or you can opt for an illuminated version of that reticle, with a pin-point illuminated center dot for a $50 upcharge. Or you can go old-school and use the Widerange Plex, which is basically a German #4 duplex without illumination.

The glass in the Crossfire HD line didn’t blow us away, but the magnification range is right in the sweet spot for whitetail hunters, and we found it to be an easy, simple, and budget workhorse.

“I think your average hunter would take this over the other $300 scopes in our test. It’s worth that price all day long,” noted Outdoor Life optics tester Todd Netto.

For $450, you get some great features and performance from the V3. It is offered in three versions of its second-plane reticle build, a non-illuminated No. 6 plex and both non-illuminated and center-dot illuminated versions of the SHR (it stands for Smart Hunting Reticle) hash-style design with MOA-based holdover and windage references. The illuminated version has a crisp center dot that shines with a red illumination.

These new Zeiss scopes are no longer made in Europe. They’re sourced in Asia, but if that suggests a decline in optical performance or brand integrity, worry not. These are excellent scopes that use premium components. Also, they’re designed for the American hunter.

For a price-point scope, Bushnell’s R5 has some surprisingly sophisticated features. The most useful is its second focal-plane DOA-LRH800 reticle.

The modified tree-style reticle is extremely tunable, meaning that for shooters and hunters willing to invest the time, the Bushnell Ballistics app can produce customized drop tables for your specific load and magnification setting. Not too shabby for a $250 scope.

The R5 doesn’t have quite the smooth and confident controls of its big sibling, the R7 introduced earlier this year, but it’s a more responsive and precise build than most scopes in its price range. If you’re willing to spend a little more, consider the R7 line which won our 2026 Editor’s Choice award for best all-around hunting scope.

This is a throwback deer scope in all the best ways. It’s hardy and durable. It’s simple and reliable.

The Maven brings a second focal-plane MOA-based reticle that’s clean and easy to see. The heart of the scope is the wire-frame CSHR-W reticle, which provides 20 MOA of windage references on the horizontal stadia at 10x, along with 20 MOA of elevation holdover. At $425, the Maven is priced in a sweet spot for deer hunters.

Sometimes you just need a simple, old-school optic for your deer gun. This stripped-down fixed-power scope from Athlon is designed for muzzleloaders, but it could just as easily work on a lever gun.

There’s remarkably little to say about this Talos HMR. Its 1x magnification is pretty solid, without the fishbowl distortion of some low-power scopes. The duplex reticle is proven. There are no illumination or parallax controls to worry about.

All that simplicity holds the Athlon back, of course. It’s not going to help you reach out beyond 300 yards to ring a steel target, and it’s not going to offer any windage or hold-over marks. But it should be durable and fast to target at shots at deer inside 100 yards.

Leupold is filling out its product line with this 4-times zoom ratio family that bridges the gap between the VX-3HD and the VX-5HD. Significantly, the VX-4HD scopes feature Leupold’s excellent push-button 2-revolution CDS-ZL2 elevation dials, and not the toolless Gen2 turrets that it’s rolling out with its other premium scope lines.

You’ll recognize many of the features of the VX-4HD line. It’s built on a second focal-plane system with a fairly standard selection of reticles: duplex, Wind-Plex with 1 MOA hashes extending to 10 MOA on either side of the center crosshair, and illuminated FireDot Twilight Hunter.

​But the new family has upgraded optics, delivering a bright, crisp, and vibrant image. An important point for deer hunters: the 4-20×50 won our low-light test, thanks to the big 50mm objective lens and the excellent glass and coatings. Its removeable elevation turret can be traded out for a free custom ballistic dial tuned to your own bullet and load (second CDS dials cost $80). The scope itself costs about $1,000, with illumination adding another $100 to $200, depending on configuration.

Sure there might be brighter, more powerful, and more feature-rich scopes on the market. But those optics aren’t what most deer hunters want, or need. The best deer scope is functional, reliable, and simple. Hopefully, it’s affordable too. So that’s how we built this list of scopes.
