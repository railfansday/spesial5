The 2026 Appalachian Trail Thru-Hiker Survey: General Information (Part 1)

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Each year, The Trek asks all Appalachian Trail hikers to complete a survey about their hike preparation, trail experiences, and personal backpacking journeys. We’ve taken all the information from our 293 responses this year and have analyzed it to help you better understand the Appalachian Trail Class of 2026.

Our hope is that this survey serves as a great resource for prospective Appalachian Trail thru-hikers as they plan and prepare for their hike. However, please keep in mind that no two hikes look the same, and you should by no means feel obligated to follow the trends or “averages” presented here. Use this data as a guide to inform your decisions, but remember that the most successful hike is the one that works best for you.

A note on the data: As this survey is intended to help prospective thru-hikers plan for the trail, we filter the responses to only include those who either initially planned to complete the entire trail or those who hiked enough on the trail to have pertinent statistics to thru-hikers.

We therefore excluded responses from hikers who hiked less than 500 miles and never intended to thru-hike; those who intended to complete an AT thru-hike but needed to stop before hiking 500 miles are still included. If you have any feedback on this benchmark, please leave a comment and let us know what you think the cutoff should be for 2027.

Using this framework, we have collected data from 294 AT long-distance hikers in 2026. The number will rise as we continue to accept responses from southbound hikers, and this survey will be updated with their information later into 2027.

This first post will focus on the broader demographics and logistics for Appalachian Trail hikers, while the next post will dig into the on-trail experiences and favorites/least favorites of this year’s Class.

The age distribution of hikers starting the trail remains generally consistent year over year, though 2026 pushed the upper boundaries of the age range even further. This season, our youngest hiker was 16 years old, while at the other end of the spectrum, the oldest hiker was 76. (For comparison, the 2025 range spanned from 12 to 73, and 2024 ran from 18 to 71.)

Notably, though he did not fill out our survey, Dale “Grey Beard” Sanders was the oldest thru-hiker on trail this year, setting the age record for an Appalachian Trail thru-hike at 91.

We continue to see a large core group of hikers in their 20s and early 30s. These are typically recent college graduates and those unburdened by mortgage or family ties. This group is followed by another notable wave of hikers in their late 50s and early 60s, as retirees use their extra time and financial stability to take on the trail.

This year, the average age of an AT thru-hiker was 39, which remains much higher than the most commonly reported age of 25. In statistics, when the mean (39) sits well above the median (34) and mode (25), it points to a long tail end of older participants pulling the overall average upward.

Since The Trek began collecting demographic data for the survey in 2016, the gender distribution has fluctuated between around 54 and 63% men. In 2025, 63% of survey respondents were men, which was the highest proportion of men to date.

This year, we stay consistent with that high proportion of men at 62.5%, while responses from women rose slightly to 34.8% and non-binary respondents held steady at just over 2%.

The trail remains overwhelmingly (96%) White, a trend that persists year over year as the proportion of White respondents has consistently landed above 90% since we began collecting demographic data in 2016.

While efforts to diversify the outdoors continue, the demographic data shows that hikers identifying as White still make up the overwhelming majority, with fewer than 4% of hikers identifying as Hispanic, Asian, Black, Native American, or Pacific Islander. This number, while already small, is even smaller than in 2025, when we saw just over 4% of hikers identifying as non-White.

As with previous years, the vast majority of survey respondents in 2026 (90%) were from the United States. Eleven other countries were represented among the respondents, which is slightly more than we saw last year, with international hikers primarily coming from Canada, the UK, and Germany.

Within the United States, the majority of hikers continued to hail from eastern and Mid-Atlantic states, taking advantage of closer geographic proximity to the trail.

As in previous years, we hypothesize that hikers from eastern states are more likely to tackle the AT due to easier travel logistics and greater familiarity and emotional connection with the area. Last year, Colorado marked the non-eastern state with the most respondents; this year, only 6 responses came from Colorado while Ohio and Texas each produced 10.

The states producing the most respondents this year were North Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Virginia, tied for the top spot with 21 respondents each and closely followed by Georgia at 15 — all states found along the Appalachian Trail.

Education levels remained consistent with previous years — our survey indicates that Appalachian Trail thru-hikers continue to be a highly educated group, with two-thirds of respondents holding a Bachelor’s, graduate, or professional degree.

Similar to prior years, the small fraction of respondents indicating they did not finish high school is largely (though not entirely) accounted for by younger hikers who took on the trail as recent graduates or during gap periods.

This year, respondents hailed from over 200 distinct occupations before starting the trail. We’ve grouped them into the above categories, with STEM & Professional Services (Engineering, Technology, Science, Finance, Law, and Corporate Administration) once again leading the pack at 35.4% of responses.

Students and educators continue to make up a significant segment of the thru-hiking community. Educators alone account for 5.5% of respondents. When we asked participants how they secured the time off to thru-hike, 7.3% of respondents cited having summers off or seasonal flexibility, while another 6.9% leveraged scheduled breaks from school.

As in prior years, nearly 38.1% of respondents quit their jobs entirely to hike. Combined with the 23.2% who are retired, career breaks, transitions, and retirement make up over 60% of the total AT Class of 2026, showing how much a long-distance trail can stem from or cause a major life pivot.

The average hiker on the Appalachian Trail in 2026 had between one and seven nights of prior backpacking experience, with nearly 30% of respondents falling into the 1-to-3-nights bracket and over 21% falling into the 4-to-7-nights range. Very few respondents reported having no prior hiking or backpacking experience at all. Meanwhile, a strong group (13.3%) reported 3 months or more of prior experience, likely representing hikers who had previously completed other long-distance trails.

This year, we also examined the rates of unplanned early exits from trail across these prior experience levels. Interestingly, the data shows that prior backpacking experience does not strictly dictate success; hikers with modest experience (such as 1-3 nights or 4-7 nights) experienced relatively stable and moderate rates of not finishing (23% and 23.4%, respectively).

Those with zero prior backpacking experience did face a higher-than-average attrition rate (31.6% for day hikers only and 50% for the two hikers who reported never hiking or camping, for what that tiny dataset is worth). Overall, though, the data continues to corroborate the idea that the Appalachian Trail can successfully be a first major thru-hike for well-prepared beginners.

This year, 73.9% of survey respondents completed thru-hikes with over 2,000 miles hiked, rebounding nicely from last year’s rate of 63.7% and surpassing the 71% mark reported in 2024.

However, this continues to trend much higher than the completion rates reported by the Appalachian Trail Conservancy (ATC), which historically hovers around a 25% to 30% success rate. There are a few key reasons for this consistent disparity.

First, the ATC captures a massive dataset of every registered start, whereas our survey relies on voluntary responses. Second, because we promote our survey through The Trek and our Instagram and community channels, we naturally attract responses from hikers who are plugged into the thru-hiking ecosystem and are often better equipped and more motivated to finish.

We speculate that readers of The Trek are simply better prepared for the physical and mental demands of the trail after engaging with our guides and community. While we can’t prove that our content is the secret sauce to a successful AT thru-hike, please let us keep believing it for the sake of our ego.

75 survey respondents this year did not complete a full thru-hike. Of that group, 13.3% (10 hikers) said they only intended to hike a section or had already reached their personal mileage goal. That represents a sharp decline from 2025, when planned section hikers accounted for 39% of the non-finishing respondents (likely because we worked harder to filter out short-distance section hikers from the dataset this year).

When we look at the 65 hikers who experienced an unplanned exit from the trail, injury remains the undisputed primary reason, accounting for 38.7% of all non-finishers. Tying for the next most common reasons were family/personal obligations and mental burnout (such as boredom or loss of motivation), each accounting for 13.3% of respondents who left the trail.

Interestingly, while 2025 was an anomaly where zero respondents cited finances as their reason for leaving the trail, money troubles returned in 2026: running out of funds accounted for 8% of exits (6 hikers), tying with illness and the general physical toll of the trail.

One important note about this data: though we advertise this survey to the best of our ability, many hikers who unexpectedly left the trail early are likely not engaging with Appalachian Trail media by the time autumn rolls around. As a result, our responses remain skewed toward those who completed their hikes as planned, meaning we are likely only seeing a partial picture of trail attrition.

Not only were injuries the largest cause of needing to leave the trail, but they were also quite pervasive among all the hikers on trail. More than half of our survey respondents (55.4%) experienced an injury during their 2026 hike, while 44.6% made it through unscathed.

Getting hurt on the Appalachian Trail does not automatically mean the end of the trail. Over 81% of injured hikers managed to stay on the trail by adapting their routine. Nearly a third of those injured (32.5%) hiked through the pain without taking any time off. Another 28.1% dialed back their daily mileage to recover, while 20.6% took some time off the trail.

Ultimately, 18.8% of reported injuries were hike-ending (9.9% of all respondents).

In 2026, hike direction returned to its traditional pre-Hurricane Helene baseline, with the proportion of Northbound hikers jumping nearly 13 percentage points to 92.9% (up from 80.2% in 2025). Conversely, Flip-Flop hikes dropped from 13.5% to 5.0%, and Southbound respondents fell from 6.3% to 2.1%.

That shift confirms last year’s spike in alternative itineraries was largely a temporary response to Hurricane Helene. Ahead of the 2025 season, the ATC and media outlets (like us!) encouraged hikers to pursue Southbound or Flip-Flop hikes to give southern trail towns and trail crews time to rebuild. With the southern portion of the trail recovered and reopened for the 2026 season, the vast majority of thru-hikers returned to the classic Georgia-to-Maine route.

Despite expectations last year that Southbound numbers would continue to climb as more people learned about the benefits of hiking south, the pull of the traditional Northbound social bubble remains hard to beat.

Most hikers began their hikes in March and April (accounting for nearly 75% of all start dates), with the single most popular start date this year shifting about two weeks earlier to March 14, closely followed by March 9.

We asked respondents if they were satisfied with their start dates, and satisfaction jumped even higher this year: 94% of all respondents reported being happy with when they set out. Because the 2026 bubble shifted slightly earlier into March, the average start date for satisfied NOBO hikers moved up to March 18 (compared to March 29 in 2025).

Meanwhile, the average start date for NOBO hikers who felt they started too early was February 12, while the average date for those who felt they started too late fell between April 8 and April 10. With over 9 out of 10 hikers satisfied with their choice, the “right” start date ultimately comes down to the type of trail experience you want to have.

The small handful of hikers who felt they started too late cited weather concerns, wanting a slower pace during the first few weeks, not having enough time to heal from an injury and still finish, or feeling stressed as a solo hiker at the tail end of the bubble. Conversely, the five hikers who felt they started too early pointed to cold winter weather in February and early March. One April 6 starter noted they actually wished the trail had been more crowded.

If you want to start in what most thru-hikers consider the “sweet spot” for Northbounders, aim for a mid-to-late March window, keeping in mind that starting right in the March bubble means sharing shelters and campsites with bigger crowds early on.

Southbound hikers start later in the season — this year ranging from May 29 to August 12, with most setting out in late May and June — to let winter conditions clear up north. All eight of our current Southbound respondents reported being satisfied with their start dates, though the sample size remains too small to track broader date trends.

This year we’re keeping the survey open for Southbound applicants who are still on trail, and we’ll be back with more SOBO data early in 2027. SOBOs: click here to take the 2026 Appalachian Trail Thru-Hiker Survey.

Most successful thru-hikers finish between mid-July and mid-October, with September (86 hikers) and August (65 hikers) as the peak months for summiting Katahdin. The single most popular finish date this year was September 15 (9 hikers), closely followed by September 19 and August 26.

While a small handful of fast-moving Northbounders finished as early as late May and June — led by one early starter who wrapped up on May 31 and noted it was too early for weather-related reasons — the vast majority timed their finishes for late summer and early autumn, well within Baxter State Park’s typical May-to-October operating window.

Overall, 94.4% of thru-hikers reported being satisfied with their finish date. The average finish date that Northbound hikers were satisfied with was August 27 (shifting a little over two weeks later than last year’s August 11 average), while the average finish date reported as too late for weather-related reasons was September 10 for Northbounders. Two Northbound hikers who summited on September 19 and September 20 felt their finish dates were too crowded, landing right in the heart of the mid-September summit bubble.

With the information on start and end dates, we can look at the duration and pacing of hikers’ treks. We filtered this data to only include respondents who completed a thru-hike of the Appalachian Trail.

The average completed thru-hike this year took 160 days (just over five months), ticking up slightly from last year’s 153-day average. The longest reported thru-hike was 230 days (a flip-flopper who started February 27 and finished October 12), while the fastest completed thru-hike was an 88-day Southbound trek, followed closely by two Northbounders who finished in 96 days.

We also asked self-reported thru-hikers about their average daily mileage, with the overall average across respondents coming in at 15.0 miles per day (up from 14.3 miles per day in 2025). The single most-reported daily mileage was once again 15 miles per day (31 hikers), followed closely by 14 and 13 miles per day.

Even though our survey asks hikers to include zero days in their estimate, many respondents likely reported their hiking-day average rather than their true calendar average, or simply guessed without tracking exact daily numbers. Treat this figure as a loose ballpark: at the trail’s official length of 2,197.9 miles, the average 160-day thru-hike works out to a true calendar pace of roughly 13.7 miles per day once zero days and town stops are factored in.

Attendance at trail festivals rebounded strongly in 2026, driven by a return to classic Northbound traffic and high engagement among thru-hikers. While 44.2% of survey respondents still reported not attending any trail festival, that figure dropped significantly from 2025 (when 56% reported no festival attendance) as crowds returned to trail towns.

Trail Days in Damascus, Virginia remains by far the most popular AT festival, with 44.2% of respondents attending. Meanwhile, the Northbound Kickoff at Amicalola Falls saw a significant attendance bump, drawing 11.6% of respondents.

Unlike in prior years, we filtered our financial questions to include only those who completed their thru-hikes, to better reflect how much it actually costs to hike the entire trail.

Likely in part due to this decision, the average cost of an Appalachian Trail thru-hike (including the cost of gear) climbed notably this year to $8,863 (compared to $7,680 in 2025 and $7,602 in 2024), with a median spend of $8,500 and many hikers reporting total costs exceeding $10,000.

The spread of hikers across the cost spectrum shows significant spikes around $6,000 and $8,000, along with 28 hikers reporting spending $10,000. At the extreme ends, only 6 hikers reported spending under $2,500, while 15 reported spending $15,000 or more.

Hikers spent an average of $2,305 on their gear in 2026 across all respondents who completed 500 miles or more on the AT, ticking up moderately from previous years. The median gear spend was $2,000, with the most common spending bracket also at $2,000 (57 hikers).

The vast majority of hikers spent under $4,000 on their gear, which covers the initial cost of purchasing packs, tents, and sleep systems along with the ongoing replacement or repair of footwear, clothing, and worn-out items over the course of their journey.

While most kept their setups budget-friendly or mid-tier with their spend peaking between $1,000 and $2,500, a few outliers scaled the high end of the luxury spectrum, with 11 respondents reporting gear investments exceeding $10,000 (including two hikers topping out at $11,000).

To those few hikers who absolutely splurged, shoot me a message. I’d love to know what on earth is in your pack.
