Meeting a Camino Werewolf

Tanggal: 2026-11-15

At 7:30 AM, we stepped outside into the crisp morning air, greeted by the quintessential country scent of farm fields. Right near our starting point stood a fascinating stone hórreo—one of those coffin-sized, traditional Galician granaries perched precariously on stone pillars. It looked like a firm push could knock it over, yet it has likely stood firmly in place for centuries. We grabbed a quick coffee and toast at a cozy café right behind it before hitting the trail.

The small, medieval hamlet of Olveiroa quickly faded behind us as we began a steady climb toward a ridge crowned with windmills. Down in the valley below, a river gorge carved through the landscape, while scrubby pines and brush covered the slopes.

Even with power lines crisscrossing the hills, the sweeping morning views were spectacular. Because the sun had not yet risen above the ridge, giant, meditative shadows of the spinning windmill blades danced across the opposite hillside.

Passing the busy hamlet of Logoso, we followed a gravel trail through pine and eucalyptus groves toward the crossroads of Hospital.

In 1489, a visiting bishop wrote about encountering a terrifying beast in these woods called the “Vakner”—a creature that Galician folklore transformed into a giant werewolf. Today, a small welcome center sits at the site. Knowing this was our last pitstop for the next 8 miles until reaching the coast at Cee, we paused for a brief rest under sunny skies and cool 50s temperatures.

Shortly after leaving Hospital, trail magic found us. As Knockerz chatted with a Portuguese couple about her persistent shin splints, they insisted she sit down. They applied a soothing painkiller cream, expertly wrapped her shin with KT tape, and gifted us their remaining tube of cream.

Surprisingly, they had also stayed with the Czech hiker Knockerz helped yesterday while waiting an hour and a half for an ambulance. They confirmed he was doing fine and back on his feet—explaining that the language barrier was simply because he only spoke Czech! Reconnecting with them felt like a true trail miracle.

Further along the ridge, we met our tramily for coffee, tea, and fresh chorizo-and-egg sandwiches before continuing together through managed pine forests. Mid-hike, we were startled to find a giant, 15-foot bronze statue of the monstrous Vakner lurking right beside the forested trail! Coming across it unexpectedly in the woods was wonderfully eerie.

Pushing further along the ridge, the trees parted, and the Atlantic Ocean suddenly burst into view ahead of us. Ancient Romans considered this coastline the literal Finis Terre—the end of the known world.

We began a steep, winding descent down off the ridge toward the coastal twin towns of Cee and Corcubión, nestled tightly into protected ocean coves. Arriving at the sea felt completely surreal. After walking hundreds of miles all the way across Spain from the French Pyrenees, we were watching waves crash against the shore. Multi-colored buildings with red tile roofs ringed the bay, seagulls circled overhead, and fish swam in the crystal-clear waters below.

We parted ways with our tramily at their albergue and climbed a steep hill to our apartment in an old medieval neighborhood of Corcubión, complete with its own ancient stone hórreo.

Realizing the only open grocery store in the neighboring town of Cee was closing shortly, I sprinted back on a 15-minute dash. Navigating Spanish grocery scales—where you enter an item’s specific number to print a price sticker before hitting the register—I helped an Australian couple figure out the system before quickly gathering our food supplies for the night.

Walking out of the grocery store with our supplies in hand, a massive wave of emotion suddenly hit me. Just like reaching the halfway point at Harpers Ferry or the summit of Katahdin on the Appalachian Trail, the profound sense of accomplishment finally washed over me. Hearing the seagulls squawk over the harbor in the evening air, I felt overwhelmed with deep, peaceful gratitude. We made it to the ocean.
