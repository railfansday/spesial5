Clearing the way for a transformative stem-cell treatment for Parkinson’s disease

Joohi Jimenez-Shahed studies movement disorders, neuromodulation and brain circuit therapeutics at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City.

Big improvements in Parkinson’s disease treatment have been few and far between. The tantalizing prospect of regenerative stem-cell therapies to address this neurodegenerative condition is therefore creating considerable excitement.

A core component of Parkinson’s is the loss of dopamine-producing neurons, which normally provide carefully controlled neurotransmitter signals to the brain’s movement circuits1. This leads to people having substantial difficulties performing routine activities.

A dopamine-replacement approach provided one of the first major advances in Parkinson’s treatment2. Levodopa, a chemical precursor to dopamine that was introduced in the 1960s, treats symptoms effectively. But the levodopa honeymoon can be short. As the effect duration of each dose wanes, extra doses or medications are required to keep dopamine levels from falling. And the drug can come with a complication that many patients dread — writhing, uncontrolled involuntary movements known as dyskinesia. A surgical approach called deep brain stimulation (DBS) can mitigate these complications effectively3.

The downsides of levodopa raise a question: rather than replacing dopamine, why not replace the cellular machinery that produces it? Enter the dopaminergic stem cell. Engineered from cells sampled from an adult’s own tissues (known as autologous) or from a different individual (allogeneic), induced-pluripotent stem cells can be safely transplanted into human brains and can improve movement-related Parkinson’s symptoms, such as tremors, stiffness and slowness, without worsening dyskinesia4.

Only a few dozen people have received this experimental treatment. Major brain surgery is required to transplant the cells. Allogeneic cell transplants also require recipients to undertake a year of immunosuppression to ensure that the cells survive. Autologous cell transplants avoid this, but it takes 6–8 months to produce the cells for each patient.

It is not yet known how cell therapies will measure up to current standards of care. Early studies have been in people with advanced disease. At this stage, the chance of benefit might be reduced: the cells are implanted into an already degenerating circuit that could be difficult to restore.

Critics might therefore argue that dopamine cell therapies cannot produce a more robust effect than levodopa or DBS without imposing excess cost or risk. But the way that Parkinson’s treatments are investigated and approved can neither fully support this pessimistic view nor provide sufficient evidence for a more optimistic one.

Therapeutic development for Parkinson’s has mainly aimed to relieve symptoms. A more ambitious goal would be to slow the disease’s progression by altering its underlying biology. Yet there are substantial obstacles to developing such a treatment.

Implicit to the concept of disease modification is the need for a biomarker that measures the underlying biological processes5. Although a biological definition of Parkinson’s is emerging, it applies mainly to the identification of early disease. Relatively little is known about how such markers evolve in Parkinson’s, or how they relate to clinical progression6.

Long-term, multi-site observational studies such as the Parkinson’s Precision Medicine Initiative (see go.nature.com/4ayapbI) provide an ideal framework for exploring digital, biological and imaging markers, while benchmarking disease progression. Their success depends on building and sustaining large consortia, and on a healthy dose of time and effort from researchers and study participants, but the studies are constrained by funding inequities.

Even if such biomarkers were already at researchers’ disposal, there is no specific guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration on developing disease-modifying treatments7. The agency’s Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy designation speeds up cell- and gene-therapy development and approval. But eligibility requires only the intent to treat, modify, reverse or cure a serious disorder. As a result, clinical trials of regenerative therapies are likely to be designed to show benefits to a person’s symptoms without incurring the added cost and burden of showing a change in biology — a pragmatic but less ambitious path.

Crucially, supportive evidence for the broader potential of dopaminergic cell therapies is what will define their advantage. Cell dose, optimal treatment timing and objective evidence of cell survival, engraftment and effects on brain-network function would demonstrate whether cell therapies can produce lasting change in people with Parkinson’s. Public–private research partnerships could refine the required measurement tools and generate such data.

The ability to transform stem cells into dopamine-producing neurons and transplant them safely into human brains is momentous. Its use as a treatment extends an opportunity to chart another course for people with Parkinson’s by limiting levodopa complications and possibly the need for DBS. This outcome would bolster the hopes of people with this condition. But the real therapeutic breakthrough will occur only when scientists and regulators converge to advance the goal of restoring neural circuitry and function. That would be a truly bold step towards conquering this this relentless disease.
