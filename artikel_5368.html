How a USDA public health alert differs from a recall on meat, poultry and egg products

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Free daily briefing on global business news.

A USDA public health alert and a recall both flag potentially unsafe meat, poultry or processed egg products, but they differ in whether the product is still sold

The same package of frozen chicken in a home freezer could end up named in a federal recall or in a public health alert. The label on that notice is not a matter of wording. It tells consumers something specific about where the product is likely to be.

Both kinds of notices come from the Food Safety and Inspection Service, known as FSIS, which is part of the U.S. Department of Agriculture. FSIS regulates meat, poultry and processed egg products. The Food and Drug Administration regulates most other foods, including fresh produce and dairy. That split defines what a USDA notice can cover. A USDA notice applies only to the three product categories FSIS oversees. A warning about a bag of spinach comes from the FDA instead.

The core of the USDA public health alert vs. recall question is availability. A recall removes product that is still in commerce, meaning it may still be on store shelves or moving through distribution. A public health alert warns consumers about product that is likely no longer available for purchase but may still be in homes. FSIS also uses alerts when it lacks enough information to request a recall. An alert does not mean the product is safe to eat.

Recalls carry another layer of information. Each one is assigned to Class I, Class II or Class III according to the health risk involved. Public health alerts carry no class at all. USDA recalls are also voluntary actions taken by the company. FSIS does not have mandatory recall authority, though it has other tools to keep unsafe product away from consumers. The FDA, by contrast, holds mandatory recall authority for most foods under the Food Safety Modernization Act.

USDA food safety notices come from the Food Safety and Inspection Service, or FSIS. FSIS regulates meat, poultry and processed egg products. The Food and Drug Administration regulates most other foods.

FSIS is an agency of the U.S. Department of Agriculture. A food safety notice that carries the USDA name is an FSIS notice.

The agency's scope is limited to three product categories: meat, poultry and processed egg products. A USDA notice can only involve a product from one of those groups.

That limit makes the issuing agency a useful clue. A notice from FSIS signals that the product involved is meat, poultry or a processed egg product. A USDA notice will not cover a bag of salad or a box of cereal. Those foods fall outside the FSIS categories.

The FDA oversees most of the remaining food supply. A warning about a food outside the three FSIS categories generally comes from the FDA rather than the USDA.

The split also points readers to the right place to check. Someone trying to confirm whether a packaged meat or poultry item is affected should start with FSIS notices. Someone checking on a food outside those categories should look to the FDA instead.

A USDA recall is a company's action to remove meat, poultry or processed egg products from commerce because they may be unsafe or mislabeled. The Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS), the USDA agency that oversees those products, publishes the notice that tells the public what is affected.

The key word is commerce. A recall targets product that is still moving through distribution or still available to buyers. The point is to pull that product back before more of it is sold or eaten.

Two kinds of problems can lead to a recall. One is safety, meaning the food itself may pose a hazard to the people who eat it. The other is labeling, meaning the package does not accurately describe what is inside. A labeling problem alone can be enough to prompt a recall.

A USDA recall applies when a meat, poultry or processed egg product is still in commerce, and a public health alert applies when it probably is not. That availability question is the main factor separating the two notices issued by the Food Safety and Inspection Service, or FSIS.

When product is still in commerce, a recall has work to do in the marketplace. Product may be sitting in warehouses, in distribution centers or in store display cases. The company responsible for it has to get it back. The FSIS notice tells the public about a removal that is under way.

A public health alert fits a different situation. The window for taking the product off sale has largely closed. Stores may have sold through their stock, or the item may no longer be offered. A removal from commerce would find little or nothing left to collect. The remaining concern sits in kitchens, refrigerators and home freezers.

Every meat, poultry or processed egg product recall announced through the U.S. Department of Agriculture is a voluntary action by the company responsible for the product. The Food Safety and Inspection Service, the USDA agency that oversees those foods, does not have mandatory recall authority.

That means FSIS cannot order a company to pull its product. What it can do is request a recall. When FSIS makes that request, the decision to remove the product from commerce still formally rests with the company.

The agency has other tools if a company does not act. FSIS can detain product, which keeps it from moving further through commerce. It can also seize product. And it can withdraw inspection services from the establishment involved.

The establishment number is the plant code printed inside the round USDA mark of inspection on meat, poultry and processed egg product packaging. It usually appears as "EST." followed by a number. FSIS notices list this code to identify the plant behind an affected product.

The number points to the facility where the food was produced or processed under federal inspection. It does not identify the brand. A single plant can pack products for several different labels. A single brand can also come from more than one plant. Two packages with the same name on the front can therefore carry different establishment numbers.

The mark itself is a small circle printed on the label. It can sit on the front, the back or the side, depending on the package design.

The FSIS recalls and public health alerts page is the official place to read USDA notices about meat, poultry and processed egg products. Questions can go by phone to the USDA Meat and Poultry Hotline at 1-888-MPHotline (1-888-674-6854).

The Food Safety and Inspection Service, the USDA agency that regulates those three product categories, publishes the page. Recalls and public health alerts are both posted there. A reader can check either kind of action in one place, without looking up a separate source for each.

Each notice spells out how to identify the affected packages. It names the products and gives their package sizes and dates. It also lists the establishment number, which appears inside the USDA mark of inspection as "EST." followed by a number. The full notice, rather than a shortened summary of it, shows every affected item at once.

The package itself offers a second clue. Meat, poultry and processed egg products inspected by FSIS carry the round USDA mark of inspection. That mark ties the product to FSIS oversight. A package bearing it is the kind of item a USDA notice can cover.

Egg items need a closer look. FSIS jurisdiction names processed egg products specifically. The wording of a notice, together with the agency that issued it, shows which category an egg item falls into.

For anyone reading a food safety headline, the issuing agency is the first detail to check. A USDA notice points to meat, poultry or processed egg products. Any other food is most likely an FDA matter.

The scope is narrow. A USDA recall applies only to the three product categories FSIS regulates: meat, poultry and processed egg products.

The FSIS notice serves as the public record of the recall. It names the company involved, describes the affected products and states the reason for the action. It also gives the recall class, which reflects the level of health risk.

Each notice is specific. It lists the product names, package sizes and dates that define which items are covered. It also includes the establishment number from the USDA mark of inspection, which ties the product to the inspected establishment that produced it.

That precision limits the recall's reach. Only the items matching the details in the notice are part of the recall. Other products from the same company, or the same type of food sold under different labels, are not covered unless the notice lists them.

Responsibility for the removal sits with the company. The business that made or distributed the product takes it back from the market, and FSIS makes the action public through its notice.

A public health alert is a consumer warning from the Food Safety and Inspection Service about meat, poultry or processed egg products. It covers products that are likely no longer available for purchase but may still be in people's homes.

FSIS is the agency within the U.S. Department of Agriculture that oversees those three product categories. It turns to an alert in two distinct situations. Each one answers a different question about a potentially unsafe product.

The first question is where the product is. Food bought before a problem came to light can stay in a refrigerator, freezer or pantry after it has disappeared from store shelves. In that case, the people at risk are the households that already took it home, not the stores that once sold it. An alert is written for those households.

The second question is what the agency knows. FSIS issues an alert when it does not have enough information to request a recall. The alert lets the agency tell the public about a concern without having the basis it would need to ask a company to act.

An alert is a different kind of action from a recall. A recall is a company removing product from commerce. An alert is a public warning from FSIS itself, directed at consumers rather than at the supply chain.

FSIS posts its public health alerts on the same web page as its recall notices. Each notice identifies the affected items by product name, package size and dates. Readers can compare those details against what they have at home.

An alert does not declare a product safe. It reflects only that the item is likely no longer for sale. Anyone holding a product named in an alert should follow the instructions in the notice itself. Those instructions may tell consumers to throw the product away or return it as the notice directs.

That is why an alert speaks directly to consumers. Its aim is to reach people who bought the product earlier and may still have it, not to direct a company to clear shelves.

Availability is not the only branch in the decision. FSIS also turns to a public health alert when the information it holds is not sufficient to request a recall. Either way, the alert warns the public without a company removal action attached.

For a shopper reading the headline, the notice type works as a quick signal. A recall points to an item that may still be on sale and could be bought today. An alert points to an item that is likely no longer available for purchase.

An alert is not a sign that the product is safe to eat. It means only that the product is likely gone from stores. Some of it may still be sitting in homes.

FSIS assigns every meat, poultry or processed egg product recall to one of three classes based on the health risk of eating the product. Class I signals the most serious risk, and Class III the least.

Class I covers cases where there is a reasonable probability that eating the product will cause serious health consequences or death. The definition sets two bars at once. One is how likely harm is. The other is how severe that harm would be. A Class I recall involves a product that clears both.

Class II applies when there is a remote probability of adverse health consequences from eating the product. Both measures fall below the Class I standard. The chance of harm is described as remote rather than reasonable. The harm is described as adverse rather than serious or fatal. A remote probability is still a probability of harm.

Class III applies when eating the product will not cause adverse health consequences. It is the only class whose definition rules out adverse health consequences from eating. A recall can cover product that may be mislabeled as well as product that may be unsafe. A Class III recall removes product from commerce for a reason other than a risk from eating it. A labeling error that poses no health risk to the person eating the product fits this class.

The class measures the health risk of eating the product and nothing else. It does not show whether the product is still on store shelves. Other parts of the recall notice describe the affected product itself.

Public health alerts sit outside this three-tier system. FSIS does not assign a class to an alert. The missing class does not mean the product is safe to eat. It reflects that an alert is a different kind of notice from a recall, with no classification step attached.

A request from FSIS therefore comes with those options in reserve. If a company declines, the agency can still act against the product itself or against the plant's inspection.

The FDA works under a different rule. It has mandatory recall authority for most foods under the Food Safety Modernization Act. FDA jurisdiction covers most foods other than meat, poultry and processed egg products. Its mandatory power reaches most of the food supply outside FSIS oversight.

The split means two products in the same grocery cart can fall under different rules. A recalled bag of salad greens or jar of peanut butter falls under the FDA, which can compel a recall. A recalled package of ground beef or chicken sausage falls under FSIS, which can only request one.

For shoppers, the voluntary nature of a USDA recall does not signal a lower level of risk. The word "voluntary" describes how the recall began. The recall class assigned to it describes the health hazard. A Class I recall is still voluntary, even though it covers products with a reasonable probability of serious health consequences or death.

Many USDA recalls and public health alerts trace back to a short list of problems: the bacteria Listeria monocytogenes, Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7, undeclared allergens, foreign material and misbranding. The cause named in a notice tells a consumer what kind of risk the product carries.

Listeria monocytogenes is a frequent concern in ready-to-eat foods such as deli meats, hot dogs and cooked sausages. People often eat those products without cooking them again. Any contamination that happens after processing can therefore reach the consumer directly. Listeria can also grow at refrigerator temperatures. Many other foodborne bacteria cannot. The illness it causes, listeriosis, poses the greatest danger to pregnant people, newborns, older adults and people with weakened immune systems.

Salmonella is most closely associated with raw poultry. It causes salmonellosis, an illness that typically brings diarrhea, fever and stomach cramps. Thorough cooking kills the bacteria. Cross-contamination from raw meat to cutting boards, hands or other foods can still spread it.

E. coli O157:H7 is a Shiga toxin-producing strain of the bacterium most often linked to ground beef. Grinding can move bacteria from the surface of the meat into the interior. Undercooking may then leave them alive. Some infections lead to hemolytic uremic syndrome, a condition that can cause kidney failure. Young children and older adults face the highest risk of that complication.

Undeclared allergens appear when a product contains an ingredient such as milk, eggs, wheat or soy that its label does not list. This can happen when a package ends up with the wrong label. It can also happen when a recipe changes and the label is not updated. For a person with a food allergy, eating the product can trigger a serious reaction.

Foreign material covers physical objects that should not be in food, such as pieces of plastic, metal, glass or rubber. These can cause choking, cuts to the mouth or dental injuries.

Misbranding is a labeling problem. It can mean the label misstates what is in the package or leaves out required information. An undeclared allergen is one form of misbranding. The category also covers errors that carry no allergy risk, such as an incorrect product name or missing label details.

FSIS notices list the establishment number alongside other identifying details. Those usually include the exact product name as it appears on the label, the package size or weight, and one or more dates. The dates can be a packing or production date, or a use-by or best-by date.

A match on the establishment number alone does not settle the question. The product name, package size and date also need to line up with the notice. A package from the named plant with a date outside the listed range may not be covered. A package with the right product name but a different plant number may not be covered either. A package with a matching name and plant number but a different package size may also fall outside the notice.

Food moved out of its original packaging loses these identifiers. Ground meat repackaged into freezer bags or deli meat moved into storage containers no longer carries the establishment number, the dates or the package size. In that case, the label cannot confirm a match with the notice.

Do not eat a meat, poultry or processed egg product named in a USDA recall or public health alert. Check the refrigerator and the freezer, then throw the product away or return it as the notice directs.

Those steps are identical for both kinds of notice. The difference between a recall and an alert concerns store shelves, not kitchens. Once a product is in a home, the label on the notice does not change what the household should do with it.

Start with the freezer. Frozen meat and poultry can sit at home for a long time after purchase. A product that left stores before any notice appeared may still be in a freezer drawer. That is the situation a public health alert is meant to reach.

Then move to the refrigerator, where fresh and ready-to-eat meat and poultry are usually kept. A notice can name refrigerated products as well as frozen ones.

Hold the package next to the notice before deciding anything. The notice names specific products, package sizes and dates, and only a package that matches those details is affected. A similar item with different details may fall outside the notice.

Check each package separately. Two packages of the same product can carry different sizes or dates, and a notice may cover one without covering the other.

Read the instructions in that particular notice once a package matches. Some notices tell consumers to throw the product away. Others say it may also be returned to the place of purchase. One notice's instructions do not necessarily carry over to the next.

Keep a matched product out of every meal. Do not cook it into a larger dish. Do not give it to someone else. Do not set it aside to decide about later. A matched product has two destinations: the trash or the store, as the notice directs.

A recall notice on the page states its class, from Class I through Class III. A public health alert posted there carries no class. Checking for that label is a quick way to tell which kind of action a listing describes. On a recall, the class also shows how FSIS rated the health risk.

The page lists only USDA-regulated foods. A notice about a product outside meat, poultry and processed egg products will not appear there. Those notices come from the FDA, which regulates most other foods.

The hotline offers a direct phone line to USDA for questions about meat, poultry and processed egg products. Its number is published in two forms. One spells part of it in letters as 1-888-MPHotline. The other gives the digits, 1-888-674-6854. Both versions dial the same line.

Keeping the package nearby during a call makes it possible to read out the product name, package size, dates and establishment number. Those are the same details FSIS uses in a notice to define which products it covers.
