Deposit a money order at your own bank or credit union, or cash a postal money order at any post office, and you won’t pay a fee. When you sell something, ask for a postal money order and confirm it with USPS before the buyer leaves, since you’ll repay your bank for any fake that bounces.

Anywhere else, cashing one can cost you. Each check casher sets its own rules and may charge a fee, MoneyGram says, and Western Union notes that not all of its locations can cash one, even the store that sold it.

So the company that issued yours decides where it’s free. The U.S. Postal Service cashes its own money orders free at any post office and lets anyone holding one confirm it’s real. Western Union and MoneyGram send you to a bank or a check casher, so deposit theirs at your own bank or credit union. Without a bank account, a postal money order is the only kind you can count on cashing for free.

Say a buyer pays $400 for your couch with a postal money order. Before the couch leaves, hold the money order up to a light. A watermark and a thread running top to bottom with the letters USPS should show through, the amount in words should match the numbers, and a domestic one can’t be for more than $1,000. Then confirm it on USPS’s money order status page, using its serial number, post office number, and amount, or by calling 1-866-459-7822.

At your bank, read the app’s list of accepted items before you snap photos. Some apps refuse money orders, and Wells Fargo’s turns away postal ones, so hand a refused one to a teller. At a post office, bring a photo ID and sign the money order only at the counter, in front of the clerk, as USPS directs. Either way, photograph both sides first to keep a record of its serial number.

Don’t treat money showing in your account as proof the money order was real. By law, banks have to make deposits available quickly, the Federal Trade Commission notes, while catching a fake can take weeks.
