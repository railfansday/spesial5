Opinion: How Kennedy and MAHA could help stop measles

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Adhikari is a journalist and co-host of the “Why Should I Trust You?” podcast.

A version of this article first appeared on the Substack of “Why Should I Trust You?”

On Tuesday, after the United States lost its measles elimination status, health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. posted on X that he wanted to stop measles from spreading in America. “We will stop measles outbreaks, protect American families, and work to regain U.S. measles elimination status. I continue to recommend [measles, mumps, and rubella] vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.”

Well, Secretary Kennedy, do I have an idea for you …

It involves mobilizing a movement you helped build, a movement that has successfully organized millions of Americans around issues related to their health. I’m talking about MAHA.

Right now, the discourse is playing out like this: Medical societies are expressing alarm over the loss of measles elimination status, while many politicians blame Kennedy and the Trump administration for failing to adequately respond.

Kennedy’s defenders clap back by reminding us that other countries have lost elimination status in recent years, too, that these are all efforts to undermine Kennedy and that his administration is doing what it can.

I understand on some level that, as a society, we need to litigate what went wrong. But candidly, I’m much more interested in something else: Who might be best positioned to reach the Americans that public health officials are struggling to persuade to get the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine?

I’ll start by saying that I believe in the measles vaccine. I was vaccinated as a child. I’ve vaccinated my two kids. I don’t identify as particularly vaccine skeptical. I’ve sometimes wondered why we have so many vaccine recommendations in this country compared with others, but I generally trust my doctors and haven’t (yet) experienced any major adverse reactions that might have led me to question that trust.

Over the last two years of making the podcast “Why Should I Trust You?,” my co-hosts and I have had the privilege of speaking with many people who feel quite differently than I do about vaccines, public health, and the medical system, and what they see as blind faith in expertise.

I’ve learned a tremendous amount. These conversations have made me question my own assumptions and better understand why I trust institutions, while sometimes realizing that I, too, harbor mistrust. While some of these conversations have been with people who are unwaveringly anti-vaccine, most have been with people who have taken some vaccines and harbor doubts about others.

They’ve also given me a front-row seat to something else: the MAHA grassroots movement’s astonishing ability to organize.

I’ve watched as MAHA supporters rally around pesticides, toxic pollutants in our air and water, microplastics, and PFAS (aka “forever”) chemicals. I’ve been to conferences and meetings, and been included in many group chats and Zoom meetings, where you can practically feel the energy around you: people exchanging ideas, meeting with lawmakers to draft legislation, and mobilizing others to organize local action. I constantly hear two questions: “What do you need? How can I help?”

That energy doesn’t seem to have disappeared after the midterms. But it’s definitely looking for places to go.

MAHA is politically weak right now. Candidates backed by the movement suffered significant electoral defeats, and there’s anger at the Trump administration over what seem like broken promises on pesticides, corporate influence, and environmental health. Yet the grassroots supporters haven’t given up. They’ve already been regrouping around issues like data centers and their effects on communities, while continuing to fight toxic pollutants and corporate influence. They don’t want to wait for the government or medical establishment to fix problems for them.

Measles presents a different kind of challenge. Public health officials need to reach parents who hesitate before vaccinating their children, including families in communities that view government institutions with suspicion.

I’ve heard from public health officials that many local health departments are so underfunded, they struggle to reach these families — and even when they have the resources, they may lack the cultural fluency or trusted relationships to connect with them.

But what if some of the people who could help actually share those families’ skepticism about pharmaceutical companies, admire Kennedy, or prefer alternative medicine — yet believe the MMR vaccine is worth getting?

MAHA is not a fringe movement. According to KFF’s analysis of the 2026 midterm election, roughly 3 in 10 voters said they support MAHA, including 21% who strongly support it.

And, perhaps surprisingly, most MAHA-supporting parents vaccinate their kids. A separate KFF/Washington Post survey found that 86% of MAHA-supporting parents believe it’s important for children in their communities to be vaccinated against measles. Nearly 8 in 10 expressed confidence in the safety of the MMR vaccine. And about 75% reported that their children were up to date on routine childhood vaccinations, excluding Covid and flu.

Truthfully, very few MAHA supporters I’ve met haven’t taken MMR or vaccinated their children against polio, even if on a delayed schedule. I’ve met plenty who feel differently about Covid, flu, or the hep B birth dose, and certainly there are those who reject MMR. But among rank-and-file supporters, I’ve encountered far fewer MMR skeptics than you might expect from listening to some of the movement’s most prominent influencers. Which is important for the challenge at hand: You can believe deeply in MAHA’s core concerns about food, pesticides, water, chronic disease, and even have mistrust in flu and Covid shots — but still have confidence in the value of the measles vaccine.

We don’t acknowledge this enough. Some of MAHA’s most prominent vaccine skeptics may not speak for the broader movement when it comes to MMR.

The science on MMR is clear: Two doses are approximately 97% effective at preventing measles. But knowing the facts and trusting the person delivering them are two very different things. A fact like that can land differently when it comes from someone who has wrestled with the same doubts you have.

I think about Roberta Devers, a mother and midwife practicing in Lancaster County, Pa., whom my colleagues and I met working on an episode about the measles outbreak there.

Roberta was vaccine skeptical, a Kennedy supporter, and chose not to vaccinate her son when he was a baby. But after seeing what measles was doing to people in Lancaster County, she changed her mind. She believes MMR is effective against measles and encourages Amish families in outbreak areas to get vaccinated, too. She tells me she now gets one or two calls a week from families who want to get vaccinated.

What’s particularly interesting to me, though, is what hasn’t changed.

Roberta still admires Kennedy. She hasn’t suddenly decided the medical establishment deserves her trust. If anything, she tells me the measles outbreaks are a reflection of a lack of trust communities continue to have for health systems. One of her ongoing campaigns is to fight for home-birth midwives to receive the same recognition as those working in hospitals. She talks about holistic living, alternative medicine, eating whole foods, even using brown paper toilet paper because she believes in living close to the earth. She hasn’t changed her values or worldview. She’s simply reconsidered what she believes about measles vaccination.

Imagine a vaccine-hesitant parent hearing from someone like Roberta, who shares many of their concerns but has seen measles up close and reached a different conclusion. Roberta doesn’t need to defend pharmaceutical companies or persuade anyone that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention is always right. Just telling her story could lead to a conversation that perhaps a public health official would have a harder time brokering.

I’m not suggesting this movement will suddenly lead the charge on getting Americans vaccinated for measles. Some of MAHA’s most influential voices have spent years not just questioning, but actively sowing distrust in vaccines, including MMR, using claims not rooted in scientific fact. And I expect for many, encouraging vaccination, even during an outbreak, would be a bridge too far for them, a betrayal of their principles.

And organizing a rally isn’t the same as running a public health response.

But shouldn’t we at least try to make an active effort to reach MAHA supporters who believe in MMR? Because the polling shows it looks like there are a lot of them.

In his statement, Kennedy said his department is working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust. He also promised to collaborate with trusted health care providers and community leaders.

He could start by elevating voices who support MMR. People like Devers, who can speak to vaccine-hesitant families with credibility and trust that public health institutions often lack. Find more people like her.

Then encourage those MAHA supporters who trust the measles vaccine to organize conversations about measles prevention. Bring in trusted physicians or other clinicians to answer questions. But also listen to families’ fears and doubts — because chances are, many MAHA supporters have wrestled with those same questions themselves. Help families find vaccines. Connect them with parents who share their values but have decided to vaccinate their children.

Would everyone in MAHA welcome that effort? Almost certainly not. Would Kennedy face backlash from the anti-vaccine wing of his movement? Very likely.

But for the past two years, MAHA supporters have pushed back against being characterized as anti-vaccine. Instead, they say, they are concerned about mandates, informed consent, conflicts of interest, and what they see as an unwillingness to acknowledge questions about safety.

Well, guys, here’s an opportunity to demonstrate what that distinction means in practice.

If you’re not anti-vaccine, helping people make informed decisions about a vaccine that the overwhelming majority of your own supporters trust shouldn’t betray your principles. It should express them.

The public health establishment has a responsibility here, too. If people within MAHA are willing to help, officials should work with them. That doesn’t mean overlooking misinformation or compromising on evidence. But it does mean recognizing common ground.

I know some readers will think I’m naive. Doesn’t she get that parts of MAHA have contributed to the distrust that makes measles prevention so difficult? I do. But even if parts of MAHA contributed to the problem, isn’t it worth asking whether some of its supporters might be particularly well positioned to contribute to the solution?

At a moment when MAHA is regrouping after an election rout, the movement now has an opportunity to demonstrate that it truly offers a big tent. Not by abandoning its core priorities, but by directing some of its powerful grassroots energy toward a disease affecting American children and their parents — the very people MAHA claims to put at the center of its mission.

Brinda Adhikari is a journalist and co-host of the “Why Should I Trust You?” podcast.
