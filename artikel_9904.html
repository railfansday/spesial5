The token factory never sleeps

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

SPONSORED FEATURE: Agentic AI turns inference into a round-the-clock production job. Here's what it takes to keep the tokens flowing in the datacenter you already have.

Agentic AI has supercharged demand for inference infrastructure. According to Goldman Sachs, token consumption is set to jump 24-fold to 120 quadrillion tokens per month between this year and 2030. Enterprises, wary of frontier model sticker shock, are now building their own GPU estates to cope.

Those enterprises shouldn't blindly follow hyperscalers' lead when taking AI inference kit into production. The indicators of success for such companies are different than those for a frontier model provider, and so are the constraints. DIY AI infrastructure at the enterprise level has to earn ROI quickly, meaning that enterprises must focus on sustained operation. That makes engineering criteria such as uptime, heat and power, service windows, and deployment times doubly important. The performance benchmarks that hyperscalers focus on won't matter nearly as much to the average bank or retailer.

ASUS is presenting the ASUS NVIDIA HGX Rubin System, built on the NVIDIA HGX Rubin NVL8 platform, as a worked example of sustained AI inference infrastructure. It announced two models at NVIDIA GTC Berlin in October: the 3U hybrid-cooled XR R1I12B-M1BHFS and the 2U fully liquid-cooled XR R1I12B-M1BLFS.

Agents represent a hockey stick curve for token consumption because an agent isn't a single prompt. It loops until the job's done, often spawning other versions of itself in the process. This is how it breaks a goal into steps and works through them iteratively.

While training is a batch workload, agent inference is a production service that runs around the clock and only gets bigger. NVIDIA is making each generation of systems more powerful than the last to keep up with the demands of both.

Running at scale, day after day, is another matter. A useful way to think about the infrastructure that does it is as a token factory: it takes in power, data and GPU compute and turns out tokens. What counts is throughput, meaning how many tokens, and how many users, the same power and floor space can serve, hour after hour.

Enterprises are moving ahead quickly with their AI factory projects. According to Deloitte's AI infrastructure survey, published in March, 64 percent of respondents have started limited or at-scale AI factory deployments, while 88 percent expect to do so by 2028. They are spending more on AI infrastructure to do it: 86 percent expect their budgets to increase over the next three years.

Enterprises have different problems from hyperscalers and frontier model companies, though. Model builders often get to design greenfield campuses around their GPUs. Conversely, enterprises are often working with small operations teams in existing datacenters that may not have been built with AI in mind. That means they need to think harder about maintenance windows, existing management tooling and predictable deployment. And they'll often be retrofitting halls whose racks rarely draw more than 30 kW.

In token factory terms, the ASUS NVIDIA HGX Rubin System is the core compute engine. Each system puts eight NVIDIA HGX Rubin GPUs on the NVIDIA HGX Rubin NVL8 platform, linked by sixth-generation NVLink. ASUS has designed its equipment to support the constraints above. Its customers can choose between the hybrid and fully liquid-cooled models, and they can opt for partial or full rack configurations by buying multiple trays. This enables them to match their facilities' needs.

The other difference between model providers and enterprises lies in which numbers they care about. Peak performance is different from sustained performance. The first is a specification sheet number, while the second is what the GPU actually delivers hours into a heavy workload.

Production inference tends to run continuously, especially when it uses agents, as opposed to training, which is a sporadic job. So the sustained figure is the one that buyers pay for. Heat becomes a real issue here because when cooling can't keep up, GPUs throttle their clocks to protect themselves. In a token factory, a throttled GPU is a slower production line.

The more advanced the architecture becomes, the higher the stakes get. NVIDIA HGX Rubin GPU has raised them to their highest point, delivering up to 400 petaflops of NVFP4 inference per eight-GPU board, according to NVIDIA's sparse specification. This is screamingly fast, but the increased heat per board means less margin before throttling.

ASUS designed both models to put direct-to-chip liquid cooling on every GPU. Only the 3U unit leaves the CPUs on air cooling. ASUS also says it puts every configuration through a 168-hour stress test.

As boards increase their compute density, pushing up the power and heat requirements, planning naturally has to move out of the server room and into the facility. That's the case whether you're talking about an entire NVL72 rack or a stack of eight-GPU servers like the ASUS systems.

There are other things to consider beyond heat. Few datacenters run racks drawing more than 30 kilowatts, says the Uptime Institute. Yet a single liquid-cooled tray of the ASUS systems draws up to 23.9 kilowatts, and a full rack of nine has a power budget of about 216 kilowatts.

Floor loading and power distribution matter as much as cooling.

NVIDIA HGX Rubin GPU raises the stakes again. The eight-GPU format enables organizations to buy their computing power in more granular steps. That's where the two ASUS systems come into their own. With few enterprises needing a training cluster at NVL72 scale, the advantage is that an HGX box lets them buy AI compute in digestible pieces.

The ASUS systems also come with a 54V busbar and cable-free board-to-board design. ASUS says this simplifies assembly and improves reliability.

So that's space, power and heat. But a GPU board won't produce many tokens if the network, storage or operational tooling can't feed it properly. Agent workloads move context and data all the time, so east-west bandwidth and local storage are key to effective utilization. Any new equipment should ideally slot straight into the enterprise's existing management and monitoring tooling.

ASUS ships the whole rack, including servers, networking, power and storage, as a single blueprint. ASUS pre-validates this AI Factory reference architecture at rack scale so that organizations can plan for future growth. That includes dedicated management nodes and a management switch.

Inside each tray, ASUS has sized the data paths to keep the GPUs fed. Eight OSFP ports on NVIDIA ConnectX-9 give every GPU 800Gb/s of east-west bandwidth to the rest of the cluster, and an NVIDIA BlueField-4 DPU adds two 400GbE ports. Within the board, the eight GPUs talk to each other over NVLink at 3,600GB/s, ASUS says. The 2U tray takes up to 4TB of DDR5 system memory and has eight E1.S NVMe bays for fast local data access, while the 3U model offers a choice of U.2 or E1.S drives.

The other issue for enterprises putting AI inference into production is service and maintenance. In a liquid-cooled box, routine fixes can mean draining a coolant loop, which is downtime, and lost token output, that most enterprises can't afford, especially when dealing with non-batch workloads such as inferencing. So ASUS set out to make components quicker to swap.

On the 2U fully liquid-cooled model, ASUS did it with independent, tool-less CPU and GPU sleds, each with its own direct-to-chip cooling. On the 3U hybrid, ASUS says the air-cooled CPU sled cuts maintenance time by up to 50 percent.

Services aren't just about server maintenance. They're also about decreasing the time to value for AI. Most enterprises won't have in-house rack-scale GPU integration skills, which is why pre-validation and design services are so important.

ASUS says its services run from early facility assessment through to operations. Its professional services cover five offerings: infrastructure design, deployment, performance optimization, AI platform development and lifecycle management. For rapid deployment, ASUS uses its own automation platform, the ASUS Infrastructure Deployment Center (AIDC).

So, with an enterprise token factory judged on how reliably it keeps producing rather than on peak FLOPS, in-house AI deployment teams will be back to focusing on the basics, albeit while dealing with an innovative technology. That means they'll be asking questions about sustained performance, heat and power in existing facilities. They'll be looking at integration at the rack level and with management software, along with service windows and deployment time. ASUS is positioning itself as a partner that can answer those questions with the ASUS NVIDIA HGX Rubin System, which it's making available this quarter.
