'GTA 6' Just Exposed Gaming’s Biggest Awards Problem

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Hannah covers horror, sci-fi, and games at Collider, where she writes news and features, reviews new releases, and interviews the people who make them. She’s helping build out Collider’s gaming coverage, with Pokémon as one of her specialties. If she isn’t writing about a monster, she’s probably watching one on a tape from her VHS collection.

There’s something very funny about being able to vote for Grand Theft Auto 6 for Game of the Year before being able to play it. Apparently, spending years waiting for Rockstar’s next game wasn’t enough anticipation. Now players get to wonder whether it deserves the biggest award in gaming while they’re still waiting for their preorders to unlock. It could be a masterpiece. It could be a masterpiece, and based on the reviews, it might actually justify every bit of excitement surrounding it. Surely, then, a game that good could survive being considered at next year’s ceremony.

On November 17, GTA 6 received a Game of the Year nomination two days ahead of its November 19 release. It joins Control Resonant, Resident Evil Requiem, 007 First Light, Phantom Blade Zero, and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time in the category. That’s a lineup that opens up plenty of opinions already, but Rockstar’s inclusion creates a question that has very little to do with whether the game is good: why are nominations being announced before all the eligible games are even widely available? The answer involves early access, voting deadlines, and an eligibility window that makes this perfectly acceptable, all of which explains how it happened without making it seem any less silly.

The Game Awards’ rules require games to be commercially available by November 20 at 9 p.m. PT, which puts GTA 6 inside the window by a single day. Publishers are also allowed to provide advance copies so voting outlets can evaluate games before submitting their ballots. There’s no special exception required here. The process already accommodates a game receiving a nomination while customers are still waiting to buy it. That's a distinction that's important to make, because the argument falls apart if it becomes an accusation that Rockstar somehow cheated. A publisher releasing an eligible game and providing access to critics is doing what the rules allow. The more frustrating part is that everyone can follow those rules and still end up here. An award intended to celebrate the year’s achievements has selected its contenders before the year it’s assessing has technically finished.

Advance review access makes sense. Games can take dozens of hours to finish, and readers deserve useful criticism when deciding whether to spend their money. Reviews have a reason to meet a release date, but A Game of the Year nomination doesn’t have that same responsibility. Nobody needs a trophy campaign underway to decide whether to pick up GTA 6, and Rockstar is hardly struggling to make people aware that it exists or excited to play the best-rated game in history. Putting it into the next awards cycle would give players time to experience what the jury is celebrating, and it would also give the game time to establish a reputation beyond the extraordinary excitement of finally being available. If that sounds like a disadvantage, it’s worth asking why an award for quality should depend so heavily on catching a game at its most exciting.

Four people, four completely different ways of being somebody's family. Here is your seat at the table, what the rest of them would rely on you for, and the thing you would have to watch.

An early release has a long road to awards season. Players finish it, talk about the ending, replay their favorite sections, and start noticing the things that bothered them. The side quests that seemed endless in a good way might eventually feel endless in a much less flattering way. An opening that promised something extraordinary might turn out to be the best part. The opposite happens, too. A game can become more impressive with distance, when a story’s choices have had time to settle or another playthrough reveals how carefully everything was put together. Having months to think about a game can strengthen the case for it, it just doesn’t tend to generate the same noise as a massive new release finally arriving.

A November game enters that conversation under very different circumstances. People are still discovering its surprises and sending their friends messages about whatever ridiculous thing just happened. That excitement is part of what makes games wonderful, but it also makes comparison difficult. One contender is being judged against the memory of an experience while another is still delivering it.

GTA 6 takes that imbalance a step further because its nomination was announced before the public could begin having the experience at all. Whatever the voting outlets had played, most of the audience was still working from trailers, previews, and expectations. The nomination became another reason to get excited about the launch, alongside everything Rockstar had already done to sell it. None of that proves an earlier release deserves to win instead. Resident Evil Requiem obviously doesn’t become the better game between the two just because it has been around longer, and GTA 6 doesn’t become worse because it arrived later in the year. But the ceremony should be interested in giving those games a fair comparison, especially when the calendar is something it can actually control.

The actual problem here is actually pretty easy to address: close the eligibility window before nomination ballots are due. Let every qualifying game reach players before the contenders are announced. Games released afterward can compete the following year, when their developers’ work will still exist and their best moments will still be worth celebrating. That won’t make every nomination universally beloved. Nothing could. This is the gaming industry, after all, and nothing will ever be universally beloved, even GTA 6. It won’t erase recency bias, either, but it would stop the ceremony from asking its audience to vote on a field that wasn’t fully available when it was selected. That seems like a fairly reasonable expectation for an event built around celebrating games people love.

Rockstar may have made something extraordinary, and its developers could deserve every award coming their way. But a game this good shouldn’t need to squeeze into an eligibility window before players can even get their hands on it. If GTA 6 is the achievement its reviews suggest, its case would still hold up next year. The biggest award in gaming should go to the game that stays with people even after they've finished it, and anything worthy of that honor can surely survive the wait until next year.
