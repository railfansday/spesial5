What the EU’s Plan to Abandon the Green Claims Directive Means for Fashion

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

On November 4, the European Commission placed the proposed Green Claims Directive on a list of legislation it intends to withdraw within six months. The proposal would have set common rules for substantiating and independently verifying voluntary environmental claims. Its planned withdrawal comes just five weeks after separate anti-greenwashing rules began applying across the EU, leaving fashion brands with continued obligations and less direction on how claims should be proven.

The November listing confirms an intention the Commission first signaled in June 2025, prompting the Council to cancel a planned final round of negotiations. The proposal remains formally pending until the withdrawal process is completed, although its revival appears unlikely. The Commission says efforts to advance it during 2025 failed and that it is unlikely to secure sufficient support from member states within a reasonable timeframe.

If withdrawn, the directive’s prescribed substantiation and pre-publication verification process would never take effect, sparing companies some upfront costs. Existing obligations under the EU Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive, or EmpCo, and broader consumer law would remain, leaving companies to interpret the rules and carry the legal risk.

EmpCo has already strengthened EU consumer law against greenwashing, including restrictions on unqualified claims such as “green” and “eco-friendly”, sustainability labels that lack a qualifying certification scheme or public authority behind them, and product claims of carbon neutrality based on offsetting. It also requires claims about future environmental performance to be supported by a detailed implementation plan with measurable targets and regular independent verification.

The Green Claims Directive would have gone further by prescribing how companies should substantiate explicit environmental claims — for example, that a garment has a lower carbon footprint or uses less water — and requiring independent verification before those claims reached consumers. Its withdrawal could leave brands with fewer mandatory steps but less certainty about the evidence regulators will expect.

For fashion, the question of proof travels upstream. Consumer-facing claims often depend on data from textile mills, garment factories and raw-materials suppliers; as brands develop their own evidence requirements, manufacturers serving several customers could face a sprawling patchwork of reporting demands.

Withdrawing the directive would eliminate a prescribed substantiation and verification process, along with some of its upfront costs. Companies would still need to assess their claims and supporting evidence under EmpCo and existing consumer law, placing more of the judgment and legal risk inside the business, leading to uncertainty.

That uncertainty is already being felt inside fashion companies. One sustainability executive at a fashion brand, speaking on background because they were not authorized to discuss the company’s regulatory preparations, says the rapid succession of EU rules has left substantial room for interpretation, making compliance difficult even for companies acting in good faith.

Though brands would avoid some of the initial costs associated with the proposed verification regime, they will still need to identify potentially risky claims, assess whether the underlying evidence supports them and decide when further substantiation, qualification or other safeguards are necessary. That analysis will happen claim by claim, with national regulators and courts interpreting the common EU rules as cases emerge.

The attorneys say companies should hold evidence that directly relates to the claim, remains current and can be independently verified. EmpCo provides clearer boundaries around prohibited practices, but it does not supply the comprehensive methodology that the Green Claims Directive was intended to establish.

That leaves room for differences among EU member states. EmpCo establishes a common EU legal baseline, and the attorneys expect national approaches to remain broadly similar. Member-state regulators and courts could still apply those rules differently to individual claims, particularly before further guidance and case law emerge.

The European Commission could reduce some of that uncertainty through guidance and coordination, but it cannot fully recreate the detailed, harmonized framework envisioned by the abandoned proposal, the attorneys add.

Consumer advocates are particularly concerned about the loss of pre-publication verification. EmpCo enables authorities to challenge prohibited or misleading claims after they enter the market. The Green Claims Directive would have required covered claims and labels to clear an independent check beforehand.

National enforcement authorities already face limited resources and an enormous volume of environmental marketing, say Blanca Morales, head of sustainable consumption and production at the European Consumer Organisation, known as BEUC, and Patrycja Gautier, its head of consumer rights and product safety.

Assessing the evidence behind a claim can require technical expertise and lengthy investigation. In the meantime, the marketing remains visible. Claims can circulate for years before authorities intervene, Morales and Gautier say, by which point “the harm is already done”.

The absence of a common methodology also makes comparison harder. Companies may use different calculations to describe the environmental footprint of clothing, leaving shoppers unable to assess products on equivalent terms. The problem is particularly acute in fashion, where retailers and platforms have introduced an array of proprietary sustainability scores, labels and filters, says BEUC.

The planned withdrawal could therefore reduce certainty for companies as well as consumers. EmpCo prohibits generic environmental claims unless a company can demonstrate recognized environmental performance and creates specific rules for labels, future targets and offset-based marketing. It provides less direction on the methodology companies should use to substantiate other explicit claims.

The enforcement picture could still evolve. On November 11, the Commission separately proposed changes to the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation that would allow it to investigate major cross-border infringements directly and impose fines of up to 6% of a company’s worldwide annual turnover. The proposal could strengthen enforcement against EU-wide greenwashing, but it must still pass through the legislative process.

Large fashion companies are unlikely to dismantle their sustainability systems because one proposed directive disappears.

“Our approach to sustainability is grounded in long-term commitments, not individual regulatory requirements,” says Jeff Hogue, head of global sustainability at Gap Inc. The company will continue complying with applicable rules and believes environmental claims should be “clear, credible and supported by evidence”, he adds. Gap also favors consistent standards across markets, which it says help companies communicate progress while maintaining consumer trust.

Those long-term programs still depend on information from suppliers. Brands will likely continue requesting data on materials, energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions, driven by corporate sustainability strategies, investor expectations, public pressure and other regulatory mandates, says Dr. Sheng Lu, professor and director of graduate studies in fashion and apparel studies at the University of Delaware.

That dependence creates legal exposure across the supply chain. A brand can face scrutiny under consumer-protection and advertising laws if its environmental claim is misleading or inadequately substantiated, even when the underlying information came from a supplier, says Meaghan Colligan Hembree of DLA Piper. An upstream supplier may separately face exposure over representation made to the brand and its contractual obligations. How that risk is allocated will depend on the facts and the agreements between the parties.

With the Green Claims Directive’s proposed verification system unlikely to take effect, contracts and internal governance could carry greater weight. Hembree expects brands to scrutinize what suppliers are asked to represent, the substantiation accompanying that information, their audit and verification rights, how environmental data is updated and who bears responsibility for inaccuracies.

“From a litigation standpoint, that paper trail can become critically important in understanding who knew what, what the ultimate claim was based on, and where the information may have broken down,” she says.

Those requirements could vary by buyer. Large fashion companies already use their commercial leverage to determine which certifications, calculations and disclosures suppliers must provide, Lu says. Manufacturers serving several customers may then have to satisfy multiple standards and reporting systems, adding to the cost of collecting and sharing data.

The burden will be particularly difficult for smaller suppliers, brands and exporters to absorb. Smaller brands and exporters without dedicated European legal teams may struggle to adapt their claims and evidence across national markets. Suppliers face a different burden: meeting multiple buyers’ data requirements, often without the staff or systems to do so, Lu says.

Regulatory uncertainty could also shape sourcing decisions. Lu expects fashion companies to consolidate their supplier bases as they seek manufacturers with the infrastructure, data and trust needed to support environmental claims. Strategic supplier relationships will become more valuable when brands need reliable information extending from raw materials through production.

The early evidence may appear in product descriptions and sourcing questionnaires rather than corporate targets. Lu will be watching whether brands replace broad sustainability language with narrower, measurable claims, alongside changes in the data they require suppliers to provide.

Fashion companies should not wait for enforcement actions to establish the boundaries, the Loyens & Loeff attorneys say. They recommend reviewing environmental and social claims, checking whether sustainability labels meet the rules already in force, aligning language across consumer communications and creating processes to document and regularly reassess the supporting evidence.

“The framework may still develop, but the compliance obligation is already there,” the attorneys say, “and so are the risks of regulatory scrutiny, sanctions and civil litigation.”
