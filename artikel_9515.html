Grab A Box Of Cake Mix To Make A British-Style Dessert In Half The Time

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Britain is home to some pretty controversial meals. There's the divisive beans on toast and the even more polarizing jellied eels. But one thing even the biggest haters can't fault Britain on is its dessert game, which is undeniably top-tier. There are countless cozy, comforting classics to choose from, like apple crumble, bread and butter pudding, and the elegant Victoria sponge. But one staple you can whip up using a boxed mix is a cake version of sticky toffee pudding.

Unapologetically indulgent, sticky toffee pudding has earned itself a spot in the British food hall of fame. But making it at home isn't as simple as making your typical sponge. Traditionally, the recipe starts with soaking and blending dates. After that comes the batter, the baking, and the toffee sauce, which all add up to a good couple of hours. If you fancy a quick fix, a boxed mix — we ranked popular brands from worst to best — gets you most of the way there in a fraction of the time, since the flour, sugar, and leavening agents are already pre-measured, taking the guesswork out of getting a reliably fluffy sponge.

The shortcuts don't stop there. To get that classic date flavor without the soaking and blending, swap in store-bought date paste instead. The dates are already crushed, so the paste can be stirred straight into the batter as the ultimate natural sweetener. The one thing a mix can't cover is the toffee sauce, but that's the easiest part anyway. Butter, brown sugar (ideally muscovado), and cream only need a few minutes of simmering to thicken and turn glossy, so you can whip it up while the cake bakes.

Sticky toffee pudding is a contender for Britain's favorite dessert and has a famous fan in Gordon Ramsay, who often serves it at his world-famous restaurants. But it's also the subject of a long-running debate that revolves around the dates. Recipes vary on whether the dates should be blended or simply soaked and stirred in chopped. Blended, they disappear seamlessly into the sponge, whereas chopped, they make their presence known with a chewy bite.

Fortunately, there's no right or wrong answer here, and if you enjoy more texture, you can swap the date paste for whole dates by pitting and roughly chopping them, soaking them in hot water (or coffee) until soft, then folding them into the batter just before it goes into the oven. The soaking softens the fruit so it turns jammy rather than leathery, and you can use some of the flavorful leftover liquid in place of part of the water the mix calls for.

Over in the U.K., sticky toffee pudding is often served with a scoop of ice cream, and the flavor is almost always vanilla. It gets popped on top of the warm, freshly baked sponge, where the hot and cold contrast adds a new layer to every bite. As it melts, it mingles with the toffee sauce to form a creamy, almost custardy pool that'll leave you licking your plate. You can experiment with other flavors, but there's no arguing that sweet, vanilla-forward ice cream is the perfect counterweight to the rich muscovado sugar and cooked-down toffee, cutting through the intensity so the pudding never turns cloying.
