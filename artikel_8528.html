Personal Investments • Re: Choosing maturity dates without date-specific needs

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

My husband and I are in our late fifties, early retired, own our home, no kids, no desire for a more lavish lifestyle. Short of serious global upheaval or similar, we have well more than enough money for life. We’re invested in generally boglehead-aligned ways.

Though our horizon is long, our cushion good, and market swings don’t keep us up at night, we now feel that more money wouldn’t make us happier, and losing a lot would suck. So, we want to move a fair bit out of the market into something conservative. I’ve been thinking CDs, fixed deferred annuities, or treasuries (probably held to maturity). But all of those require choosing a maturity date. And investments that don’t have a maturity date don’t lock in the interest rates that I’d like to take advantage of.

Question 1: Is there a better choice, that’s about as conservative, and about as advantageous in terms of locked-in rates, but that doesn’t have a pre-specified maturity date (or dates, plural, in the case of an income annuity)?

Question 2: If not, then how do you choose maturity dates for CDs, fixed deferred annuities or treasuries when there’s no anticipatable year that the money will be needed? We’ve laddered them before, when there were years we expected to need the money. But now this is money that, if we ever touch it, will be at some undeterminable time, e.g., for long-term care, total failure of social security, or another major and unpredictable expense. Otherwise, it will mostly go to charity when we die.
We are in a somewhat similar situation with an uncertain time horizon for tapping bond funds. After reading Bernstein, etc., I just found that a shorter overall effective duration worked for us. We're about two-thirds in short-term funds and one-third in intermediate funds (both mainly in Treasuries). We also have an ultrashort Vanguard corporate fund that provides more yield while limiting corporate risk with a short duration (which can be a negative if rates drop, not likely any time soon). Our overall effective duration in all the funds is about 2.8 years. Adam Grossman provides thoughtful commentary on bonds; this recent column was helpful: https://humbledollar.com/2026/09/structuring-bonds/.

We've also been tinkering with a small ladder of six-month Treasury bills rather than leaving some of the funds in a money market fund.

-- Dennis
