Wedding Budget – Add Up Your Cash Before You Book a Venue

Your wedding budget is the cash you and your families can put in without borrowing. Before you call a single vendor, total your savings, what you’ll save by the date, and what each family will give. Then fit the guest count and every category inside that number, with 10% held back for costs no vendor quoted.

Tour venues before you know your number, and the first one you love sets your budget. When that budget runs past your cash, you either borrow the difference or start cutting after the deposits are paid.

Start with each family, before the first tour, and ask whether they’ll give a set amount or pay for specific things, like the photographer or the rehearsal dinner. A set amount goes into your total, while a bill they pay is one less cost for you to fund. Add the cash you have and what you can save by the date without touching your emergency fund. That total is your budget. Leave out any money you’d borrow, such as a personal loan or a balance you’d carry on a credit card.

Set the guest count next, since catering, the bar, rentals, and the size of venue you need all grow with it. Then pick your top three priorities. Hold back 10% of the total for tips, fees, overtime charges, and anything a quote left out. Split the rest by category, and give your three priorities their shares first. Expect the venue and catering to take the largest shares.

Say you’re planning for 100 guests. You have $16,000 saved, can save $8,000 more by the date, and your parents will give $10,000. That’s $34,000, close to the $34,200 average The Knot’s 2026 Real Weddings Study found for 2025 weddings. Holding back $3,400 leaves $306 a guest for everything else.

Keep one spreadsheet or planning app with every quote, deposit, due date, and who’s paying.

Pay every deposit by credit card. The Better Business Bureau recommends it because a deposit paid by card is easier to dispute than one paid in cash or through a payment app if a vendor closes before your date. Then pay the full statement balance so none of the wedding gets financed.
