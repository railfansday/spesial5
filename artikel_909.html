Open up peer review to early-career researchers

Early-career researchers power science’s engine room and yet they make up a small fraction of peer reviewers. When they are involved in reviewing, their contributions are not always recognized. In some cases, they do the work, but their senior colleagues take the credit. This is not acceptable and the Nature Portfolio journals are working to change it. Researchers at all career stages should have the opportunity to be peer reviewers — especially early-career researchers (ECRs). Now, the first large-scale analysis of the Nature Portfolio Early Career Researcher Co-Review programme reveals the benefits, for researchers and science.

Although senior researchers can provide knowledge of the bigger-picture, ECRs are often closer to the latest techniques for practical experiments and analysing data. They also tend to be more up to date with rapidly changing technologies. Involving ECRs in the peer-review process gives them a necessary voice that can help to shape the scientific record.

At the start of 2025, Nature joined Nature Portfolio’s ECR Co-Review programme (see Nature 641, 560; 2025). This initiative, which began with Nature Reviews journals and later Nature Communications, invites established reviewers to involve an ECR in the process, while retaining responsibility for the final review. Journal editors also provide training to participating ECRs and those who have taken part in co-reviewing are then offered the opportunity to review studies on their own.

The Nature Portfolio analysis finds that the programme is increasing the representation of ECRs among peer reviewers and is helping to bridge the gender gap in research.

Overall, ECRs accounted for 17% of reviewers at Nature and 11% at Nature Communications. The analysis is based on data from 78,661 reviewers and 24,645 research manuscripts submitted to Nature Communications (between 1 April 2024 and 31 July 2025) and to Nature (between 1 February 2025 and 31 July 2025).

More than one-quarter of manuscripts at both journals had at least one reviewer who was a PhD candidate or postdoctoral researcher. Women accounted for around 40% of ECR reviewers compared with 24% of established researchers.

In a survey of around 2,500 established researchers and 2,400 ECRs who participated in the co-review programme, 88% of established researchers said they are more likely to agree to review a manuscript if they can co-review with an ECR. Nearly all of the ECR respondents rated the initiative as an ‘excellent’ or ‘good’ way to involve ECRs and to improve transparency in peer review. Many respondents also reported that they thought participation of ECRs sped up the turnaround times for reviews.

These are promising results, although the analysis also highlights areas in which more progress is needed, such as the inclusion of reviewers from a wider range of countries. Researchers affiliated with the United States made up the largest share of ECR reviewers on both journals: around 42% at Nature Communications and 49% at Nature. The overwhelming majority of the remaining reviewers were also from high-income countries.

Nature Portfolio journals are not alone in creating structured pathways for ECRs to become peer reviewers. Several other publishers have developed initiatives that combine mentoring, training and formal recognition for ECRs.

For the past 15 years, EMBO Press, which publishes five journals including The EMBO Journal and EMBO Reports, has encouraged established researchers to co-review manuscripts with postdocs in their laboratories, while requiring that co-reviewers are recognized. In 2024, EMBO Press, which is run by the European Molecular Biology Organization (EMBO), launched an initiative inviting ECRs with at least two years of postdoc experience to participate in peer reviews on their own. The publisher has been monitoring uptake of the programme and a representative told Nature that the quality of ECR review reports match those of reviews completed by established referees.

In September, the journal FEBS Open Bio (published by Wiley on behalf of the Federation of European Biochemical Societies) launched an effort called the Early Career Reviewer Hub that helps PhD students and postdocs to gain peer-review experience in molecular sciences. Participants in the initiative receive training from the journal’s editors and must nominate established researchers to mentor them. The ECRs review articles known as Research Protocols, which detail experimental procedures. There are other initiatives, too — a positive sign that the publishing community is doing more to involve ECRs in the peer-review process.

Peer review is fundamental to research and provides a way to rigorously evaluate science. It is a process through which researchers learn how to assess their colleagues’ work and, at the same time, learn about how their own articles will be assessed.

However, the current peer-review system is stretched and scientists face ever-increasing demands on their time. Publishers need to ensure that more ECRs have the opportunity to contribute to this key service and that they are recognized for their work. Peer reviewers must reflect the true diversity of research itself.
