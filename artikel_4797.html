AI-tiquette: When Did That Become Okay?

Posted November 5, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

There was a time, not long ago, when asking a machine to write a birthday message would have seemed absurd. Having it compose a condolence note might have felt callous. Sending an artificial intelligence (AI)-written love letter? Surely that crossed a line. Using it to answer a colleague’s thoughtful email bordered on insulting. Now consider the same acts in 2026. From love life via friends to work, generative AI outputs have become all-pervasive. We may still hesitate, yet ever less. Behaviors that once provoked outrage are evolving through a familiar cycle: questionable becomes tolerated; tolerated becomes normal; and gradually normal becomes invisible.

Welcome to AI-tiquette: the rapidly evolving, largely unwritten etiquette governing what we delegate to AI, what we still believe deserves human effort, and how we judge one another as that boundary shifts.

Social norms arise without trumpets. They morph and migrate as behaviors evolve, and contribute to that evolution. Answering a phone at the dinner table once signaled importance; eventually it was regarded as plain discourtesy. Texting instead of calling initially seemed impersonal; today, an unexpected phone call feels intrusive for many. AI accelerates the technological integration process—but it changes something more fundamental than the style in which we communicate. It moderates the amount of human effort behind the communication itself. That process is ongoing, and experiments involving email, texting, dating, and social media reveal how unstable our AI-tiquette is. Initially, many people failed to suspect AI involvement unless the possibility was explicitly raised (or the output was suspiciously spiked with AI-favorites like "profound" or "it is not...it is..."). Nowadays, AI is more and more taken for granted. Once its use is disclosed, however, judgments tend to become negative. Research identifies an “AI penalty,” as AI-mediated messages are perceived as less trustworthy and authentic.

AI-tiquette is a paradox. Concealing assistance can feel deceptive; revealing it can diminish the message. Most people simultaneously believe AI involvement ought to be disclosed, and do not disclose it.

Then there are our manners toward the machine. Do you say “please” to ChatGPT or "thank you" to Claude? Apologize when you change direction? Or type: “Wrong, redo. Shorter." Perhaps it does not matter to the machine. (And yet, politeness toward your bot may actually pay off. Research into human-AI information-seeking conversations suggests that impolite prompts can produce more language while yielding less useful information relative to the energy consumed.) The interesting question is whether it matters to us. Research following repeated human-AI interactions found that politeness toward AI deteriorated over time, and faster than in comparable human-to-human exchanges. Give the AI a more human-like presence and courtesy persists longer. Manners are messages to others, but they are also habits of the human producing them. If commanding, interrupting, and dismissing an infinitely patient artificial subordinate becomes something we practice hundreds of times a week, why assume the behavioral boundary will remain perfectly sealed around the machine?

The deeper etiquette problem begins when another human being is at the other end. Imagine spending time to carefully craft an email to a colleague. She asks AI to summarize it and formulate a response. You receive three polished paragraphs and, pressed for time, ask your AI to summarize them. Two humans have now “communicated” without ever actually encountering what the other truly produced. Everything that is being written has been filtered. Was anyone rude? Your instinctive answer may be yes. Yet our inherited formal etiquette struggles because every visible feature of the exchange may be impeccable. The sentences are courteous, the grammar polished, the response prompt, the content professional. The potential discourtesy lies beneath the words: Attention has been withdrawn from the relationship while its appearance has been preserved. This withdrawal is now increasingly twice removed from humans, as AI agents are communicating on behalf of the sender/receiver.

The tension becomes sharper when emotion is involved. We still use effort as evidence of care. Yet even that norm is becoming porous. AI-assisted writing experiments indicate that people can produce messages perceived as warm and trust-building while spending considerably less time writing them. And research on emotionally engaging conversations suggests that AI-generated responses can produce strong feelings of interpersonal closeness when recipients believe another person wrote them. We are approaching a painful threshold: The signal of care can increasingly be produced independently of the experience and expenditure of care.

Human culture has long attached meaning to effort. A handwritten letter mattered partly because somebody sat down and wrote it. Generative AI removes much of it. Often that is valuable: It can bridge languages, clarify muddled thinking, overcome barriers, and help people articulate something genuinely felt. Yet friction was never merely an inconvenience. Sometimes it was part of how value was created. If everyone can generate elaborate proposals, comments, images, and responses on demand, abundance may gradually drain those outputs of informational and emotional value. The emerging breach of AI-tiquette may be surprisingly old-fashioned: wasting somebody else’s time. It is an exploit which we now master at industrial scale. In an attention-scarce environment, it is an insult that will backfire as our recipients will simply learn to ignore us.
