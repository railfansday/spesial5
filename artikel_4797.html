Dog-ma: Jung, Synchronicity, and the Beach Yoga Class That Was Ruff

I sat cross-legged in the sand, doing my best impression of someone with their thoughts under control, when the yoga instructor said the line every meditator has heard a thousand times: Your mind is like a puppy. It wanders off; you gently bring it back. It wanders off again; you bring it back again. No yelling. No drama. Just patient redirection, over and over, forever, amen.

And at that exact moment—not as a vague afterthought, but right then—a woman 20 feet away in front of the class, behind the instructor, and in front of the ocean, was locked in a full-contact wrestling match with an actual dog on an actual leash. Pulling. Tugging. The dog wanted the water, she wanted the boardwalk, and neither of them was interested in gentle redirection. It was the least zen scene on the entire beach, unfolding in real time as a soundtrack to a lecture about zen.

I opened one eye. I am fairly sure I laughed out loud, which is not great yoga etiquette.

Here's the thing about moments like that: You can shrug them off as a coincidence or let them stop you in your tracks. I let this one stop me, because it did something a metaphor alone never quite manages—it made the metaphor physical. I didn't just hear that my mind is like a puppy. I watched a mind-sized puppy misbehave in real time, a very short distance from where I sat trying to master mine.

Carl Jung had a word for this—synchronicity—meaningful coincidences that seem to carry significance beyond ordinary cause and effect. Jung wasn't claiming the universe is a puppeteer pulling strings so your yoga class gets better sound effects. He was pointing at something subtler: that meaning sometimes arrives sideways, through timing, rather than head-on, through explanation.

For decades, that idea lived mostly in Jungian circles, treated as a little too mystical for serious psychology to touch. But it turns out some very serious psychologists have been touching it anyway. Pninit Russo-Netzer and Tamar Icekson, researchers who study meaning and well-being, spent years interviewing people about their synchronicity experiences—the goosebump moments, the "You would not believe what just happened" moments—and out of those interviews they built something researchers love and mystics tend to distrust: a model.

They call it REM, and no, it has nothing to do with sleep. It stands for: Receptiveness—being open, attentive, quietly available to what's happening around you (which, not coincidentally, is exactly what a beach at sunrise with your eyes half-closed is designed to produce). Then an Exceptional encounter—the unexpected event that rhymes with something already stirring inside you. And finally, Meaning-detecting—the moment you consciously connect the two and let it mean something.

Notice that meaning-detecting is a verb, not a lightning bolt. The dog and the puppy metaphor didn't hand me a revelation. I had to reach out and pick it up. Russo-Netzer and Icekson later built a whole scale around this—the Synchronicity Awareness and Meaning-Detecting Scale—and their data suggests something wonderfully unmystical: People who notice these moments and let them land tend to report more optimism, a stronger sense that life holds together, and higher life satisfaction. Not because coincidences are magic, but because noticing them is a skill, and it's a skill that keeps paying you back.

Which brings me to the dog, one more time. The woman eventually won. Or the dog decided the fight wasn't worth it; it's hard to say who negotiated whom. But watching it, I thought: That's what my brain does to me on a slow Wednesday when a worry won't drop the leash. And what the instructor was really teaching wasn't a metaphor about puppies at all. It was a rehearsal for exactly that moment on the beach—so that when my own mind starts pulling toward the water, I remember I'm allowed to just...turn, and walk, and not make it a fight.

I don't think the universe scheduled that dog for my benefit. I think the universe is simply generous with material, all the time, and most of it goes right past us because we're not receptive enough to catch it. That morning, for once, I paid just enough attention to catch the pitch. The dog never did learn to heel. Neither, most mornings, have I. But I’m a tad more hopeful.
