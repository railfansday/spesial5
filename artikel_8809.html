What Top Tri Coaches Fix Between November and March

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Top triathlon coaches share what they have their athletes work on in the off-season. (Photo: FinisherPix/Triathlete, Aaron Palaian, Oliver Baker, and courtesy David Tilbury-Davis)

One of triathlon’s attractions – and frustrations – is that there is always something to work on, and there are few better times to continue that never-ending quest for perfection than during the off-season.

With no race commitments, it gives triathletes the opportunity to step back, assess their strengths and weaknesses, and identify what to work on heading into spring.

For Northern Hemisphere athletes, that time is now. So, to help you along the way, we turned to four of the sport’s leading coaches to get their views — in their words — on what triathletes should focus on between now and March.

One of the world’s leading coaches and applied sports scientists in endurance sport, Plews has coached elite triathletes, including world champions Chelsea Sodaro, Javier Gomez, and Terenzo Bozzone.

He is also the founder of the coaching platform Endure IQ for both athletes and coaches. Plews himself is a former age-group Ironman world champion and still holds the fastest amateur time at the Hawaii Ironman World Championship.

“I never did proper strength training when I was younger, but now that I do a lot more, I’m seeing the benefits. But it is only as good as the quality and intent you put in. One of the big mistakes I see with many triathletes is that there’s no real intent.

“Get someone to help make sure you’re doing proper lifts. If you need to learn the correct movements, winter would be an ideal time. Then strength work needs to challenge the system. We typically talk “reps in reserve” when it comes to strength training [i.e., how many more reps you could complete before reaching failure].

“I’d advise working to two to three reps in reserve, but you can go to four if you’re not confident. But the main thing is you want to see a change in the speed of movement as you fatigue through the set.

“I would focus mostly on compound lifts: barbell hip thrusts, Bulgarian split squats, Nordic hamstring curls, and isometric holds and calf raises to add injury prevention. As time goes on, pair it with more plyometric and ballistic exercises, which might be a squat into a box jump.”

“Winter can be a period where you become unstuck from a psychological point of view. What’s important is to find balance. You want the volume, but don’t want to become stale so that by the time you get to the season, you’ve lost enthusiasm.

“If you really push the envelope early, especially with long, cold rides in January and February, by the time you get through to the season, you’re just sick of it. So, I think there’s a very fine line between doing too much training and not enough.”

The founder and head coach of NVDM Coaching in Austin, Texas, Van Der Merwe leads a team of more than 30 coaches supporting over 700 athletes. A former top-50 world-ranked junior tennis player from South Africa, she raced as a professional triathlete from 2010 to 2023 and has coached since 2009. She also leads Ironman U, Ironman’s official coaching certification program.

“Winter is when we raise the ceiling. We reverse periodize, so short, focused threshold and VO2 work comes before the long volume, because every triathlete races at a percentage of their FTP.

“If an athlete raises FTP from 220 to 235 watts this winter and rides an Ironman at 70% of it, race-day power goes from 154 to about 165 watts. Spring then becomes about extending that fitness.”

“Most athletes book a fit in spring, which leaves little time to adapt before racing. Our order is comfort first, then aerodynamics, then power, because the fastest position is the one you can hold for the whole bike leg and still run well afterward.

“In one study, the lowest torso angle tested cost riders about 16 watts at threshold compared with their own positions. Winter trainer rides are the perfect place to build into a new position gradually.”

“This is one most athletes never think about, and it can quietly derail a whole season. Vitamin D drops through the winter, especially for athletes training indoors at northern latitudes.

“A review of more than 2,000 athletes found over half had inadequate levels. Iron stores matter too, especially for female athletes. We ask athletes to get a baseline panel with vitamin D and ferritin now, so their doctor can address anything before the big training blocks start.”

Head coach at KIS Coaching, Cunnama is a former elite professional triathlete from South Africa who won major long-distance races, including Challenge Roth and Ironman Hamburg, and owns two top-five finishes at the Ironman World Championship in Kona. Following his racing career, he became a presenter with the Global Triathlon Network (GTN), sharing his knowledge and experience with a worldwide triathlon audience.

“Everyone knows they should work on their weaknesses over winter, but that usually translates to a broad goal like, ‘I want to work on my swim,’ which then turns into ‘more swimming, less biking and running.’

“I find it more effective to keep a balance in all three triathlon disciplines, but try to get ahead on the things that you always fell behind on last season. Determine which aspect of your training made you feel most underconfident, and then tweak your off-season training to target that area.

“For example, if you found that using paddles destroyed your arms, do extra paddle work. If hills were your nemesis on the bike, hit the hills (or simulate them on the trainer). Rather than tackling physical limitations, target the mental hurdles – use the off-season to get to a place where you love the sessions you hated last season.”

“It is hard to make changes to technique during the season when you have volume goals in training, because technique changes (or position changes on the bike) require you to only do as much of the new technique as you can sustain, and no more.

“You can’t ingrain a new technique when the second half of your session is falling back to the old technique! So, make those changes in the off-season and slowly build back that technique better. The advantage of triathlon is you have two other sports to keep you fit while you reset your technique in the third sport.”

A highly respected triathlon coach, Tilbury-Davis has worked with professional and age-group athletes since 1995. Through Tilbury-Davis Coaching, he has coached leading athletes such as Lionel Sanders and Ashleigh Gentle. Based in Finland, he works with a small group of international athletes.

“Efficiency of movement sounds vague, but while I’m a big proponent of doing strength and conditioning work throughout the year, winter is a time to understand that swimming, biking, and running all require expression of force from the core – when I say core, I effectively mean from the knees to the chest.

“You’re talking around 64 different muscles and creating the capacity to have a stable base to express force from will improve your body alignment in the water, your ability to maintain an aero position on the bike, and your ability to have hip extension and knee drive in the run. It centers around doing the kind of exercises that are multi-plane, multi-muscle recruiting as part of a several-times-a-week program.

“For example, you see a lot of triathletes train with a Masters group and the swim coach says to do a high elbow or chicken wing drill. The reality is that 99% of triathletes do not have sufficient mobility to swim in a manner that a swim coach would like. So, to step back, I would say to improve your swimming, go away and work on your thoracic and scapular mobility.”

“A lot of people don’t talk about saddle choice. We all buy expensive running shoes that are made out of expensive foam that breaks down over a period of time, but we forget that saddles are also made out of foam.

“If you’re serious about maintaining a good position on the bike, you probably should be changing your saddle once a year and for a pro that might be twice a year because the foam in the saddle breaks down the same way that the foam in a running shoe breaks down.”

“Get used to having to cope with uneven surfaces on the run, shortening and lengthening your stride length, stepping over rocks and roots, dealing with off camber, on camber, dealing with gravel, sand, mud, all of those proprioceptive stimuli that vary significantly improve your body’s ability to be efficient. A worst-case scenario for me would be an athlete that just runs on a treadmill the entire winter at a very narrow bandwidth of running paces.

“A good way of explaining this is that people talk about the way the Kenyans train. They do this famous long run in Iten where it’s a progressively descending pace. But what’s special about that workout is that they’re running on trails, gravel fire roads, wooded areas, and gradient changes. Probably the most powerful thing about that run is that they’re working on different surfaces, different terrains, different obstacles, different gradients, while they’re still working on a physiological stimulus.”

“That’s probably when people ask me what’s the number-one most important session for Ironman training: I would say go run on trails before anything else, because it builds resilience and strength, which is what you need for an Ironman.”
