Scientists found two tiny new fish species deep in the Red Sea, but they have completely disappeared from the wild since the discovery

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Two gobies under an inch long, netted at 98 feet off Saudi Arabia in May 2023, remain the only known specimens of Trimma charon.

On May 15, 2023, Viktor Nunes Peinemann and Matthew Tietbohl Talas of King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST) collected fish during a scuba dive outside a Red Sea cave off Walih Island, Saudi Arabia. The depth was roughly 98 feet (30 meters).

The team nabbed two small gobies at the bottom of the cave entrance, where the fish had been moving over dead carbonate rock.

A paper from September 2026 names them Trimma charon. Other surveys done after 2023 found no other specimens, and the authors can think of only two possible explanations: a truly rare fish, or it has a home on deeper reefs.

The methods section lists clove oil, a fish anesthetic, and hand nets as the only collecting gear.

The site sits in the northern Red Sea at 27.7897°N, 35.1630°E. The reef there is a steep slope that drops past 328 feet (100 meters).

The authors described the bottom as hard carbonate with little structure and few living or recently dead corals, which they called unusual for the Red Sea. The region’s corals are better known for recovering from heat stress that bleaches reefs farther south.

Peinemann photographed the paratype fresh from the water in an upright photo tank, using a Nikon Z7 body, a 105 mm macro lens and a Godox flash. Almost all the body color faded later in preservative, the paper says, and only the eye kept a faint trace of its pattern.

Both fish went to the ichthyology collection of the Royal Ontario Museum, under catalog numbers ROMI118236 and ROMI118237. The larger one, 0.66 inch (16.8 millimeters) long without its tail fin, is the holotype, the specimen that formally defines the species. The second, the paratype, measures 0.48 inch (12.1 millimeters).

The paper also thanks the captain and crew of the research vessel R/V Al-Azizi for support during field operations.

In life the goby is translucent, with a broad orange-red stripe running from the eye to the base of the tail. Three narrow pale bars interrupt the stripe, and each iris is split into four uneven sectors of white and orange.

The tail carries the feature that gave the fish its name. Peinemann described the golden spots in a statement from the publisher Pensoft. “They cover most of the fin and resemble small gold coins,” he said.

Charon is the ferryman of Greek mythology, who carried the souls of the dead across the river Styx in exchange for coins. The acknowledgements credit Jesse Cochran with suggesting the name.

Peinemann also connected the name to the reefs below the dive limit, saying those dark, deeper and unexplored reefs could be seen as a kind of underworld.

Before this description, scientists recognized 10 species of Trimma in the Red Sea. The new fish raises that count to 11, and at least nine appear to live only in that region.

That makes Trimma the most species-rich goby genus in the Red Sea, the authors wrote, though Gobiodon, Eviota and Hetereleotris are close behind. Peinemann and his colleagues also built an illustrated identification key to all 11 species, with photographs of each.

Three other members of the group have been named since 2015. One is Trimma berumeni, a yellow goby known from large caves in the Farasan Banks, also collected near 98 feet (30 meters).

KAUST scientists also described the grumpy dwarfgoby on Red Sea reefs, and small bottom-dwelling species of this kind supply nearly 60 percent of the food eaten by other reef fish.

The closest look-alike is Trimma anaima, a pygmygoby recorded from the Comoros to Fiji but never from the Red Sea. The two share the orange-red stripe, but T. charon has 14 rays in each pectoral fin, while T. anaima usually has 16.

The genetic comparison used COI, a mitochondrial gene often used as a barcode for animal species. Among 93 described Trimma species with available sequences, T. anaima came closest, at 85 to 86 percent identity.

The authors used that gap for a rough calculation. Modern reefs have existed in the Red Sea for only about 5.5 million years, so a split inside that window would need at least about 3 percent COI change per million years.

That pace falls within rates estimated for other small reef gobies. The authors noted that it would still imply a long presence in the Red Sea, and that the fish could instead be more widespread or related to an unsampled lineage.

The size of the record is the main limit of the study. “So far, the entire species is known from only a single encounter and two specimens,” Peinemann said in the Pensoft statement.

The team surveyed comparable habitats at similar depths on other Red Sea reefs and found no more. The fish also never appeared in the rotenone sampling stations of two earlier reef studies, or in a survey that identified fish larvae by their DNA.

Depth complicates that absence. The authors’ surveys ran mostly between the surface and 98 feet (30 meters), along the Saudi coast from the Gulf of Aqaba to the Farasan Banks, and 98 feet was the deepest the team dived for science, according to Pensoft.

The steep wall at Walih Island continues far below that line. The paper raises the possibility that 98 feet is only the shallow edge of the species’ range, with more of the fish on mesophotic reefs, the dim coral zone below normal diving depth.

The paper offers no deep-reef collections to test that idea. Deep work elsewhere has turned up a new reef fish about 400 feet down off Brazil, and the authors called the Red Sea and wider Indian Ocean particularly undersampled at these depths.

The catalog entry for both specimens still gives one locality and one date, outside a cave off Walih Island on May 15, 2023.

The full study was published in the journal ZooKeys.
