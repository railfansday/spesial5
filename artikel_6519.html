Eagles may hunt baboons as heavy as some early human relatives

Tanggal: 2026-11-20

Punctured baboon skulls found beneath cliffs in Chad suggest Verreaux’s eagles may hunt larger primates than scientists once thought possible.

Eagles are known to kill monkeys. A full-grown male baboon, however, has long been considered too heavy for any bird to take.

That view is now challenged by skulls of adult male olive baboons found below desert cliffs in the Sahara. The damage on them suggests that Verreaux’s eagles can kill such animals anyway.

A hole was punched through the bone of the eye socket in each skull. It is the kind of wound that an eagle’s talon leaves. One of the baboons weighed an estimated 48 pounds (21.8 kg).

If the researchers are right, an eagle can kill prey as heavy as some early human relatives.

The study was led by Catherine Hobaiter of the University of St Andrews. She worked with colleagues at Dartmouth College, the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior, and African Parks.

No one on the team saw an eagle attack a baboon. The case rests on bones.

Three sites on the Ennedi Plateau were visited by the team in February and March 2025. The plateau is a sandstone landscape in northeastern Chad.

Among the fallen rock below its cliffs, the skulls of two adult male baboons were found at two different sites.

Neither puncture showed signs of healing, so the damage could have occurred around the time of death or afterward.

“We saw the eagles while we were searching for the baboons, but we never imagined that they might be a threat to them,” Hobaiter said.

“Then we found the first skull with a puncture mark, and then another, and another.”

The right shoulder blade of the first male had been punctured and scraped as well. Eagles leave that pattern when they open a body to reach the heart and lungs.

The team also needed to know how heavy the baboons were. The ball at the top of each thigh bone was therefore measured in scaled photographs.

An established formula for monkeys converts that width into an estimate of body weight.

Both thigh bones were found with the first punctured skull. From them, the team estimated that male’s weight at about 48 pounds (21.8 kg).

These weights matter because of a rule of thumb in research on primates and their predators. Primates under 29 pounds (13 kg) are known eagle prey. No heavier primate has ever been confirmed as an eagle’s kill.

Primates between 29 and 44 pounds (13 and 20 kg) are treated as vulnerable in theory. Anything heavier is assumed to be safe from birds.

A third punctured skull was recovered on a return trip in June 2026, along with part of the skeleton. This male weighed an estimated 58 pounds (26.2 kg), and both of his shoulder blades had been scraped.

He was at least twice as heavy as any primate confirmed as eagle prey.

In all, thigh bones were collected from nine baboons. Eight were adult males with an average estimated weight of 49 pounds (22.2 kg).

A hole in a baboon’s skull does not have to come from an eagle. Other causes were therefore considered by the team.

A gunshot would be expected to do much more damage to the skull, and none was found. Male baboons also kill each other with their canine teeth. Such fights tend to leave crushed bone and extra tooth gouges, yet the skulls had neither.

Verreaux’s eagles are known to dive at baboons that come near their nests. No nest was found above or near the skulls, however.

One possibility was harder to dismiss. Verreaux’s eagles sometimes eat dead animals. The scraped shoulder blade could be explained by an eagle pulling apart a carcass.

The authors wrote that this cannot be definitively ruled out. Two reasons to doubt it were given.

First, the skull punctures are far more severe than the shallow pits seen in an earlier experiment. In that experiment, a golden eagle had gripped a dead monkey.

Second, nearly all the bones came from adult males. An eagle that feeds on carcasses would be expected to eat a dead baboon of either sex.

“At first, the idea of eagles hunting adult male baboons seemed to exist on the thin edge of possibility, until it didn’t,” said Nathaniel Dominy of Dartmouth, the study’s senior author.

An eagle does not have to lift its prey to kill it. Golden eagles land on the back of a hoofed animal and hold on until it collapses. Similar methods are used by Verreaux’s eagles.

Verreaux’s eagles also hunt in pairs. The prey is startled by one bird, and the other attacks from below to force it off the cliff.

The grip of a Verreaux’s eagle can span up to 16 inches (40 cm). That is more than twice the width of an adult male baboon’s skull.

The Ennedi baboons appear to spend much of the day on vertical cliff faces. Some are as tall as 490 feet (150 meters).

Water and palm fruit are found on the canyon floor, but it offers little shade or shelter.

When young baboons are threatened by an eagle, the males bark and lunge at it. The team suggested that this could make males more likely to be killed on a cliff.

On June 19, 2026, the researchers watched a pair of Verreaux’s eagles.

The birds flew along a cliff where baboons were resting. Several baboons gave alarm calls, and those in exposed spots dropped quickly to shelter.

“We’d seen them very relaxed around other big birds of prey, but as soon as the Verreaux came into view they alarm called and dropped down to safety,” Hobaiter said.

The argument reaches past baboons because of a famous fossil. In 1995, researchers argued that the Taung Child had been killed by an eagle.

The Taung Child was a young early human relative.

The punctures in the Ennedi skulls resemble the damage to that fossil’s eye socket. The Taung Child, however, weighed an estimated 22 to 26 pounds (10 to 12 kg) at most.

That weight falls inside the accepted limit for eagle prey.

Research on what hunted larger early human relatives has focused on mammals, especially big cats. Hobaiter’s team argued that eagles deserve attention too.

If eagles can kill adult baboons this large, the authors argue that some early human relatives may also have been vulnerable.

Monkeys defend themselves differently against eagles than against cats, and males often step in during eagle attacks.

Several traits could have been shaped by pressure from eagles, the authors wrote. These include the size difference between males and females, cooperation among males, and infant care.

That part of the argument was called speculative by the authors.

Three punctured skulls point to possible eagle attacks, and baboons sounded alarm calls when a pair of Verreaux’s eagles flew past.

The researchers have yet to see one of these eagles attack an adult baboon.

Nearly all the bones came from adult males, and the authors called that pattern puzzling.

3D scans of the bones have been posted in public archives by the researchers, where others can examine the punctures.

Future fieldwork at Ennedi could show whether eagles kill adult male baboons there, and how often.

The full study was published in the journal American Journal of Biological Anthropology.
