New Jersey utilities have less than a year to implement virtual power plants

Data from the two-year interim version of PowerUp NJ will inform the development of a long-term, open-access virtual power plant tariff, state utility regulators said on Nov. 12.

PowerUp NJ is a “first-of-its-kind statewide virtual power plant program that will give ratepayers more control to drive down their own energy costs,” Sherrill said in a statement that also referred to the plan as a “voluntary community energy program” that allows customers to “lend power from their homes” to support the grid.

The BPU’s order this month cements the program features and goals outlined in a July straw proposal from the agency.

Among other things, the order envisions a market-based framework that compensates distributed energy resources for grid services, rather than subsidizing their deployment; has standard program design elements across all four regulated utility territories; is technology neutral, with eligibility extended to net-metered solar-plus-storage systems; and requires utilities to test localized dispatch with an eye towards developing grid services that could defer or avoid infrastructure upgrades, according to a program factsheet.

The order also establishes technical working groups on measurement and verification, cost-benefit analysis, and program marketing; and aims to coordinate existing utility and state programs and proceedings involving distribution system planning, net metering, energy efficiency and electric vehicles, according to the factsheet.

The BPU expects smart thermostats, stationary home batteries, internet-connected electric water heaters and vehicle chargers and electric vehicles themselves to be eligible for PowerUp NJ at launch. Participating utilities and manufacturers will notify customers of specific eligible devices and models in mid-2027.

PowerUp NJ will be an opt-in program, meaning customers must affirmatively choose to participate. Once enrolled, customers will be able to “choose which devices and events to include” to avoid inconvenient or uncomfortable situations, like ceding control of a thermostat on a hot day, the BPU said.

Like other state-level VPPs, such as California’s Demand Side Grid Support program, PowerUp NJ aims to reduce grid stress and mitigate power price spikes by shifting load away from demand peaks. It will target both “distribution-level grid stress events” and PJM capacity and transmission coincident peaks, according to the program factsheet.

A preliminary staff analysis found that a 3% cut to peak demand could avoid about $60 million in annual system capacity costs while cutting local and greenhouse gas emissions from fossil-fueled peaker plants in New Jersey, the BPU said.

In a statement, BPU President Ben Hertz-Shargel said his agency would apply lessons from the two-year interim version of PowerUp NJ to the development of a long-term framework for the program.

“We will develop this program carefully, learn from real-world results, and expand it over time, growing the earnings opportunities for residential and business customers,” Hertz-Shargel said in a statement.
