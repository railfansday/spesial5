Inca children chosen for sacrifice were given cocaine, alcohol, and ayahuasca before being killed on mountaintops

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Analysis of eight mummified children from the Inca Empire has revealed that they were given coca leaves and other drugs before they were sacrificed.

The frozen bodies of children sacrificed by the Inca Empire over 500 years ago still carry traces of the drugs they were given before they died.

Toxicological analysis of hair and nail samples from eight mummified children has revealed cocaine in every single one. Half showed signs of alcohol consumption.

And a 14-year-old girl found near the summit of a Peruvian volcano carried traces of harmine. Researchers link this hallucinogenic compound to ayahuasca.

The study, led by Dagmara Socha at the University of Warsaw, is the most comprehensive chemical analysis of Inca capacocha sacrifices to date.

Researchers from institutions across Poland, Peru, Argentina, and the United States screened for ten different psychoactive compounds.

The word capacocha comes from Quechua. Capac means royal. Cocha means lake, water, or body.

The ritual of making child sacrifices was among the most sacred in the Inca Empire, which dominated some 2,485 miles (4,000 kilometers) of South America’s western coast from the late 13th century until the Spanish conquest.

The Incas offered their children and young women to mountain deities known as Apus. Martín de Murúa, a Spanish Friar who wrote a 1590 account, said the Inca performed the ceremony to prevent famine, drought, pestilence, rebellion, and a lack of fruits of the earth.

Young women often came from the acllas – virgins designated for the cult of the Sun. After death, these children became revered ancestors. Their families received great honor.

Some relatives went on to serve as priests in the cult of the capacocha mummies, delivering oracles in their name.

The Incas did not choose children for sacrifice at random. Some families volunteered their own children. Conquerors held some as hostages and raised them in the capital, Cusco, or drew others as tribute from conquered territories.

The eight mummies in this study span decades of discovery and thousands of kilometers of Andean geography.

The oldest find is the Chuscha mummy, unearthed by grave robbers in the 1920s on a volcanic peak in Argentina. She was about eight years old.

The most famous is the so-called Ampato Maiden, a 14-year-old girl discovered by Johan Reinhard and Miguel Zarate in 1995 on an artificial platform at 6,300 meters above sea level.

Three more came from Llullaillaco – at 6,715 meters, currently the highest archaeological site on Earth. The remaining mummies were found at the Quehuar and Sara Sara volcanoes.

Seven of the eight Inca child sacrifices were girls. They are thought to have been between six and 14 at the time of death.

Some were likely killed with sharp weapons. Others appear to have been buried alive.

Cocaine and its metabolite benzoylecgonine turned up in every hair sample the team analyzed. That wasn’t entirely surprising since coca leaves were a central element of the capacocha ritual.

Dried coca quids were found inside the mouths of the Llullaillaco Maiden and another sacrificial victim from Cerro Esmeralda. Male figurines buried alongside the children had bulging cheeks – carved to show coca pulp packed inside.

But the chemical signatures varied dramatically between individuals. Cocaethylene, a metabolite produced only when cocaine and alcohol are present in the body simultaneously, appeared in just two cases: the Chuscha mummy and the Llullaillaco children.

The Llullaillaco Maiden’s cocaine concentration was staggering – 16,740 picograms per milligram of hair, roughly 550 times higher than the Ampato #4 individual.

The Chuscha mummy offered something rarer: a timeline. Her hair was long enough to be cut into six 10-millimeter segments, each representing roughly one month of her life.

The cocaine levels fluctuated, but a noticeable decline appeared in the final month before death. Cocaethylene showed up only in that last segment.

The estimated blood ratio of cocaethylene to cocaine was approximately 166 percent. This figure suggests heavy alcohol consumption alongside coca chewing in her final weeks.

The study’s most striking finding came from the Ampato Maiden’s nails. Harmine – a compound found in Banisteriopsis caapi, the primary ingredient of the Amazonian hallucinogenic brew ayahuasca – was detected at low but measurable levels.

No other hallucinogens turned up. That isolation matters.

Harmine is a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. It blocks the metabolism of serotonin and dopamine. Modern clinical studies have shown that a single dose of ayahuasca can produce antidepressant effects lasting several weeks.

The researchers suggest that harmine may have been administered not for its hallucinogenic properties but for its mood-altering ones – a way to calm a child facing death on a frozen mountaintop.

The Banisteriopsis vine grows only in the Amazon rainforest. Its presence at a high-altitude Andean sacrifice site implies long-distance trade networks stretching across dramatically different ecosystems.

One of the study’s most important conclusions is that no two sacrifices looked the same, chemically. Each child’s hair and nails carried a distinct profile.

Socha and her colleagues argue that this wasn’t a coincidence. Each Andean mountain deity had its own personality – its own gender associations, its own domain over weather, water, or agriculture.

The way a child was prepared likely reflected which god was being addressed, not who the child happened to be.

The sex of the child didn’t determine which substances they received. Boys and girls buried on the same mountain got the same drugs.

Socha has cautioned that it would be a mistake to assume that selection for a prestigious religious ceremony necessarily made the experience any less frightening or traumatic for the children involved.

The emotional reality of what these children went through may be impossible to fully reconstruct.

The study also carries a methodological lesson. The Sara Sara and Chuscha mummies had previously tested negative for psychoactive substances.

The new LC-MS/MS analysis overturned those results, detecting cocaine and benzoylecgonine that earlier, less sensitive techniques had missed.

As toxicology improves, mummies that once seemed chemically silent may still have stories to tell.

Socha describes the emerging picture of capacocha as something far more layered than a single sacrificial event.

It was a complex process, involving extensive preparations, long pilgrimages across the empire, and ritual practices that varied from one mountain to the next.

The chemical evidence now locked inside the hair and nails of these children is beginning to confirm what colonial-era chroniclers hinted at centuries ago.

The study is published in the journal Antiquity.
