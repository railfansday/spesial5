Seabirds that nest together spend winter thousands of miles apart – and DNA may predict where

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Seabirds that share nesting sites can winter thousands of miles apart, with their genes helping guide their migration.

Two seabirds can nest side by side and leave for winter at about the same time. You might expect them to fly together. In the western Mediterranean, they often do not.

Cory’s shearwaters and Scopoli’s shearwaters nest in the same colonies in parts of the western Mediterranean and leave on migration at about the same time each fall.

A new study found that birds from those shared colonies generally wintered in areas typical of their own species.

Some ended up thousands of miles from their neighbors, while hybrids tended to travel intermediate distances.

The results suggest that genes shape these birds’ routes more than their colony neighbors do.

Beñat Garcia-Urdangarin, a doctoral student at the University of Barcelona, is the study’s first author.

Raül Ramos and Jacob González-Solís, who heads the university’s Seabird Ecology Lab, are among the lead authors.

The team tracked the migrations of 50 breeding shearwaters and sequenced the genomes, or complete sets of DNA, of those birds and eight others.

Only seven of the 58 birds were hybrids. For that reason, the team wrote, the hybrid result should be interpreted cautiously.

Across most of their ranges, the two species breed apart. Cory’s shearwaters nest mainly on Atlantic islands such as the Azores, Madeira and the Canaries.

Scopoli’s shearwaters nest on small islands across the Mediterranean, as far east as the Aegean Sea.

The two species separated about 350,000 years ago. Today their ranges overlap around the Alboran Sea, the part of the western Mediterranean closest to the Strait of Gibraltar.

Garcia-Urdangarin’s team worked in that overlap zone, at four colonies where both species nest. For comparison, the researchers also sampled a Cory’s colony in the Canary Islands and a Scopoli’s colony on Zembra Island in Tunisia.

Why would neighbors matter? Some migrating birds do learn routes from each other. Pink-footed geese set up a new migration route and breeding area within a decade. Birds from the original population switched to the new route.

Shearwaters that copy their neighbors should winter where the rest of their colony winters, whatever their ancestry. With an inherited route, each bird should follow its ancestry instead.

Each bird wore a small light sensor on one leg for a year or two. The sensor recorded light levels. The team used the timing of each dawn and dusk to estimate the bird’s position twice a day.

The estimates are rough. Each one can be off by a couple hundred miles (a few hundred kilometers).

When the researchers recovered a sensor, they took a blood sample from the bird. The blood was used to sequence its genome. That genome showed how much of the bird’s ancestry came from each species.

From 2008 to 2020, the team recorded 127 migrations, including eight by the same shearwater.

The tracks are available through the Seabird Tracking Database.

The two single-species colonies showed what each species normally does.

About 70% of the trips tracked from the Canary Islands ended off southern Africa, along the Agulhas Current and the area where it meets the Benguela Current.

Most trips tracked from Tunisia ended in the tropics instead, around the Gulf of Guinea and the equatorial Atlantic.

In the mixed colonies, most birds went where their own species goes. Palomas Island, a colony of fewer than 100 pairs, had only one tagged Cory’s shearwater.

That bird flew to the Agulhas Current four years in a row, while every Scopoli’s shearwater tracked from the same island wintered along the tropical African coast.

The team’s statistical model estimated that Cory’s shearwaters from the mixed colonies traveled about 7,200 miles (11,660 km) to their wintering areas.

For Scopoli’s shearwaters there, the estimate was about 2,100 miles (3,340 km). Both figures are for males. Females of both species traveled almost 1,900 miles (3,000 km) farther.

A few Cory’s shearwaters from the mixed colonies did winter off West Africa, in food-rich waters heavily used by the other species. But Cory’s shearwaters from colonies outside the overlap zone are known to winter there too.

The team also found seven recent hybrids in the mixed colonies, 17.5% of the birds sampled there. Two were first-generation hybrids, with one parent from each species. The rest had mostly Scopoli’s ancestry.

Six of the seven hybrids were tracked. Four of them wintered off Namibia and the other two off West Africa.

For hybrid males, the model’s estimate was about 3,800 miles (6,060 km). That distance falls between the estimates for the two species.

“This provides solid evidence that the long-distance migrations of these species have a genetic basis,” Ramos said.

In-between routes have been reported before, in songbirds. A past study found hybrid songbirds taking routes between those of their parent populations.

Birds tracked across multiple years tended to travel similar distances each year.

In the team’s model, differences among individual birds accounted for 82% of the variation in travel distance.

The authors suggested that this habit builds up over time. A young bird starts with an inherited tendency, then refines the route as it gains experience.

Young shearwaters have years to gain that experience. They typically begin breeding at about nine years old.

Earlier tracking studies found that young Cory’s shearwaters shortened and straightened their routes as they aged.

The two species may also meet in their wintering areas. Both seabirds use the food-rich waters off West Africa, Angola and Namibia.

Birds that mix there may later settle in a colony of the other species, the team proposed.

González-Solís said conservation strategies need to consider the zones where the two species breed together, including the transfer of genes between species there.

For now, the hybrid result rests on nine migrations by six birds.

That is too few to test how the trait is inherited, or to compare first-generation hybrids with birds whose ancestry is mostly Scopoli’s.

Hybrid wintering areas also looked more like those of Scopoli’s shearwaters than those of Cory’s shearwaters, the authors wrote.

Every tracked bird was also a breeding adult, so the study didn’t follow young birds on their first migrations.

The next question is which parts of the genome are involved.

The team wrote that finding them will take many more tracked and sequenced birds, sampled across the full breeding ranges of both species.

The full study was published in the journal Molecular Ecology.
