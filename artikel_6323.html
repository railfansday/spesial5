Driving an RV lets you bring the comforts of home along on your road trip, but having a portable generator is essential. It powers your electric appliances and charges electronics, like your phone and laptop. If your RV doesn’t come equipped with a built-in generator, then you’ll need to bring one along.

To be clear: There are no official, task-specific “RV generators,” but some portable generators are better-suited to the task than others. For starters, most RVs have a 30-amp (A) outlet that lets you provide power to your entire RV, charging its batteries while running everything from lights to the air conditioner. The best RV generators need to be fairly beefy–powerful enough to keep multiple appliances running with juice to spare–but also portable enough to pack and, often, move in and out of the RV door.

An RV generator is a big investment, but an important one. We’ll help you find the right option for your setup and walk you through the shopping process and recommend the best models we’ve found and tested. With our picks, you can keep the power flowing wherever you roam.

Size matters–in watts (W), that is. Though power needs of RVs can vary widely, we generally suggest that most RVs need at least 2,000 watts at any given time—enough to power an air conditioner, fridge, lights, and a few devices. However, with just 2,000 watts, you’ll have to be careful about running the a/c along with other appliances, or you might run out of power quickly. If you’re spending a fair amount of time inside and plan to use your a/c often, look for a larger generator that can put out upwards of 3,500 watts.

If you’re using a solar generator (i.e., a power station connected to solar panels), you also need to consider your generator’s capacity, or its ability to store energy that’s been harnessed by a solar panel. To power an RV, you should look for a power station that can store at least 3,000 watt-hours (Wh), which is enough to power a fridge, lights, devices, and small appliances for a couple of days. And it needs to have an output of at least 2,000 watts, which allows it to power said essentials simultaneously.

Keep in mind that solar generators generally cannot generate as much juice as gas or a direct electrical hookup. Unless you have an extraordinary solar panel array and perfect sun to keep the power flowing, you probably won’t be able to power a full suite of appliances on solar alone.

Many RV generators run on gasoline, but there are also dual-fuel generators that allow you to use gas or propane. The advantage of propane is that it can run for longer when using, for example, a 20-pound tank (sometimes twice a long as the gas tank). It will also allow the generator to run more quietly. The disadvantage of propane is that it will deliver slightly less wattage.

A solar generator, a battery-based power station that you can recharge using a set of solar panels, is an increasingly popular and attractive option as well. You’ll need both to harness the power of the sun. While it takes time to fully charge a power station via solar panels, they’re more sustainable and work well if you’re camping off-grid. Plus, if you need to charge it in a hurry, all power stations can recharge by plugging them into a wall outlet.

Remember: It is never safe to run a gas or propane generator inside your RV. They emit carbon monoxide, which is deadly when allowed to build up indoors. Power stations, however, are safe to use indoors.

In addition to the standard 30-amp/120-volt outlet that you want to properly hook up your RV, the best RV generators will have a variety of plugs that will allow you to power or charge other devices directly. We usually look for standard AC outlets, like you’d find at home, which come in handy for plugging in power tools, outdoor appliances, or whatever else. Many generators also come with USB-A and USB-C ports to charge laptops, phones, and other tech.

If you plan on running everything off your RV, port variety may not be as important. But it’s a very nice value add if you plan to use the generator away from your RV.

Since any RV enthusiast is bound to find themselves in a campground or RV park during their travels. The locations often have rules around noise levels and won’t allow noisy generators to bother other guests. The National Park Service, for example, prohibits “operating motorized equipment or machinery that exceeds a noise level of 60 decibels.” This is based on a range of 50 feet. That gives you a little wiggle room, but we recommend 65 decibels or lower.

Traditional, fuel-based generators are often louder than this, so for RV use, we recommend using inverter generators. Not only do these run much quieter, but they provide more stable current, which is important if you are powering sensitive devices such as TVs, laptops, and microwaves. Meanwhile, power stations run virtually silently, so noise level is never an issue for them.

There’s no “set it and forget it” RV generator: You will need to move it around often, so it needs to be light enough for one to two people to carry in and out. In general, lighter is better, but we’d generally look for one around 40-50 pounds for a 2,000-watt generator, or 50-100 pounds for 3,000-4,000 watts. Some generators feature built-in wheels and handles, which makes life easier when moving them around.

All this moving also means the generator needs to be durable enough to take a few bumps here and there. Plus, you want it to last as long as possible without needing repairs. It’s hard to gauge durability on spec, but certain brands have reputations for strong build quality and sturdy construction. Even then, it always pays to look at user reviews and find out if your pick has a reputation for holding up over time.

Portable gas generators run anywhere from 6 to 18 hours, depending on their fuel capacity. When properly well-maintained, you should be able to run it until the tank runs dry, fill it back up, then start over. Never attempt to refill the gas tank while the generator is running, which can lead to fires.

Power stations, sometimes referred to as solar generators, can run for several hours to several days, depending on the capacity of the battery and how much power you are using. Once depleted, the battery can take 1-2.5 hours to recharge using AC power, or 9 or more hours using solar panels.

Various experts recommend that fuel-based (gas and propane) generators be placed anywhere from 5-25 feet away from the RV or home, but we’ve found that 5-10 feet is about right. Just make sure the generator is clear of doors and windows. You don’t want to run the risk of carbon monoxide blowing into the RV. Also, it shouldn’t push up against a wall or other gear–you’re your generator well-ventilated on all sides.

Because they don’t emit any exhaust fumes, power stations can be set up wherever you like; however, if you are pairing them with solar panels, you’ll need them to be within cable distance of the panels, and your panels will need to be positioned to capture sunlight.

You can, but you shouldn’t. Electricity and water don’t mix, and running a generator in the rain can have unpredictable results. At best, it may fry the generator or your RV electrical system. At worst, it could cause a fire or electrocute someone.
