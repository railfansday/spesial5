Cyprus Becomes Espionage Target for Iranian Hackers

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Breaking cybersecurity news, news analysis, commentary, and other content from around the world, with an initial focus on the Middle East & Africa, the Asia Pacific, Europe, and Latin America.

A previously undocumented Iranian threat group called "Volant Manticore" is targeting Cypriot organizations as part of an intelligence-gathering effort for Tehran.

Cyprus' growing security ties with Israel and its use as a staging ground for Western forces operating across the Middle East appear to be making the island an increasingly attractive target for Iranian cyber espionage.

Volant Manticore is an early example of that interest. The previously undocumented advanced persistent threat (APT) group has been targeting organizations in Cyprus as part of what researchers at Check Point describe as an Iranian intelligence-gathering campaign focused on the island.

Its targets so far have included a Cyprus-based airline operating primarily between Cyprus and Israel, telecommunications providers, critical infrastructure organizations, and a suspected government entity with military connections. The selection of targets suggests an effort to gather intelligence on the island's government, infrastructure, and relationships with regional partners, the researchers said.

Check Point has assessed with medium confidence that Volant Manticore is likely an offshoot of the Iranian threat group MuddyWater, tasked primarily with spying on Cypriot organizations on behalf of Iran's Ministry of Intelligence and Security (MOIS). It is among the first known Iranian groups to have a dedicated focus on Cyprus, although it has recently expanded its targeting to organizations in Saudi Arabia, says Sergey Shykevich, director of threat intelligence at Check Point Software.

"Cyprus' defense and energy ties with Israel and Greece, along with its role in Western military operations in the region, may help explain why it is a target for intelligence gathering," Shykevich says.

He points to a Check Point report from earlier this year on a surge in attempts by Iran-linked actors to exploit vulnerabilities in Internet-connected surveillance cameras across the Middle East, including Cyprus, beginning on Feb. 28, when US and Israeli strikes against Iran began. Access to the cameras could have provided Iranian intelligence with a way to monitor military and other strategically important facilities, gather information ahead of potential attacks, and assess the damage from strikes afterward. "The timing is another indication of how Iranian cyber activity tracks with security developments in the region," Shykevich adds.

Check Point described Volant Manticore as relying heavily on phishing to gain an initial foothold using lures tailored for the organizations and sectors it targets. The security vendor's investigation of the group began when it spotted a fabricated resume on VirusTotal associated with "Athena Marinou," a fictitious, AI-generated travel-and-tourism professional.

The document directed recipients to download the full CV from what appeared to be a legitimate recruitment website. The link, however, pointed to an archive hosted on Catbox, which delivered KuboShell — a custom backdoor that the group is using to establish an initial foothold on compromised systems, conduct basic reconnaissance, and receive commands from its operators.

Check Point found that the Windows-based backdoor uses the decentralized InterPlanetary File System (IPFS) for command-and-control (C2) communications, potentially making them harder to block or disrupt than communications routed through a conventional attacker-controlled server. The malware allows attackers to collect details about compromised systems and networks using basic PowerShell instructions. Check Point found evidence that the group then deployed a more extensive, multistage post-compromise toolkit it calls the Homa Framework.

Volant Manticore also uses legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) software such as N-able, NetLock, and MeshCentral in its campaigns. The tools enable remote access and administrative capabilities while allowing the attacks to blend in with legitimate traffic. Check Point also identified infrastructure associated with credential-harvesting campaigns, including domains designed to resemble corporate email services. The researchers found evidence of operators testing their malware against antivirus products and using virtual machines and VPN-related software in their operational environments.

Check Point's analysis showed overlaps between Volant Manticore's techniques and code and those used by the long-active Iranian group MuddyWater. However, Check Point assessed Volant Manticore is a separate operation likely run by former MuddyWater members. "The group has a well-developed toolkit that combines techniques we’ve seen from MuddyWater with tools it developed itself," Shykevich says. "One of the less common techniques we observed was its use of KuboShell, a backdoor that provides remote access and communicates through IPFS, a decentralized file-sharing network."

In September, Check Point observed the group suddenly broadening its focus to include Saudi Arabia. "We are currently seeing the group active in both countries," Shykevich says. While Cyprus remains its primary espionage target, the group has been targeting Saudi organizations as well, mainly in the healthcare sector, he adds. "The increase in activity coincided with escalating tensions between Saudi Arabia and the Iranian-backed Houthis. The scale of the group's activity in Saudi Arabia and elsewhere is likely to vary as the situation in the region evolves."
