Why AI Failures Capture Our Attention

A while back, Paul Bloom wrote about the death of Kit Kat, a beloved San Francisco cat struck by a Waymo taxi. His broader point wasn't really about Kit Kat, though. He used that story as a springboard to discuss how we evaluate new technologies. His argument was essentially that we often judge a new technology by whether it ever fails rather than by how it compares with the technology or practices it might replace.

That creates an impossibly high standard. Failures are going to occur, whether we're talking about new technologies or the people and practices they might replace. The relevant question is how frequently those failures occur relative to the available alternatives. But Bloom's essay got me thinking about a slightly different question: Why are we so naturally drawn to evaluating new technologies against such an unrealistic standard?

Every decision we make involves weighing costs, benefits, and risks, whether consciously or otherwise. And while that sounds straightforward enough, there are a few important points worth making. The biggest of these is that there's a difference between actual risk and perceived risk. Actual risk refers to the objective likelihood that something bad will happen. Perceived risk reflects how risky we believe that outcome to be, regardless of whether our assessment is accurate. Of course, the two don't always align, and that's really not a surprise.[1]

One reason for that is that some risks become much more salient than others. Risk salience reflects how much attention we devote to a particular risk at a given moment. When a risk becomes especially salient, it tends to occupy a disproportionate amount of our attention, increasing the weight we assign to it when making decisions.

I'll use commercial air travel to help explain. Fatal airplane crashes are extraordinarily rare, yet they receive extensive media coverage and often dominate the news cycle for days. As a result, many people become temporarily more anxious about flying, even though the actual risk has changed little, if at all. As the story fades from the headlines, so does the salience of that particular risk. While the objective danger remains largely the same, our perception of it often doesn’t.

Of course, there's a good reason our minds work this way. From an evolutionary standpoint, paying attention to newly emerging or highly apparent threats is often adaptive. A rustle in the bushes, an unfamiliar predator, or a recently contaminated food source may deserve immediate attention, even before we have precise information about the actual level of danger. A tendency to rapidly shift attention toward potential threats can improve survival, even if it occasionally leads us to overestimate their likelihood.

But this tendency becomes less helpful when we're deciding between competing alternatives. Those decisions require us to weigh multiple costs, benefits, and risks simultaneously. When one risk becomes especially salient, it can crowd out consideration of the others, distorting the comparison.

One reason some risks exert such a strong influence on our decisions is the availability heuristic.[2] Rather than calculating probabilities every time we face a decision, we often rely on a mental shortcut: The easier it is to recall examples of something happening, the more likely we assume it’ll occur again. In everyday situations, it serves us reasonably well. Events that happen frequently are often easier to remember simply because we encounter them more often.

The relationship isn't perfect, though. Some events become highly memorable because they’re emotionally charged, unusual, or receive extensive media attention. A commercial airplane crash, a shark attack, or a widely publicized crime may remain vivid in our minds. Their memorability can make them feel more common than they actually are, increasing the weight we assign to those risks when making future decisions.

This is one reason highly publicized artificial intelligence (AI) failures can have such a powerful influence on public opinion. A tragic story involving an AI companion, a driverless car, or an incorrect AI-generated medical recommendation is memorable precisely because they're emotionally compelling. And while those stories deserve attention, that attention makes them unusually easy to retrieve from memory. This can increase the weight we assign to the associated risks, making them feel more common than they may actually be.

Now, this doesn't mean the risks themselves are unimportant or imaginary. Sometimes a widely publicized failure reveals a genuine problem requiring immediate attention. As those memorable examples accumulate in our minds, though, the associated risks often begin to occupy more space in our thinking than less memorable, but potentially more common, risks. And that sets the stage for another subtle judgment shift. Familiar risks associated with the status quo begin to fade into the background.

The availability heuristic helps explain why certain risks receive disproportionate attention, but it doesn't fully explain why we sometimes evaluate new technologies against an unrealistically high standard. To understand that, we also need to consider the role of the status quo.

Once a technology becomes familiar, many of its risks gradually fade into the background. They don't disappear, of course. We simply stop devoting much conscious attention to them. When driving, most of us buckle our seat belts, check our mirrors, and navigate traffic with little conscious thought about the possibility of a serious accident. The risks remain real, but they’ve become part of the background of everyday life.

The same thing happens across countless domains. Physicians sometimes make diagnostic mistakes. Human drivers become distracted, impaired, or fatigued. These risks are well documented, but because they’re familiar, they don't often dominate our thinking in the way a novel AI failure might.[3]

As such, we often don't evaluate a new technology and the status quo under the same psychological conditions. One side is evaluated under a spotlight, the other under ordinary lighting. Familiarity changes which risks command our attention, with our focus often on newer, more visible risks.

This is not to suggest that we should ignore highly publicized failures. Sometimes they reveal genuine problems requiring immediate attention. A widely publicized AI failure may expose a design flaw, identify an unforeseen risk, or demonstrate a technology isn't yet ready for widespread adoption. So, while salient risks deserve investigation, they shouldn't become the entire basis for our evaluation.

This brings me back to Bloom's original point. When deciding whether a new technology should be adopted, restricted, or abandoned, whether it ever fails isn’t the right question. Almost every technology does. Instead, we should be more focused on whether the technology performs better or worse than the alternatives available to us.

So, the next time a dramatic story about AI captures your attention, consider whether its salience is influencing your judgment. Rather than evaluating the failure in isolation, ask how the costs, benefits, and risks of the new technology compare with those of the available alternatives.

2. Availability is just one of many heuristics we rely on for everyday decision-making. For a fuller treatment of how we use them regularly, see “Navigating Decisions with Heuristics.”

3. Familiarity does not eliminate risk salience, though. Particular situations or environmental cues can quickly make familiar risks salient again. For example, someone weaving across lanes may attract much more attention late at night, when impaired driving feels more plausible (a case where evidence and the heuristic align), than the identical behavior might during the middle of the day. So, risk salience is dynamic and context-dependent rather than fixed.
