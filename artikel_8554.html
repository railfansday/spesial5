Find Small Business Grants Close to Home – Not in Washington

Look for small business grants close to home, from city and state economic development offices, utilities, corporations, and foundations. The SBA gives no grants to start or expand a business, and federal grants for companies mostly fund research or exporting. Apply only where your business fits the grant’s purpose.

A grant is the cheapest money a business can get, since you never repay it and no one takes a share of the company.

The U.S. Small Business Administration “does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business,” according to its own grants page. The federal grants a company can win go mostly to research, through the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs, and to exporters.

So for a shop, a restaurant, or a service business, the grants worth chasing are local.

Take a bakery owner who searches the federal grant listings and finds research and export programs, nothing for a bakery. Her city’s economic development office runs a storefront program that pays back half the cost of exterior work, up to $10,000. She applies before hiring anyone, as the program requires, wins a grant, spends $16,000 on a new sign, awning, and lighting, and gets $8,000 back once the work is done.

Here’s where to look, starting close to home, with what each source pays for and who can apply.

A real government grant never charges you a fee. The Federal Trade Commission says no agency will reach out to demand that you pay for a grant, and it warns against paying any up-front fee.

It also warns against paying for a list of grants, since the complete list of federal grants on Grants.gov is free.

Check the sender, too. The SBA says it writes only from email addresses ending in @sba.gov, and that anyone claiming to be the agency from another address should be reported as fraud.

Read what the grant pays for first, since many fund a single purpose. Philadelphia’s Storefront Improvement Program covers exterior work such as signs, awnings, lighting, and paint, and STEP money goes only to export activities the SBA approves.

Then check what you’d have to put in yourself. Philadelphia’s program pays back up to half the cost of approved work, so you cover at least half and pay the whole bill until the city reimburses you. USDA’s Value-Added Producer Grants require a dollar-for-dollar match.

Many grants also want a business that’s already running. STEP is for established small businesses, and the $25,000 Wells Fargo grants the Manhattan Chamber of Commerce offered in 2026 required two years in business.

The competition is steep. Boston’s storefront program says it lacks the money to give a grant to every eligible business, and Hiring Our Heroes names five winners a round. Winning takes an application that answers the funder’s scoring criteria point by point.

Start with one call to your city’s economic development office, and ask which grants it’s funding this year, what each one pays for, and how much of the cost you’d have to cover.
