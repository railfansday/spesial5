Jacob Murphy is not our future but he IS the here and now

In the grand and often unforgiving theatre of Premier League football, where stars are minted in bursts of brilliance and extinguished by the next transfer window, there are players whose value is measured not solely in highlight reels but in the quiet accumulation of purpose.

Jacob Murphy belongs to that rarer company.

At 31, the Newcastle United winger continues to embody a particular kind of professional resilience—one forged in loans, tempered by reinvention, and now expressed in moments that still make the away end rise as one.

I know, we’ve heard the complaints of many fans and pundits that Jacob Murphy is “not good enough”, that he disappears for long stretches during the match, that we need an upgrade at the position. Maybe. The lad is not perfect or to everyone’s liking but he is my cup of tea. He produces with first-time crosses into the box, always gives 100%, readily gets back on defence, and adds a bit of needed comic relief.

Born in Wembley on 24 February 1995 to parents with roots in NE England, Murphy carried Newcastle in his blood long before he wore the shirt. A boyhood Magpie who grew up visiting family in Gateshead, he and his twin brother Josh cut their teeth at Norwich City’s academy. Together they helped win the FA Youth Cup in 2013. Jacob’s path thereafter was the classic English apprenticeship of the lower leagues: temporary homes at Swindon, Southend, Blackpool, Scunthorpe, Colchester, and, most formatively, a season-long stay at Coventry City in 2015-16 where he scored ten goals and announced himself as a player of genuine threat. Returning to Norwich, he produced a breakthrough campaign of nearly double figures in goals and assists, enough to persuade Rafa Benítez to bring him to St James’ Park in the summer of 2017 for a fee in the region of £12 million.

The early years on Tyneside were not seamless. There were loans—to West Bromwich Albion and Sheffield Wednesday—and the familiar struggle of a young wide player trying to impose himself in a club that was itself searching for direction (certainly, Steve Bruce was not the man to coach him up).

What changed everything was the arrival of Eddie Howe.

Under Howe, Jacob Murphy was transformed from squad option into indispensable operator. He became the energetic wide presence who pressed, crossed, tracked, and, with increasing frequency, delivered. The 2024-25 season marked his high-water mark: eight Premier League goals and a dozen assists, a body of work that placed him among the league’s most productive creators from the flank. He scored in the Carabao Cup semi-final against Arsenal and provided a decisive assist in the final as Newcastle United ended a 70-year wait for a major domestic trophy. For a player once dismissed by some as a useful squad man, it was validation of the highest order.

Murphy’s assets have never been those of the purest technician or the most glamorous dribbler. He is direct. He is tireless. He possesses a left foot capable of bending a ball from distance with sudden, almost unfair venom, and a right foot that can pick out a player in the box. Was Alexander Isak worth the money Liverpool paid without the assists of Murphy? I’d argue that we got top money from the Reds for the rat – and Murphy’s crosses were a major contributor. His willingness to occupy the spaces that make others look better. He crosses with purpose, contributes defensively without complaint, and brings a structural intelligence that managers prize.

In a squad that has undergone significant change—younger faces arriving, senior voices departing—his experience has become a form of quiet leadership. Even after being omitted from the formal player leadership group under new head coach Matthias Jaissle, Murphy has spoken of an unchanged responsibility: to add value, to set standards, to help the younger players understand what the Premier League demands week after week.

This season has been more measured than the explosion of 2024-25. Minutes have been shared; the goal tally has been slower to climb. Yet the importance remains as he’s more dependable than Anthony Elanga and much more experienced that Matias Fernandez-Pardo.

On a November afternoon at Craven Cottage, with Newcastle searching for a response after consecutive defeats, Murphy produced the kind of moment that defines him. Intercepting a loose pass, he drove forward, cut inside, and from fully 25 yards unleashed a curling left-footed strike that left Bernd Leno helpless. The goal was his first of the campaign. It was also a reminder.

Three minutes later Fulham equalised (of course they did) but the point was earned in part because of that individual quality and the industry that preceded it. Days earlier he had provided an assist in a clean-sheet victory at Crystal Palace. The pattern is familiar: when Newcastle need a spark or a measure of control on the flank, Murphy is often the player trusted to provide it.

There is something almost old-fashioned in his story. In an era of astronomical fees and instantaneous celebrity, Murphy has built a career through persistence, adaptation, and an unshowy professionalism. He has scored against Manchester City in his early days, netted a brace against Tottenham (including a scorcher!), contributed to European nights and a first major trophy in generations. He has survived the doubts that accompany any player who does not fit the neatest marketing narrative. Now, with his contract situation under discussion and interest from elsewhere already rejected by the club, he continues to state the obvious with clarity: he knows what he brings.

I am not advocating that Jacob Murphy is our future but he IS the here and now. The best we have at his position and, until we can get in a player with better talent and upside, indispensable for the team this season.

For Newcastle United in this transitional season under Jaissle, that contribution—energy, experience, the capacity for the sudden decisive strike—remains quietly essential. Jacob Murphy does not demand the spotlight. He simply continues to occupy the spaces where the game is still won, one determined run, one precise cross, one unforgettable curler at a time.

In the long narrative of a club that has known both glory and patience, his is a chapter written in steady ink.
