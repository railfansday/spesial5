Op-Ed: Crime Drops—Locally Collected Data Drives National Headlines

This opinion piece by CJIS Special Agent in Charge Timothy A. Ferguson was published by the Bridgeport News on October 19, 2026.

Across the country, you saw the headlines. Violent crime declined historically last year—dropping an estimated 9.3% from 2024 to 2025. Murder and nonnegligent manslaughter estimated offenses saw an even larger dip of 18.1%.

In West Virginia, crime was also trending downward.

These headlines are based on data reported to the FBI’s Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) Program by more than 17,000 agencies, covering 96.2% of the U.S. population. It is the responsibility of the FBI’s UCR Program, located within the Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) Division, in Clarksburg, West Virginia, to collect and publish the data from all 50 states to provide a national picture of reported crime.

This is no small feat. Every day, states and agencies translate local and state crime categories into the national standard and submit their data to the FBI. The UCR Program publishes this data monthly on the Crime Data Explorer to promote transparency and allow you to view timely crime data.

The program also uses the data to develop special reports on topics that matter to you — such as crime in schools, aggravated assault with a firearm, and teen/domestic violence. Then once a year, the program sorts through more than 700 million data points to present you the annual snapshot of crime.

Data collection is just a small fraction of what is accomplished at the CJIS Division. Other efforts also make headlines or prevent you from seeing a tragedy on the news.

Tips actioned by the National Threat Operations Center (NTOC) stop school shootings and help with investigations. Super intelligence capabilities incorporated into NTOC enhance the speed and precision of tip triage, strengthening our ability to process the highest priority tips first so we can promptly share critical threat information with federal, state, and local law enforcement.

Criminal history information from the Interstate Identification Index prevents criminals from becoming your child’s teacher or bus driver. Wants and warrants within the National Crime Information Center help police arrest violent criminals.

Detectives can identify subjects and victims through fingerprints submitted to the Next Generation Identification System.

The division’s mission is to provide our partners with the criminal justice information they need while preserving civil liberties. Sharing that information with officers on the frontlines results in arrests that take criminals off the street and keep neighborhoods safe.

It helps law enforcement return to their families at the end of their shifts. It crushes violent crime and defends the homeland.

When you see headlines of declining crime, know the work to determine that, and the investigations that preceded it, are touched by your neighbors dedicated to the mission at the FBI’s CJIS Division.

Timothy A. Ferguson is assistant director in charge of the FBI’s Criminal Justice Information Services (CJIS) Division in Clarksburg, West Virginia. CJIS provides vital systems and services to local, state, tribal, and federal agencies that help ensure public safety. These include the National Crime Information Center; the FBI’s Next Generation Identification System and associated biometric services; the National Instant Criminal Background Check System for firearm purchases; the National Threat Operations Center, which receives and processes tips from the public; and the Uniform Crime Reporting Program, which publishes crime and law enforcement statistical data.
