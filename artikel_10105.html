GNU Mes and the 0.28 release

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

From the series: GNU Mes interpreter speedup

It’s been a while since the latest blog post in the series but we have been working very hard behind the scenes 1. This means I’ll probably forget a few things from all we did, but there’s a good reason for that: we were making a release.

This is the first official and serious release I make as a (co-)maintainer, and the whole thing makes me uncomfortable. Even though people treat me as an expert programmer sometimes, there are many things in programming that I struggle a lot with. The first one, that I think I improved on in the last few years, is writing software from scratch. The second is delivering things to people.

This is normal, as all my career has been focused on R&D and that’s the way I’ve been trained to work. In the case of this specific release, something inside me still thinks my most valuable contribution was that: the idea and demonstration that this attempt could work.

Most of my career has been based on learning in a short time period the right amount of information so I can achieve a reasonable result. Then I normally move on to the next project.

Thinking about that, trying to read through my feelings, I started to realize more and more that whatever that drives me is pushing me to somewhere else, maybe to those parts I struggle more with…

Releasing code is important. It’s the only way to make people actually enjoy the result of your work. I have no problem to admit I struggle with this.

As an R&D guy I’ve always considered myself an “enabler”. I do something, I show how it is done and let people learn from it and actually implement it. That’s fine in some contexts but it has been driving me to some level of frustration recently, as the size of the projects I take part in grow.

An example of this is the RISC-V bootstrap on Guix. I spent a couple of years of my life working on it. We made a working bootstrap for live-bootstrap. But it’s not yet included in Guix because I delegated the final step. Now, even after the years I spent on that, all the sleepless nights and everything, it’s something I cannot enjoy fully2, and you cannot enjoy at all.

This “my job here is done” mentality is not enough for me anymore.

The kind of projects I do now deserve something else. I deserve better.

In Mes I had like 300 commits in a branch, pretty ugly, doing, undoing and redoing some stuff over and over again. The whole codebase was upside down, the commit messages were sometimes good and sometimes lacking, I had fixup commits that fixed things that were changed so much the amount of conflicts the reordering produced was overwhelming… It was a mess. It was me trying new things, learning and cleaning things up as I went. My arborescent thought process at its fullest.

In my usual way of doing things, I decided to promise NlNet a release, something I’ve never done before 3.

Once the bytecode adventure was done, showing some good performance improvement was possible and that Janneke made me a co-maintainer, it was the only acceptable thing to do.4

But, if I wanted to do it, I would need to slow down.

In the last part of the bytecode adventures, you remember how we included Popsyntax, and we reworked the whole macro expansion game. That wasn’t me, but Tim Sample.

I don’t remember very well how he joined my efforts here but I guess I bothered him with questions because he’s a long term Mes contributor and he’s also the author of Popsyntax, the Germ scheme implementation and many interesting things more.

He decided to start over. Chose a Mes release and walked through my steps. Removed the macro expander from the internals and wrote one in Scheme. From there, the direction was set.

I worked through the huge branch that I had, removing the stuff that turned out to be unnecessary, and he took over the git history, and made sure that every single commit was working.

I cannot imagine how many conflicts the guy has been resolving. But damn this looks good now.

This week, I released GNU Mes 0.28. The first release that I do as a GNU maintainer.

Janneke has been guiding me through the whole process — including the part of publishing the website to GNU using CVS5. I’m sure I would’ve never been able to figure out everything on my own. What a ride it has been!

But after more than a year of work, I’m very happy that most of the comments on the release have been positive, specially the ones from people that I worked with and I have a huge respect for.6

But, what will you find in GNU Mes 0.28 that we didn’t mention in the blog yet?

First of all, the main difference from the earlier blogpost is now Mes can be compiled with both M2-Planet and MesCC, and also with GCC as before.

It’s a tricky process, because the error reporting is very limited (I’m being benevolent here), and I was being a very nasty boy with some gotos inside switch statements. After some wrestling with allowed C syntax, I figured it out.

M2-Planet is limited, and we had to leave some things out. When Mes is compiled with M2-Planet it doesn’t have support for errors (aka exceptions) and those abort the interpreter, this is because M2-Planet’s C library doesn’t include setjmp. The stack operations had also to be rewritten as functions for M2-Planet, as the macros with array access were too complex for it. With that, Mes is a little bit slower when compiled with M2-Planet.

None of those are a big deal because that’s only the starting point of the bootstrap process and Mes is rebuilt with MesCC right after it.

The 0.28 release has been tested on real RISC-V hardware that I have access to,7 uncovering a few issues here and there in the Mes C Library.

First, the assembly parts that take care of program initialization in RISC-V (written by myself a few years ago, when I was working on the bootstrap) were broken.

setjmp and longjmp that we use for error handling in Mes, and were not used before, are also faulty. Because of this, the RISC-V version of Mes in the bootstrap, when built with MesCC that is, does not support errors until the issue is fixed.

I also found some extra stuff because GCC 14 decided that implicit function declaration warnings should become errors, breaking the bootstrap that we didn’t test in a very long time in RISC-V, first, because hardware unavailability and, second, because Qemu is also faulty for RISC-V.

There is still a known issue with M2-Planet-built Mes that explodes when it calls strcpy, this can be triggered running (procedure-disassemble map) in the REPL. We didn’t manage to fix this yet.

Tim reworked the macro expansion and made a simple and understandable initial macro expander in Scheme.

With that ready, many things started to fall off naturally.

Most of our driving issues simply disappeared. The macro expander takes the steering wheel from the start, giving us zero garbage collection issues during boot.

The earlier macro expander I wrote in C had to also drive execution, which could possibly call the garbage collector, killing all the C pointers that we had. The new macro expander does everything from Scheme, so no pointers are lost: gc_preserve and gc_release are gone. The boot-done! mark is gone, too.

Continuing with macro expansion, even if in previous posts we talked about Popsyntax integration, we decided to leave that out the release for now. We focused on having a faster interpreter with a new VM.

Keeping macro expansion simple, we are faster than ever.

Take in account the numbers are tricky because mescc-mes means Mes building itself running MesCC. From one version to the other Mes changed, making it a little bit larger (+200 LoC), which means it should take longer to compile. The new version overcomes that, and even achieves a 40% reduction on execution time.

The fib benchmark is the most savage to me. There you can easily see how running has become way cheaper. On the other hand, mescc-hello shows how building a small hello-world program is more expensive, as Mes now needs to compile the whole MesCC to bytecode. When running MesCC for longer, like in a real program (mescc-mes), shows the startup time is compensated by far by the execution speed.

In summary, we got exactly what we were aiming for: a change so big the benchmarks don’t need to be precise. An improvement you can just feel by running Mes.

Comparing to the code in the earlier post in the series, the released code has proper support for values and call-with-values that I thought I did right but I didn’t, really.

According to R7RS standard, the values procedure returns its arguments as values to the current continuation. If the continuation only accepts one value, only the first value is sent to the continuation. That’s what a regular continuation would do. With call-with-values the producer is called in a continuation that accepts multiple values that go directly to the consumer procedure.

If you take a look to the Guile case, that’s exactly how it works. Chibi takes the pragmatic approach not implementing the value discard and uses values just as a container. That’s what I implemented in the previous blogpost, but in the release I managed to do the standards compliant case, and make a REPL that now supports multiple return values, like Guile does:

The tricky part of values is solved with a very primitive support for continuation marks.

When call-with-values is called, the current frame is labeled with a mark, saying it accepts multiple values. When values is called, it checks the current mark in order to decide if it should keep or discard its arguments. It looks like this:

This whole thing works very well thanks to proper tail-calls, which reuse the frames so the marks are right in the current frame all the time. It’s clean and simple.

It also uses the simplest approach possible. The %values builtin wraps values in a list, adding an special car to it. core:cdr can be used to extract the list of values back from the wrapper.

Of course, we could do this “better” just putting the values raw in the stack, but this is good enough for now. If Chibi can get away with doing less, we should be fine with this.

I would never solve this issue if it wasn’t because some random research I did, encouraged by Tim and all the knowledge he has about Scheme implementations.

I was looking at a few papers on delimited continuations, like “Final Shift for Call/cc: Direct Implementation of Shift and Reset” by Martin Gasbichler and Michael Sperber, when Tim told me about his approach in Germ using continuation marks.

With that in mind, I made a very fast sketch on how would continuation marks look like and I realized that was more than enough for the values implementation I was struggling with when I saw the Mes tests (which I didn’t bother to run during the research time) started to fail for the value discard case.

I ended up using only the most minimal thing for it, but I hope to take it a little bit further in the future and make a proper continuation marks based Delimited Continuation support for Mes.

There are many things happening in this release. The diff is huge. But mostly you have been following the effort during the latest blogposts so there’s no need to go deeply in detail.

You saw me re-write the garbage collector, the macro expander, the driver, the builtin implementation, vectors and structs, values, continuations … Some of the code is gone (gc, macro expander and driver, the first implementation of continuations) but the knowledge stays. I basically visited every single corner in this interpreter.

I’m used to hop from one thing to the next. That’s the R&D me again. Now that I know this software the best, I don’t want to just go do anything else. Now I’m the best position to improve it further.

My goal for the future is to improve Mes in all those areas that I found it could be improved but I couldn’t focus on.

Delimited continuations are the best way to represent continuations in an stack based VM like the one I added to Mes. They are more lightweight, better for the GC, and they are composable, allowing for more sophisticated control. Guile supports them, so it would also help us get a step closer to Guile.

Pointer tagging could be a huge deal for speed and reduced memory usage in contrast to the tagged union approach Mes has. To give you an example, in a 64 bit machine each value is at least 24 bytes, including characters. This means if you (string->list "Hello") the result takes 24 bytes per cons cell, i.e per element, plus 24 bytes per character. Pointer tagging would put the bytes for the characters inside the pointers the cons cells have, reducing the total memory to just what the list needs (half of the previous case) and removing the need to access memory again to get the value of the characters.

With some type punning we could also revisit a few data structures we had some trouble with (mostly frames and templates) and make them flat and easy for the CPU to cache and access with no indirection.

In the case of the Scheme to bytecode compiler, just making it slightly better than the dumbest thing possible can make Scheme code fly: builtin inlining, avoiding boxing of non-mutated values… There are many things we can do while keeping complexity under control.

Some GC improvement is also needed, maybe with a simple generational-style garbage collector (does that exist?), at least for a few things like symbols and globals that are never collected it could improve the performance. Even if it doesn’t, we could always use some cleanup in the GC, which is very error prone now.

Those are low-hanging fruits that the new architecture made more accessible. If I just stop here they could be lost.

On the mid term, with all this together and all the improvements Janneke is doing, we could bring MesCC to the next level as a C99 compiler written in Guile that can be incidentally run in Mes.

On the long term, the GNU Mes project would become a very powerful bootstrapping toolchain for languages of any kind.
