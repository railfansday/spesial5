Kidney disease is common in older cats. The kidneys slowly lose their ability to filter waste, which shows up as drinking and urinating more, tiredness, weight loss, and a scruffy coat. Because FeLV cripples the immune system, affected cats are more susceptible to disease, and an otherwise simple infection can be deadly.

Lowering the risk: Vaccination is the best protection. Keeping cats indoors and away from possibly infected cats helps, as does quarantining newcomers and regularly cleaning dishes, toys, bedding, and litter boxes.

Rabies is almost always fatal, and cats are the domestic animal most likely to get it. It spreads through bites and scratches, often from wildlife such as bats, skunks, raccoons, and foxes. Some infected cats become withdrawn, others irritable or aggressive, and drooling and wide pupils are common. It is dangerous for people too.

Spread by mosquitoes, heartworms are less common in cats than in dogs, but they can still cause serious disease, including sudden death. Wheezing and coughing are the most common signs, and the infection is hard to detect.

Lowering the risk: A monthly preventive is the best defense. If you live in an area where heartworm is present, it takes only one mosquito, so even indoor cats can be exposed.

Not every disease on this list can be prevented, but many can be avoided or managed simply by providing your cat with a high-quality diet and keeping up with regular vet checks. Blood and urine tests, appropriate vaccines, and monthly flea, tick, and heartworm protection all help, and keeping cats indoors lowers exposure to injury and illness. Catching problems early is often what makes treatment most effective, and a vet can help tailor a plan for each cat.
