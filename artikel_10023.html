A Chinese bottle-cap manufacturer brilliantly deflated AI hype

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Pre-digital tech can't be transformed and automated

Shanghai is one of the world’s largest and busiest cities but is surprisingly quiet. Electric automobiles cruise silently, chased by hushed flocks of electric scooters.

When I visited in early November to attend the local edition of the AI Engineer conference, I found China's attitude to AI is also quite relaxed. That's not to say interest in the topic is low, but I didn't perceive any anxious artificial-general-intelligence-is-coming-and-will-destroy-us vibe.

The only conference talk that referenced AGI came from Ying Yang, a neuroscience PhD and serious influencer who has over 3.4 million followers on Chinese micro-blogging service Weibo, who enthused about the marketing and product development opportunities opening up to those who prepare for what’s coming.

AGI isn't P(doom) for the Chinese AI practitioners I met; it's a great new marketing tool.

Some of this may be due to the fact that the major US model makers like OpenAI, Anthropic and Google are effectively inaccessible on the Chinese side of the Great Firewall.

Engineers in China use DeepSeek or Kimi or GLM or MiniMax or MiMo or Qwen, all of which are now more than good enough for almost any task, and all offered without reference to The Terminator.

That's not to say that Chinese engineers and designers aren't facing the same crisis of self-worth we've seen elsewhere. A spectacular keynote from Ryo Lu asked, "When we can do anything, do we still know what we want?" Are our measures of self-worth pointless when we can no longer prove who we are by how many lines of code we've written, or frameworks we've designed?

Ryo landed on a line I'm hearing more frequently: "I want to build tools to help me be more and more involved, not prove that I'm needed less and less."

What followed showed just how good that tooling has become and what an AI engineer can do with it.

I saw an impressive demo from AutoArk of a 35 billion-parameter model running in just 2.5GB of smartphone RAM on an iPhone 16 Pro. DYNA's Zhong Shen showed how it builds robot hardware that will work with AI models updating every month.

My favorite presentation came from a bottle cap manufacturer called named Zhu Shihui, who reminded everyone in the audience that “the last mile is half the journey.”

Zhu flayed the class of consultants who never realize some manufacturing equipment is pre-digital but still insist every process can be easily automated. Never mind that workers in those factories lack deep digital skills (Zhu's average employee is in their late 40s) and that companies of this sort operate on margins so thin that resources simply aren't available to mount a complete transformation. Instead of swinging in on an AI wrecking ball, Zhu suggested the best approach is to exercise humility by seeking out an organization's tacit knowledge and using that as the basis for transformation.

Not long after Zhu left the podium, one of my Australian fellow travelers noted the significant proportion of women at the conference. I hadn't noticed, because having equal numbers of men and women at an engineering conference felt perfectly normal. Yet it's not something I've ever experienced at AI events in Australia or America. Whatever else the Chinese are doing with AI, they've put gender politics aside in AI engineering.

To put that in context: not long after leaving Shanghai, Alice Huang became major global news. A Shanghainese woman working in AI might look suspicious to an American who only ever sees men working in the field. Fei-Fei Li, Mira Murati and Daniela Amodei stand out as exceptions that prove the rule, but none of them, despite stellar work, have anywhere near the profiles of their male peers. In China, it's different.

It's become commonplace to be dismissive of Chinese progress in AI, ascribing it to copying, "distillation" and outright theft. Yet the Chinese make both the best small models and the best robots, and are moving ever-closer to making them work well together.

Chinese labs share their results with their peers and their competitors: nearly every model, closed or open, has benefited from the research done by DeepSeek and Alibaba. Practicality, balance and openness seem to be serving them well. Perhaps, after OpenAI follows Anthropic out to the public markets, and shareholders get a voice, stability, sanity and a bit of quiet will cross the Pacific. Those are some Chinese imports I'm looking forward to. ®
