The 8 Best Ergonomic Keyboards Reduce Wrist Pain and Strain

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

It may look strange, but an ergonomic keyboard can be a game changer if you’re worried about repetitive strain injuries such as carpal tunnel syndrome.

As an editor, I type for a living, so I’ve seen and felt firsthand how quickly long hours at a keyboard can take a toll on your wrists, hands, and shoulders. Ergonomic keyboards feature wider, angled typing beds that allow you to type without tightening your shoulder blades or twisting your forearms quite as much as you normally would. That minimizes the strain caused by the subtle ways we twist and tilt our bodies when we sit at our computers all day.

Strain-related and musculoskeletal injuries (damage to your body's bones, muscles, ligaments, tendons, or joints) are a major portion of lost-workday injuries in the U.S.: The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) identifies repetitive tasks and awkward postures as potential risk factors for musculoskeletal disorders such as carpal tunnel syndrome, which contribute to the reported 335,570 days-away-from-work cases involving sprains, strains, and tears recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) in 2023 and 2024. Ergonomic office gear, including split keyboards and vertical mice, is not a silver bullet for these issues, but can reduce the long-term strain you put on your body while using a mouse and keyboard working nine to five, five days a week.

It’s also worth noting that the distinctive shape of an ergonomic keyboard often comes with an adjustment period. Depending on the design, it can take days or even weeks to get used to using one. The short-term inconvenience will be worth it, though, especially if you are starting to feel pain in your wrists, arms, shoulders, or back while working at a computer.

To help you make the switch, I’ve broken down the basics of what you need to know about using ergonomic keyboards and recommended the best models I’ve found based on hands-on testing at my home and office. After making the change myself, I noticed I could work longer without the same tension in my hands and shoulders.

Ergonomic keyboards are designed to encourage a more natural typing position by reducing how much you bend your wrists inward, rotate your forearms, or hunch your shoulders while typing. Many use split layouts, angled keys, or “tented” centers to help maintain a neutral posture.

These keyboards aren’t a magic fix for pain or repetitive strain injuries. A smattering of studies commissioned by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) and other government agencies over the past 20 to 25 years have shown that using this gear minimizes pain and strain in folks with musculoskeletal disorders, but have not established that using them long term will prevent them. That said, there is enough anecdotal evidence to suggest that they do reduce muscle strain, which should theoretically slow the onset of meaningful muscle pain.

At the very least, they can make extended computer use more comfortable in the long term, especially when combined with a well-adjusted workspace and better typing habits.

I’ve recommended these ergonomic keyboards based on a mix of hands-on testing and in-depth research into some of the most popular models available today.

I have personally tested four keyboards in real-world daily scenarios over five months, spending about three to four weeks with each keyboard, using them for four to six hours a day for at least three days per week.

Using each keyboard over a relatively long period of time is crucial. It often takes days (or even weeks) to get fully comfortable typing on a new model, especially if you’re switching from a standard keyboard for the first time. I’ve taken enough time to not only judge each keyboard on basic performance, but also how much time and effort you may need to put in before you resume typing with your usual speed and accuracy.

Of course, I also evaluated based on overall usability, including how the keyboards felt for both productivity and extended typing sessions. Typing feel and comfort were paramount, as well as quality-of-life features that make a keyboard more functional or helpful.

I vetted the four models I haven’t tested yet in person using the same criteria, but relied on reviews from my technology reporters at other publications, as well as user reviews published by brands and retailers. Over time, I will amend or replace these reviews with firsthand testing notes and recommendations.

The Logitech Ergo K860 provides the balance of ergonomic support and accessibility for new ergo users trying this kind of gear for the first time. Its soft, unibody form factor splits between the two sides of the keyboard, reducing shoulder blade stretch, with angled keys to match. While it isn’t a perfect ergonomic solution, I found that it provided enough support to ease my fatigue at the end of a long workday.

And unlike conventional keyboards that lift the back of the keyboard to tilt it toward you, this model tilts at the front. As it turns out, “reverse tilting” is actually easier on the wrists because you aren’t flexing to reach the keys. It makes a bigger difference than you think: My wrists and fingers were a lot more relaxed when they draped naturally over the keyboard.

The keys are super smooth to type on, and the very large wrist rest makes typing more comfortable. The downside is that the Ergo K860 takes up a reasonable amount of space on your desk. That’s the price you pay for a better typing experience, but it may be an issue for folks with a compact setup.

The Periboard 512 is a very budget-friendly soft-split keyboard with a decent number of quality-of-life features and a very office-friendly vibe. It sits flat and is more compact than it looks, making it at least a bit more convenient than the Logitech Ergo K860. It still takes up its fair share of desk space, though.

It features an integrated palm rest, though it isn’t cushioned, along with a classic curved key layout to help keep your hands in a more neutral position. The keycaps themselves are contoured to better match the shape of your fingertips.

The Periboard 512 also includes multimedia shortcuts at the top of the keyboard for quick access to functions such as browser navigation, email, volume controls, mute, sleep mode, and search.

The Logitech Wave Keys was my first ergonomic keyboard, and I would recommend it as a starter model for anyone who’s hesitant to try one. It’s a small investment at only $60, and it doesn’t take long to get the hang of typing on its more compact but ergonomically angled keys.

Though that tighter key formation doesn’t provide relief for your shoulders, it can be helpful if you find yourself hyperextending your fingers to reach certain keys. That was the biggest benefit in my case: With the keys closer together, I didn’t have to strain my fingers as much as I usually do, even with a laptop keyboard. Still, the best and worst part of the Wave Keys is that its ergonomic impact is limited: It helps a little, and it’s very easy to use right away, but if you have a documented repetitive strain injury, it may not go far enough to relieve pain.

The keyboard connects via Bluetooth or Logitech’s USB receiver. I just popped the little gadget into the back of my monitor, turned the power switch, and I was up and running. It’s a pretty no-frills design overall, but it’s a very easy adjustment if you’re making the initial change to an ergonomic keyboard.

The Kinesis Freestyle2 represents the deep end of the ergonomic keyboard pool: It’s a full-blown split keyboard, which breaks up the keys into two separate pieces so you can position the sides to type without squeezing in your shoulder blades. It also features interior risers, which enable both tenting and reverse tilting to one of three heights. This is a full suite of ergonomic features, and should allow you to find the most comfortable option possible.

Having three fairly intensive ways to customize how the keyboard feels is, as you might expect, pretty daunting. It takes time and experimentation to find the settings that both feel comfortable and usable. But once I figured things out, I found that having all that control was really helpful. The more you’re willing to commit, the more you’ll get out of it.

And teaching yourself to type on any split keyboard takes some effort. You have to adjust to operating both sides of the keyboard independently. It isn’t that much different from how you normally type, but it feels like a whole new world. For me specifically, it took some time for my fingers to not instinctively reach over to the other side, and to remember that the “B” and “N” keys were on either half.

That said, the Freestyle2 comes with a detachable joint so you can push the sides into a standard keyboard layout. It’s helpful while you’re learning, but don’t get too comfortable: The Freestyle2 is a good keyboard, but there’s no reason to invest in this design if you aren’t committed to mastering the split configuration and optimizing for ergonomic support.

Once I got the hang of using it, the Freestyle Edge RGB Plus has become my favorite ergonomic keyboard because it’s so full-featured. I played around with configuration for a few weeks, and found that shoulder-width and rotated inward was the most comfortable. I also prefer the padding on this model over the Freestyle2 because it's thicker and feels more supportive.

The lift kit, which enables tenting, comes pre-installed. That alone may be worth the extra $50, as it is much easier to set up than other true split keyboards, including the Kinesis Freestyle2. That said, you also get improved typing feel of mechanical keys, which makes for more comfortable typing across the board.

As the name suggests, the Freestyle Edge RGB Plus adds RGB backlighting to the mix. That, in conjunction with its mechanical keys, is meant to give it a gaming keyboard vibe. It features nine programmable lighting profiles, all accessible without a configuration app. (Personally, I liked the third option the best—it’s a color spectrum that is always changing.)

And just like most gaming keyboards, it offers a lot of customization options, including a set of Mac-specific keycaps so you can have an Apple key. It is a tenkeyless model (TKL), so it doesn’t have a number pad, and doesn’t come with the connector piece that you get with the Freestyle2. So this may serve better as an upgrade for experienced split keyboard users, rather than a starter model. The mechanical keys are a good enough reason for keyboard enthusiasts and gamers to invest right away.

If you want a fully featured ergonomic split keyboard, the Advantage360 stands in a league of its own. Unlike most fully split keyboards, it combines fully wireless connectivity, mechanical switches, and contoured keywells into a premium design. The concave layout reduces finger travel and strain, while the completely separate halves let you position each side naturally for your shoulders and wrists, without a tether cable locking them together.

The Advantage360 also places commonly used keys like delete, backspace, space, and enter into dedicated thumb clusters, shifting the workload from your pinkies. Beyond the hardware, Kinesis also offers SmartSet programming support, allowing you to remap keys, create your own macros, and customize layouts through a companion app, which is a feature that’s still relatively uncommon among ergonomic keyboards.

With multiple tenting options and wireless flexibility, the Advantage360 is made for people who truly want a customizable ergonomic setup rather than just a more comfortable keyboard. The advanced configuration is an adjustment, so be prepared for a steeper learning curve than other, more beginner-friendly split models we recommend.

Similar to the Logi Ergo K860, Razer’s Pro Type Ergo is a low-profile soft split with a wide, domed shape and a large wrist rest, with several height and angle adjustments. Its concave keycaps give your fingers a target to sink into for increased typing accuracy, helping new users find their footing.

In that vein, I also like that it has a three-part spacebar, one for each hand as well as a little extra key in the middle in case you accidentally cross one hand over. There are two “B” keys on either side of the split, which may take some getting used to since a majority of keyboards only have one of each key.

Though it’s from Razer, which is primarily known for gaming, this model is part of the brand’s Pro Type productivity line, which is theoretically made for the office. Of course, you might not realize that, given the prominent RGB lighting. It also features media keys and two customizable rollers in the top corners, which you can customize (along with the lighting) through Razer’s Synapse configuration app.

Fans of gaming keyboards will likely feel more at home on the Pro Type Ergo than other keyboards and, again, I think it has a lot of nice features that help ease you into using a split design.

The Q11 Ultra also does a good job helping typists who are new to split keyboards find their way around. The unique design allows you to push the two pieces so that they’re basically flush so you can use it as a standard keyboard. And unlike some of my other recommendations, it doesn’t have a domed shape or tenting, so it sits flat and feels more like a standard board.

Keychron is one of Popular Mechanics’ longtime favorite keyboard brands, and the Q11 reflects many of the reasons why: It features great mechanical typing feel, Bluetooth and 2.4-gigahertz wireless support, and a web-based configuration app to customize its RGB lighting, remap keys, and create macros.

Its Achilles’ heel as an ergonomic keyboard is its lack of tenting. You won’t get quite as much relief from this model as you would from the Kinesis keyboards, especially in the back and shoulders.

Ergonomic keyboards change the design of the keyboard in a few different ways so you can use it without holding a position that twists or pulls your muscles for an extended period of time. Unfortunately, these changes can make the keyboard a little more tricky to use (at least until you get used to it), so every model strikes a balance between offering ergonomic support and making the experience approachable for new users. Here are some of the changes you’re most likely to use.

Fixed or “Soft” Split: The more approachable ergonomic keyboards create a small, static split between the two sides of the keyboard and tilt the keys out so your hands can approach from the sides, rather than the front. This doesn’t allow for as much customization as a true, two-part split keyboard, but it takes less time to get the hang of things.

Split: Split keyboards have two distinct halves, one for each hand. This offers support similar to the soft-split: By positioning the two sides so they tilt away from each other, meeting your arms where they naturally hover over your desk, you reduce strain on your back and shoulders.

Split keyboards have two sections to keep your hands, wrists, and forearms at a comfortable distance to reduce strain. Fixed split keyboards are one-piece designs where the key sections are separated and angled, but still connected. Fully split versions have two separate keyboard halves that can be positioned as needed.

Tenting: Some split keyboards have risers on the inner sides, which tilt the center of the keyboard up, creating a tent-like triangular formation. Like a vertical mouse, this allows you to type without twisting your wrists down to type on a flat surface.

Some fixed split keyboards feature a contoured dome-like design, which provides a small amount of tenting. But, similar to the unibody split, it does not provide as much customization or support as the two-part solution.

Reverse Tilt: Lastly, while most keyboards feature feet in the back that tilt the keys toward you (so you can see them better), an ergo keyboard may have feet in the front to tilt the keyboard away from you. Reverse tilt minimizes the need to keep your wrists flexed throughout the day.

Like most things wireless, the beauty of a wireless keyboard is that it eliminates the tangled mess of wires that cramp up your workspace. Likewise, anything wireless requires a battery and a reliable wireless connection, which can sometimes be a chore to set up. Go for a wired ergonomic keyboard if you prefer a plug-and-play setup, and opt for a wireless version if you’d prefer more flexibility.

All of these keyboards will work on any PC, but most have the Windows key layout with “Control,” “Start,” and “Alt” in the lower left corner instead of “Command” a.k.a. the “Apple” key. Some of our recommendations do offer a Mac-specific design if you use a Mac and, understandably, want the keys to match your OS.

If you regularly alternate operating systems, look for models with built-in OS toggles such as the Logitech Wave Keys, or a design with replacement keycaps, such as the Kinesis Freestyle Edge RGB+. For the best result, it’s worth checking the manufacturer’s specifications to confirm compatibility.

Once you determine the style and shape of the keyboard you want, compare each model’s specs to narrow down any extra features you desire in your keyboard. Some ergonomic keyboards have one-touch internet access or multimedia keys that allow you to do things like quickly change the volume or create your own shortcuts, as well as backlighting options.

There’s no way to avoid the fact that typing on an ergonomic keyboard feels weird if you’re used to a regular layout. The first time I laid my hands on an ergo keyboard, everything felt just a bit out of place—all the keys were not quite where I remembered them and were tilted at a different angle. It can take a little while for your muscle memory to adjust to typing on an ergonomic keyboard, but once you get over that hump, the payoff can be hours of more comfortable typing.
