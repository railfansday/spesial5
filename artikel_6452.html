Cotton plants in poor soil lean on two hormones to grab enough potassium

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Two plant hormones, ethylene and jasmonate, work together to help cotton take in potassium, and sprays of both raised yields in potassium-poor field soil.

Ethylene is the gas that ripens bananas on a kitchen counter, but in cotton plants it has a second job. Together with a partner hormone called jasmonate, it turns on the machinery that pulls potassium into the plant when the soil runs short.

Spraying cotton leaves with versions of both hormones also raised yields in potassium-poor field soil.

A team led by Professors Zhaohu Li and Xiaoli Tian at China Agricultural University in Beijing worked out the steps in upland cotton, Gossypium hirsutum.

Scientists recently traced the species’ origin to Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula. The team describes the work as a possible basis for helping crops use potassium more efficiently.

The team tested its idea in two ways: lab experiments on the plant’s genes and proteins, and field experiments on cotton grown in potassium-deficient soil.

It ran the field work only in that kind of soil, so the field results don’t say whether the sprays would help where potassium is already plentiful.

Potassium helps plants run photosynthesis, switch on enzymes, and balance water inside their cells. Cotton is one crop that needs a lot of it. Researchers have found that more potassium is leaving agricultural soils around the world than is going back in.

Yet scientists understand less about how plants take up potassium than about nitrogen and phosphorus. In crops, the way genes control the proteins that carry potassium into cells remains largely unexplored. Other researchers have searched cotton’s genes for ways to make the crop tolerate drought.

The team started with the protein that does the work. It’s a transporter that carries potassium into the plant’s cells. This one grabs potassium even when little is around, and the plant needs it to keep its potassium level steady during a shortage.

A control protein switches the transporter’s gene on by attaching to the DNA right beside it. Two other proteins, one from the jasmonate pathway and one from the ethylene pathway, each attach to the DNA beside the control protein’s gene and turn it up. Then the two link up, and as a pair they turn up the control protein more than either one does alone.

In cotton, then, the two hormones act as partners rather than as separate signals. Hormones do plenty of jobs besides telling plants when to grow. In one earlier study, a drought hormone helped plants block spider mites.

A plant with enough potassium doesn’t need the extra uptake, and the team found how the plant keeps the system off.

When potassium is plentiful, two proteins that hold others back grab the jasmonate-pathway protein and the control protein. In the team’s reading, that stops the plant from spending resources on a system it doesn’t need.

Crops run a similar shutoff for nitrogen. Researchers recently found a protein that tells crops to stop absorbing nitrogen even when plenty remains in the soil.

To see whether the finding could matter on a farm, the team ran field experiments in soil with a shortage of potassium. Each kilogram of that soil held less than 60 milligrams of available potassium (60 parts per million). The team calls that a moderate-to-severe shortage.

The team sprayed cotton leaves with methyl jasmonate, a form of jasmonate, or with ACC, a compound plants turn into ethylene. Either spray raised the potassium level in the leaves and improved photosynthesis.

Using both did best. It raised seed cotton yield by 11.6% compared to unsprayed plants. Seed cotton is the crop as it comes off the plant, with the seeds still inside.

In the field, as in the lab, the two hormones worked best together.

The 11.6% gain came from soil with too little potassium, so it doesn’t show whether the sprays would help where potassium is already plentiful.

Anyone considering sprays like these on a working farm would first need to see the gain repeat across more fields and growing seasons.

The full study was published in the journal Science Bulletin.
