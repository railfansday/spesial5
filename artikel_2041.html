Andy Robertson set to scale Scotland’s Everest but King Kenny remains untouchable

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Thursday is the 40th anniversary of Sir Kenny Dalglish winning his 102nd and final Scotland cap in a 3-0 win over Luxembourg at Hampden Park.

There was no formal announcement of a retirement afterwards. Paying a visit to the Dalglish family home in England, Andy Roxburgh asked the great man to help get him up and running as manager before focussing on his expanded role as player-boss of Liverpool.

At the age of 35, injuries were beginning to take hold and Dalglish had enough on his plate at Anfield without carrying the hopes of a nation on his shoulders as well. That night in November 1986 marked the end of the road.

It took four decades to reach a point where Andrew Robertson will match King Kenny’s record in Friday night’s Nations League game against North Macedonia. If he then plays against Switzerland on Monday he will move to 103 appearances and claim the crown as the most capped Scotland player of all time.

If someone has to topple the king then Robertson seems as good a candidate as any. Comparing players from different eras is always tricky and the Tottenham defender can do nothing about the fact that he plays in an age when players earn more caps than they did in the seventies or eighties.

Nor can he do anything about the fact that he plays left-back, a position which lacks the crowd wow of a goalscoring No.7 like Dalglish or John McGinn.

Brazil gave Cafu more games than Pele and if it’s good enough for the most successful nation in history, there should be no issue with Scotland handing more appearances to a full-back than we did to the most accomplished British attacker of all time. The achievement means the world to Robertson and his family and rightly so; they should savour the moment.

To children of the eighties like this one, the Dalglish cap haul felt like an Everest which would never be conquered; a record which never should be topped. Like retiring a shirt number in honour of a legendary player, it felt as if no one else should ever be allowed to surpass Scotland’s greatest player.

Kenny Dalglish (Image: Mark Leech/Offside)

Robertson would be the first to admit that the record will never make him the greatest Scotland player of all time because that’s a title which sticks with King Kenny. If Denis Law couldn’t pinch it, no one will.

Scoring 30 goals in dark blue and playing in three World Cups, Dalglish bore a talent which deserved that most overused of tags; that of the world-class player.

Not everyone thought so at the time, mind. Kenny was forever accused of performing better for Liverpool than he did for Scotland. Praise, and he received plenty, would be muttered through slightly gritted teeth.

The fulcrum in one of the greatest club sides of all time, he won six English league championships, three European Cups, an FA Cup, four League Cups and major honours as player-manager.

With Scotland, it was all about the moments. Rising to head home the goal against Wales which confirmed a place at the World Cup in Argentina. That quite magnificent individual strike against Belgium in 1982, his best, for me, and the thumping left-foot beauty against Spain in November 1984.

When he actually made it to the major tournaments, the dazzling smile which followed every goal was seen a little less than people would have liked. He struck in a memorable, if futile, win over the Dutch in 1978 and added another against New Zealand four years later.

For any player, scoring at back-to-back World Cup finals is a stamp of quality. His international goals total has only ever been matched by Law and, despite a valiant effort by John McGinn, that record will be harder to surpass than his cap tally.

If there is a ‘but’ attached to Kenny Dalglish, it’s the failure to lead the national team to the second stage of major international finals. Alas, the same asterisk can also be placed beside the name of Andrew Robertson.

Released by Celtic as a teenager for being too small, Robbo rebuilt his career at amateur Queen’s Park and then Dundee United, before winning two Premier League titles, a Champions League and a Club World Cup with Liverpool.

His nine years and 373 appearances at Anfield ended in the kind of tributes which feel like premature obituaries.

As an international player, he has driven the standards and culture which restored Scotland to two Euros and a first World Cup finals in 28 years.

It was Robertson’s idea to present players with personalised gift packs and handwritten notes before major tournaments.

On reaching the World Cup finals, his first thought was for his late Liverpool team-mate Diogo Jota.

Even if he never scores the kind of goals which make McGinn and Scott McTominay the darlings of the Tartan Army, his work rate, intensity and intelligence are the reasons managers love him and keep playing him.

Alex McLeish returned to manage the national team for a second time in February 2018 and made Robertson his skipper at the relatively young age of 24 after Scott Brown’s international retirement.

McLeish had won 77 caps over 13 years as a Scotland defender and knew what the role entailed. Describing Robertson as the ‘outstanding candidate’ and an ‘inspirational figure’, a leadership role at three major tournaments has vindicated that decision.

“By the end of this camp Andy should be above Kenny and I must be honest, I thought nobody would ever beat Kenny,” McLeish told Herald Sport.

Laughing, he adds: “It looks like Andy will and Kenny will be jealous of that.

“But I made Andy Scotland captain because he is a fantastic guy first and foremost.

“I could see that he was not scared to have a go at others and say, ‘hey, come on…’

“You need those qualities on the pitch and I think he is entitled to feel very proud of the footballer that he became after he joined Queen’s Park.

“He is a great fella, he has been a great captain, he has been a fantastic, ruthless winner in the club game as well. And, for me, Andy deserves everything that has come his way.”

Andy Robertson (right) in action for Scotland in 2014 (Image: Marcin Wziontek/Shutterstock)

The reality of professional football dictates that Robertson is 32 now and won’t be around much longer. The same might be said of McGinn and Ryan Christie and the group of players who have clawed Scotland back from the international wilderness.

When they go, it’s not clear who takes their place. Look at the under-21 team set to play Ukraine on Thursday and Aberdeen’s Mitchel Frame has played no first-team minutes this season. Bailey Rice of Kilmarnock has only managed 34 minutes since mid-September.

While the nation is still capable of producing proper players like Billy Gilmour, managers don’t seem to trust them enough to play first-team football.

Robertson derived the benefit of playing against men at an early age at Queen’s Park and then Dundee United and the same observation might be said of McGinn, Ryan Christie, Lewis Ferguson and Kieran Tierney. That’s how internationals are forged and right now the door is being slammed in the faces of the aspirational.

The lack of a first-team pathway for those aged between 16 and 21 is a ticking time bomb. A menace to the job prospects of Scotland managers of the future and of the here and now.

In a stark, if unsurprising, admission Sebastien Pocognoli spoke at the weekend of a lack of goalkeeper alternatives to Angus Gunn. Beyond Falkirk’s Scott Bain, Rangers reserve Liam Kelly and Jon McCracken, an English League One keeper, the cupboard is bare.

If the top clubs won’t play young players of their own accord, then they need to be incentivised or compelled to get their act together and, at a recent summit in St Andrews, SFA chief football officer Craig Mulholland unveiled a package of measures to address the situation.

Five top-flight teams need more persuasion over the plans, forcing Mulholland to tweak the details to reach a point where they might have some chance of being voted through.

If they won’t fly at all, then the SFA may have to consider the nuclear option. The governing body have the option of changing the rules to make it harder for clubs to secure Governing Body Endorsements, which are required for visas, for the Joel van den Bergs and Kosta Nedeljkovics of this world.

A points-based system, similar to that utilised in England, would separate the wheat from the chaff and compel clubs to get with the programme.

Andy Robertson knows better than anyone how hard it is for young Scots to make it at a club like Celtic. It took hard work, perseverance and talent to make it after an early rejection and for that reason alone Scotland fans should turn up at Hampden on Friday to salute the man on the cusp of claiming Sir Kenny’s caps record.

Debating which of the two deserves to be Scotland’s most capped footballer, then, obscures the bigger and more important point.

In both cases, the cream rose to the top and unless clubs get serious about fixing the current crisis in youth development, it could be some time before the nation sees their likes again.
