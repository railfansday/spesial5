Gila discusses his mental health journey, perfectionism and current state: “Changed my perspective”

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

In the latest episode of AC Milan’s The Chamber series, Mario Gila was the guest and spoke about the positive mental health journey that he’s currently on.

It was last month that Milan unveiled their new series, called The Chamber, which focuses on the mental health of the players at the club. Luka Modric was the first guest, a perfect start in many ways, and the second episode has now been published with Mario Gila as the guest.

The concept, in case you’re not familiar, is that the players are placed in a ‘Chamber’ with no outside noise for a few minutes. Then, they are interviewed by the host Luca Casadei, who focuses on the mental state of the players.

As said, Gila was the guest in the most recent edition and he has a lot to say, having been on a mental health journey in the last few years. Indeed, after hitting ‘rock-bottom’ at Lazio, he found a way through and is now very happy.

How are you feeling right now? [after being in the chamber, editor’s note]

I feel very peaceful, I feel really good here. I think experiences like this are also closely linked to how a person feels and where they are emotionally at that moment. And I think it amplifies everything… a little. I wanted to experience it to the fullest when they told me about it.

I think when there are no outside influences clouding your mind that’s when you connect most with yourself. That’s why I closed my eyes. I wanted to feel as deeply as possible what was inside me and how my mind works in moments like this.”

What did you feel during this experience?

Well, I really enjoyed it because I felt so peaceful. I felt completely at ease, and it was an experience I’ve rarely had before. I’m someone who likes to meditate, to reconnect with myself, and I’m interested in spirituality, it’s something I genuinely enjoy.

“And I felt so much peace, so much peace because with everything so quiet, such complete silence and being in touch with myself, it really is an experience I’m not sure I’ll ever forget. I really liked it.”

Speaking about yourself, are there moments when you’re alone, away from the public eye, away from everyone expecting something of you, when you take some time just for yourself? I’d like to know what you feel, who you are emotionally in those moments.

It doesn’t happen to me often because, whether you like it or not, footballers tend to have very active minds. It’s a profession that takes a huge mental toll.

“But I try, for example, during holidays or whenever we get some time off, to find situations where I feel, well, maybe not exactly like in that chamber, but where I can connect with myself, where I can rediscover that Mario who sometimes gets a little lost because of the job he does.

“And in those moments, well, I feel at peace, I feel that connection with myself, I feel like a person. Because in our profession, whether we like it or not, we lose touch with ourselves a little. And that’s what I’m trying to achieve when I’m alone with myself.”

You told me that because of the job you do, you’re always very focused. I wanted to talk about control because I’ve heard you say you need to have everything under control.

Yes, I think that, given the way I see the profession and the position we footballers are in, I think it may not seem like it, but you have a lot of things to manage. How you behave off the pitch, you’re a footballer 24 hours a day, your diet, physical preparation, training sessions, the physio.

And it’s like… I have to keep everything perfectly under control so that at the weekend or in a midweek match I’m at my best.  So the profession itself pushes you to keep everything under control and if you’re also someone who likes that and believes things should be that way, you take it even further.

“And in those situations, I feel comfortable when everything’s under control.”

Is that just at work, or does it apply everywhere, with your family, your friends, always needing to have everything under control?

I’ve been like that with my family too, with my teammates, with my friends, but I see my personal life differently now. I didn’t before. Because perfection is exhausting.  In the end, so many expectations… Striving for perfection wears you out.

“I think that, to a certain extent, it’s boring. You don’t get to see the real person, their authentic self. So that’s how I’ve been for much of my life. I’m young, but I’ve been like that. Now I’m beginning to understand better that everyone is different, that every moment is different, and to appreciate that more, that authenticity.

Did you used to feel weak when you weren’t in control? Vulnerable?

Now, though, you’ve realised it’s a strength?

“Yes, yes, yes. I understand that people and situations aren’t always as we want or expect them to be. And I think it’s somewhat connected to ego, but now we’re getting into some pretty deep territory. But…I like it! I think so, yes, I used to be like that. I used to be. I’m less like that now, and I appreciate difficult moments more because there’s also a lot of good to be found in those moments.

“I mean, we can’t control everything, we can’t expect everything to be perfect because perfection doesn’t teach you anything. I don’t think there’s anything particularly positive about it.

“In the end, we grow a lot through… or we become stronger during difficult times than during good ones. So I’ve largely let go of that kind of perfectionism. And those incredibly high expectations, I don’t like them anymore.”

But is there a positive side to perfectionism for you? Do you think you became the player you are thanks to your perfectionism?

“Yes. This is a struggle I have with myself, for example. In this profession, I believe that perfectionism and ego are two things that drive us to succeed at work, in our professional lives. Ego drives you to be better than, say, the opponent you’re playing against.

“Perfectionism makes you work harder, train better, take better care of yourself and I think that helps you develop a lot professionally. But there has to be a balance. It can’t all be about perfectionism because it makes you incredibly demanding of yourself and when you go through difficult times, you won’t know how to deal with them.

When you make a mistake, are you very demanding and hard on yourself? I mean, how do you treat yourself when something doesn’t go the way you wanted?

“In the past, for example… I’ve changed a great deal. I went through a really difficult time early in my career here in Italy and hit rock bottom, but with outside help, psychological support, I got through it.

“That’s why I keep saying ‘before’. Back then, I was very hard on myself. I didn’t allow myself to fail. If I did, I was extremely negative towards myself, I couldn’t accept that I might fail or make mistakes.

“Now I handle it much better. Now I feel that making mistakes is part of the job, it’s something that helps you improve, something that helps you grow. So I deal with it much more calmly, I process it much better internally.

“I think you still need to hold yourself to high standards and you also need to know how to find a solution to that mistake, whether in your career or in your personal life, but with much more calm and more kindness towards myself.”

This interview is also meant to share some values with others, right? In those moments, what exactly do you tell yourself to be kinder to yourself?

“Well… What a good question! In moments when I’ve gone through difficult situations, perhaps, after that period I was telling you about, I’m aware that everyone can make mistakes. I know that making mistakes is part of the job and it’s what helps me grow.

“So I changed my perspective. I used to see it very negatively, now I find the positive in the negative. So I understand that if I focus on something positive or look at it from a positive perspective, everything surrounding the mistake changes.

“All that noise stops being noise and turns into a sense of calm. It’s normal to make a mistake and be demanding of yourself, to want to avoid making that mistake again, but it might still happen.

“So at that moment, I believe that being kind to yourself and recognising that it’s something that can happen and that you can use it to improve, is, I think, the way forward to avoid falling into negativity. But instead maintain that positivity and balance that can help you feel at peace, as I was saying in there, like the experience I had in there earlier.”

Regarding that difficult period you went through, which helped you grow and taught you so many things, you mentioned therapy. Could you tell me, not so much what happened, but what your experience with therapy was like? I mean, what did it give you at first?

“I sought help from a psychologist because I was going through a very difficult time and couldn’t find a way to move forward. We’re expected to deal with situations… that I find mentally overwhelming, at just 21 years old, when you have no professional experience, because in the end, everything comes at you so fast.

“So I went to a psychologist, and completely changed my perspective. I mean, I started seeing everything negative that happened to me as a step forward, thinking: this is something that will help you improve, something you needed to go through.

“Everything changed when I changed my perspective. I used to be very hard on myself, very hard, I demanded so much of myself. Now I’m someone who feels much more at ease.

“We can all fail, make mistakes, go through difficult situations, So the way I treat myself, the way I respond to myself, the way I talk to myself, I think it plays a crucial role in maintaining a consistently positive mindset.”

Today, with the maturity and mindset you’ve developed, how do you handle pressure? Those moments when expectations are high, when your heart starts beating faster, how do you deal with those moments?

“It depends. Because speaking for myself, I see pressure as something that helps give me that extra push to… I mean, I’m someone who believes that footballers naturally feel pressure or feel a little nervous before a match, even the day before, depending on the match, some matches are more important than others, but you still feel that pressure.

“I think accepting that pressure is positive because it helps prepare you, but I haven’t had situations where pressure has paralysed me, because I experienced that in the past.

“In the past, I’ve felt that kind of pressure, and pressure that paralyses you is very negative because it’s very different from the pressure you feel when you’re in a good place mentally, which helps you prepare.

“It’s a kind of pressure, the pressure I used to feel, that completely paralyses you, it’s a pressure that makes your heart race, that makes you afraid, so they’re two different kinds of pressure. I’ve experienced both.

“The first is difficult to handle because it’s anxiety, the expectation of performing well, it’s the feeling that so many people are watching you, you have to perform well, you have to play well, and that gets into your head, and I don’t think it’s very positive.

“The other, on the other hand, helps you prepare, you feel a little nervous, you get that slight feeling of getting yourself ready, but if you manage to channel it properly, it becomes something that prepares you, a natural response to having something important to face.”

Listening to you speak and watching you play, I see someone who’s very composed. That confidence you have in yourself, have you always had it? Where does it come from?

“I’m someone who has always given the impression of being very confident. I mean, I’ve always believed I had a lot of confidence in myself, but actually, when things got tough, I didn’t have it because whenever I went through a difficult time, I doubted myself a lot.

“I think you develop that through psychological work, but also by feeling confident in your work. Those things go hand in hand. I mean, it’s not all down to the psychologist and nothing to do with my career.

“No. I mean, everything has gone hand in hand. When I started going through this process, things also started going really well for me in football, which helped along the way. So I’ve managed to develop confidence in both areas, which has made me the person I am today, someone who I believe really does have a lot of confidence in himself, because I genuinely feel it.

“It’s something I’ve worked on a lot, learning to believe in yourself, not because of what other people say or the value others place on you, but because of the value you give yourself. And that’s where I’ve found my stability, my peace and my confidence in that environment.

You’ve played alongside some incredible players. How did you manage… I mean, did you use the comparison with their talent as a way to grow rather than feeling envious, as something positive to help you improve?

“Yes. The thing is, this is very closely connected to confidence. You know? When you believe in yourself and have that confidence, you learn to appreciate what you can learn from others. If you’re perhaps someone who doesn’t have much confidence in yourself, you might feel envious of others.

“And then you fail to recognise the lesson that the other person is teaching you, even when you can learn just by watching them. So I began to understand that when I started reaching a place of emotional wellbeing, where I was more focused on the present.

“Before, because I had less confidence in myself, I compared myself to others more. Maybe I didn’t appreciate what someone else was doing or perhaps I couldn’t fully appreciate what they were trying to teach me.

“Once you manage to build a solid sense of confidence and truly believe in yourself, you see what others can teach you in a positive light. And you see it as an opportunity to grow, you look at it from a different perspective.

“So it’s not just about what people might be teaching you directly. I’ve learned from many players just by watching, just by paying attention. But sometimes I’ve learned from negative things too. I mean, not just from positive things. I understand that learning isn’t just about someone saying: ‘Listen, you need to do this, and I think you should do it this way’.

“No, it’s also about how they behave. For example, right now we have Luka Modric in the team. And I pay a lot of attention to him, to how he behaves, to the humility he shows, to how he handles different situations.

“And that’s something you understand when you’re comfortable with yourself. If you’re not comfortable with yourself, I don’t think you’ll be able to understand that. I mean, you won’t see the other person as a positive example. You’ll see them in a negative light.

And when you’re experiencing a great moment, are you able to recognise it? To feel proud of yourself? To enjoy that moment of happiness? To stop and appreciate it?

“That’s something I struggle with, for example. It’s something I find difficult because I’m aware of it, but I think it’s something that people generally struggle with because we tend to notice the negative things more or give more importance to difficult moments than to the positive ones.

“And I think that’s a mistake everyone makes, I don’t know why. But I’m trying to work on it, I’m aware of it. But I have to tell you that if we were to compare how much I enjoy the good moments with how much I suffer during the bad ones, no.

“I suffer more during the bad moments than I enjoy the good ones. And unfortunately, that’s something I still haven’t learned how to manage or how to truly enjoy those positive moments because we’re also here to enjoy the good moments and to experience them fully. But I find that harder than dealing with a difficult moment.”

I really like how much you’ve grown in terms of maturity, everything you’ve come to understand. So I’d like to ask you one last question to reflect on everything. It’s a simple question: How are you?

Not how you are today, but looking at your life, what kind of chapter are you going through right now? Is Mario happy?

Mario is very happy. I mean, this whole conversation we’ve had has a lot to do with the positive place I’m in right now. If I were going through a difficult time, perhaps there are many things I wouldn’t understand the way I do now.

“But I’m in a really good place right now because I’m exactly where I want to be. I’m somewhere I’m appreciated and loved. I became a father a year ago. I have a wife who loves me very much too.

“It’s as though so many things are coming together, things are going well in my career, things are going well with my family. I have friends who I believe are truly valuable people. I also have a family that means so much to me.

“So it’s like everything is going really well right now. So I feel very balanced, I feel very much at peace. I feel like I’m exactly where I want to be. I’m really appreciated. So right now I’m at a very happy point in my life. Very happy.

“If negative things happen, I have a support system I can rely on. I have that support that helps me feel okay. And I think that’s really important because I believe you’ll always experience difficult moments in life. So there’s no way to completely avoid those difficult moments, you can’t, it’s just not possible.

“And life would lose its meaning. But having that safety net and that support system, I think it also allows you to thrive in every other area of your life. So I’m very happy. Right now I feel incredibly happy, very peaceful, very balanced, very happy with myself too, and I’m genuinely very happy we had this conversation because I’m really interested in all these psychological aspects,” he concluded.
