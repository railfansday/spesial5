Beau Lamarre-Condon’s ‘wounded pride’ after breakup with Jesse Baird led to ‘darker plan for revenge’, crown alleges

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Prosecutor argues ‘stalking and break-in’ was ‘astonishing conduct for a serving police officer’

Beau Lamarre-Condon’s “wounded pride” after his breakup with Jesse Baird drove him to a “far darker plan for revenge”, a court has heard.

The former police officer is accused of fatally shooting Baird and his new partner, Luke Davies, with his police service weapon in Baird’s Paddington home in Sydney on 19 February 2024.

He has pleaded not guilty to two counts of murder and one charge of aggravated break and enter.

The crown prosecutor Brett Hatfield SC began delivering his closing statement on Thursday as the double murder trial entered its ninth week.

He told the court Lamarre-Condon was infatuated, obsessed and insecure about his relationship with Baird from an early stage after the two began casually dating in late 2022.

Following their first breakup in April 2023, Hatfield alleged that obsession grew.

The prosecutor pointed to evidence given by Lamarre-Condon’s friends, who previously gave evidence the former officer told them he had watched Baird have sex with other men, broken into his home to access his phone and watched Baird’s home from his mother’s car.

“That’s classic stalking and the break-in, [is] again, very astonishing conduct for a serving police officer – to take such a risk to do that type of thing over essentially sexual jealousy,” Hatfield said.

The two briefly got back together but Baird ended things for the final time in November of that year.

Around this time, Lamarre-Condon “clearly … wanted to harm Mr Baird”, the prosecution argued.

“His wounded pride had driven him to a far darker plan to revenge, which ultimately led him to enter [Baird’s home] with his police Glock, disguised as an Uber Eats driver on 19 February 2024,” Hatfield said.

The defence had argued the two men died in struggles with the accused when he acted in “self-defence”.

Lamarre-Condon had previously told the court he was aiming to shoot Baird in the shoulder to get him to drop a knife, and accidentally shot him a second time as they fell to the ground.

But Hatfield told the court the injury from the first shot would cause someone to pass out.

“Common sense renders it completely improbable that you could suffer such an injury yet immediately grab hold of a pistol with your left and forcefully pull a person brandishing it to the ground,” Hatfield said.

“Such a scenario, you might think, is fanciful in the extreme.”

He argued Lamarre-Condon could be convicted on the strength of the forensic and ballistics evidence alone.

Forensic evidence of the directionality of the bullet and the wounds on Baird’s body indicated Lamarre-Condon shot Baird in the back as the other man fled towards the back door, Hatfield alleged.

The height of the bullet marks meant Baird must have been ducking or stooping at the time, according to the prosecution.

Hatfield said the near-vertical trajectory of the bullet through the floor spoke to a controlled shot through Baird’s temple, with the prosecutor calling it an “execution-style shot”.

The timing between the two shots allowed only for a quick two to three steps, the crown prosecutor told the court.

“Not the bizarre wrestling and yanking the accused has described,” Hatfield said.

At this point, Davies must have realised something was “seriously amiss”, the prosecutor said, and attempted to call triple zero.

There was no doubt he was then killed by a bullet that passed through the door and into his head, Hatfield said, telling the court there was gunshot stippling on the door but not the body.

Aspects of the defence’s case – that Baird lunged at Lamarre-Condon with a knife and that Davies picked up the gun and pointed it at the accused – came only from the former officer himself, Hatfield said.

“Nothing independent of him supports any of what he said about those events,” Hatfield told the jury.

The trial before Justice Natalie Adams continues.
