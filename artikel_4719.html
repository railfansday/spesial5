The existence of zoos of all sorts in which a wide variety of animals are held captive raises countless ethical questions. Despite the best of intentions, most zoos do not overcome what are called "captivity effects" and existing laws do not protect their residents. For these and other reasons, I was pleased to learn of a new, well-researched book titled Born to Be Wild: Reckoning with the Zoo in the Age of Extinction, in which readers are taken behind the scenes of the most iconic zoos by award-winning writer Andrew Westoll, who shows that zoos often fail to protect the animals they work to save.

I found the book to be a valuable, balanced view of what really is happening on the ground at most zoos. It is not an anti-zoo rant but does ask a lot from them in the years to come.

Marc Bekoff: Why did you write Born to Be Wild?

Andrew Westoll: The central idea at the heart of the book is that no matter what you think about the zoo, we can all agree that absent a convincing argument to the contrary, every animal should be able to live the life it evolved to live.

One of my goals is to model how to explore a topic that challenges or confuses us with an open mind. Another is to help open a renewed dialogue about the position and power of a place that, aside from houses of worship, are the most influential public institutions in our society. Around the world, about 700 million people visit a zoo or aquarium every year. That’s about a tenth of humanity. Shouldn’t we all know a little more about how these places work?

And finally, my other main goal is to take the reader to the heart of the modern zoo by taking them behind the scenes at some of the most interesting zoos on the planet. Readers get to go on every adventure I did, from the urban wilds of Central Park to the remote jungles of Central Africa.

MB: What are some of the topics you consider and what are some of your major messages?

Recent reporting suggests that giant panda breeding programs in China have very little to do with actual conservation efforts, and everything to do with exporting Chinese “soft power” (sorry for the pun) around the world. I visited the giant pandas of Vienna, at the Schönbrunn Zoo, to understand why the Austrians have had such success in breeding the bears naturally (i.e., without artificial insemination). It’s a remarkable story of persistence and expertise. But I also explored the fact that most countries to receive cute and cuddly diplomats from Beijing have first signed major trading deals with the Chinese state.

Panda diplomacy is a troubling, exploitative practice in which the bears’ welfare is rarely the main priority. The next time we see a head of state posing with pandas at their local zoos, we should remember this fact.

Breeding programs at zoo are enormously controversial, because no matter what a zoo tells us, animals born into zoos are destined to live their entire lives in a zoo. These breeding programs are supposed to be about building “assurance populations,” genetically robust populations that could one day be released back into the wild.

However, when you look deeply at the breeding data, you discover that the majority of vertebrate populations being bred in zoos are not genetically viable (i.e., not genetically diverse enough to counteract the impacts of inbreeding). In other words, zoos around the world are failing at their most fundamental task: to build healthy, sustainable populations into the future.

MB: How does your work differ from others that are concerned with some of the same general topics?

AW: Most books about the zoo are either scholarly or theoretical in nature, focused on specific histories or through the lens of animal rights. But most of them don’t do a great job of meeting regular people where they live. I want to engage everyday people in these ethical and moral questions, to help them think through their impressions of the modern zoo without feeling they’ve missed an entire semester of college!

MB: What has it been like to levy criticisms at the modern zoo as a whole, when so many people who work in them are well-intentioned and clearly care a great deal for their animals?

AW: This is probably the hardest part of my job as a journalist, to separate the people I’ve met and spoken with from the larger topics I’m dealing with. And this was especially hard in this case. Just about every zoo employee I interviewed or met while researching this book was a passionate animal person, wholly dedicated to the well-being of their charges, whether they were curators, keepers, or marketing folks.

But after reading so much of the zoo literature, where so many progressive people in the industry are begging their institutions to modernize and change for the better, and after learning just how deeply the zoo is failing to meet its own targets, it became clear to me that many zoo professionals have a permanent blind spot when it comes to considering the role of the modern zoo and the ethics of captivity in the 21st century.

It seems to me that if the zoo wants to maintain its social license to operate, it is going to have to radically update its thinking and its operational mandate. Zoo insiders have been calling for this to happen for more than 30 years now. Unfortunately, we’re still a long way off from the zoo renaissance we’ve been promised.
