Stressful jobs do, in fact, pay more. But there’s a catch

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Do more stressful jobs come with a bigger paycheck? It depends.

According to a recent study conducted by online resume building platform Resume Genius, the most stressful jobs pay an average of 62% more than the least stressful jobs. And yet, not everyone is enjoying a high-pressure premium.

Workers with a high school diploma or postsecondary certificate typically see a 24% pay premium for taking on a more stressful job; those with a Master’s earn 27% more in high-stress roles, while those with a doctoral or professional degree earn a whopping 78% more.

Among bachelor’s degree holders, however, higher work stress doesn’t pay. In fact, bachelor’s degree holders in high-pressure roles earn 1% less on average than bachelor-holding colleagues in lower-pressure positions.

“Not all forms of workplace stress are rewarded equally,” says Resume Genius career expert Nathan Soto. “The data we found suggests that a stress premium exists, but it’s concentrated in particular educational and occupational groups, rather than being a universal feature of the labor market.”

The study is consistent with other research that suggests workers are less willing to take on more stressful jobs, even if they come with a higher salary. A survey of 14,000 workers by emotional insight platform Hint App, for example, found that 58% would reject a promotion that required them to be available after hours.

Putting a price on workplace pressure

Soto says Resume Genius developed a “Stress Index” to help quantify workplace pressure across 754 occupations.

One-third of that 100-point scale is based on the role’s “Stress Tolerance” level, according to the U.S. Department of Labor’s Occupational Information Network (or O*NET) database. The agency defines “Stress Tolerance” as “a tendency to cope and function effectively in stressful situations at work.”

The remaining two-thirds were based on seven stressful job attributes: time pressure, workplace conflicts, dealing with unpleasant or angry people, the consequence of errors, responsibility for others’ health and safety, competition and long working hours. Stress Index scores were then compared against the latest pay data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

“Among six-figure occupations, workers in high-stress jobs were much more likely to deal with unpleasant or angry people, handle conflict regularly and face time pressure every day,” Soto says. “Responsibility for other people’s health and safety and the seriousness of potential mistakes were also major factors.”

Soto explains that high-paying, highly stressful jobs that don’t require a bachelor’s degree often involve physical risk, demanding work conditions or significant responsibility. For example, electrical power-line installers earn an average of $95,320 and have a Stress Index level of 97. Detectives and criminal investigators, who also aren’t required to earn a bachelor’s, scored 100 on the stress index and earned an average of $93,790.

High-paying jobs that require a professional or doctorate degree, meanwhile, often require specialized training or expertise, come with greater responsibility as well as higher stakes. For example, anesthesiologists scored 100 on the Stress Index and earned an average of $391,490 per year.

But: “Jobs that require bachelor’s degrees follow a different pattern,” Soto says. “Many of them pay high salaries without necessarily rewarding additional workplace stress with higher pay.”

More than half of the six-figure jobs included in the study scored above 67 on the Stress Index, but the researchers did identify 19 high-paying low-stress jobs. They include electronics engineer, software developer, statistician, data scientist, computer and information research scientist and actuary.

A high tolerance for stress has a dollar value

Many workers look to avoid stress, and are even willing to take a pay cut for it. But those who can take the heat can often earn more.

According to a 2025 study conducted by researchers at the University of Erlangen-Nuremberg in Germany, workers are willing to take a 9.6% wage cut to avoid frequent, tight deadlines, and earn about 6% less to avoid having to multitask often.

“Avoiding pressure comes at a price. So you have to think whether you’re willing to pay that price,” explains economics professor Markus Nagler, who co-authored the study.

Swapping a low-pressure job for a high-pressure job within the same occupation, according to the study, came with a 12% earnings premium on average, though half of that raise was due to an increase in working hours.

The researchers, however, also found that those who didn’t mind certain stressful job aspects were more likely to find jobs that paid them for that sacrifice. For example, those who take on jobs with tight deadlines may be able to stomach the pressure more than the average worker.

“The kind of people who select into these jobs and remain in these jobs typically are those who have the highest tolerance for these kinds of working conditions,” Nagler says. “The problem is that you choose a job, but you don’t get to choose specific aspects of the job.”

Not everyone gets to choose a lower-stress job

The research suggests that workers in higher-stress jobs tend to earn more. But other research finds that workers in low-wage jobs are more likely to face harsher working conditions.

According to a 2023 study published in the American Economic Review, workers earning lower wages were less willing to give up part of their earnings to enjoy non-work amenities like schedule flexibility, more paid time off and less heavy lifting. The study also found that higher-wage workers are more likely to enjoy those additional perks than workers in lower-wage jobs.

“The people that are getting paid more are also getting better working conditions,” explains one of the study’s co-authors and an associate professor at Penn Medicine, David Powell. “People who are compensated better want a combination of higher wages and better amenities, so when you look at disparities, and you include amenities, they typically widen.”

That is not to say that high earners aren’t dealing with difficult working conditions. However, Powell’s research suggests those with higher earnings are in a better position to sacrifice some of their earnings for a lower-stress role. Those struggling to make ends meet, however, are typically less willing to forfeit some of their pay for better working conditions.

As a result, lower earners tend to enjoy fewer of the amenities that make work less stressful, while higher earners can more easily swap a high-stress job for a more laid-back alternative, even if it pays less.
