The most dangerous election disinformation campaigns

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

You scrolled through your feed, and a video clip of a candidate’s speech popped up. It doesn’t matter who you planned to vote for, the video struck an emotional—and negative—response. The clip was not AI-generated; the candidate really uttered the words you found upsetting. But the story the video told was fundamentally false. That was the 2026 midterms.

Candidates and their supporters spent a whopping $80 million to make AI-generated ads, as part of what AdImpact projected will be the most expensive election cycle on record. This is one attempt to capture eyeballs and sway voters. Another is through foreign interference. In just one example, researchers tracking a Russian influence operation recently found a fake video masquerading as a Mother Jones report that captured over 11 million views.

But disinformation is not stopping at deepfakes. Authentic videos that are repurposed and stripped of context represent a new frontier. I am not talking about a mere twisting of words. What we’re seeing is next level.

THE NEW LOOK OF DISINFORMATION

Think about the recent catastrophic flooding in Nepal and India. Some footage released within hours of the floods, intended to showcase the magnitude of the disaster, turned out to be from entirely different events. The footage was authentic. It just happened to be from a glacial flood in Alaska, a landslide in Japan, or a flood that occurred years before. These videos racked up millions of views. They also caused panic among friends and relatives trying to find information about their missing loved ones, and they potentially hindered rescue efforts by confusing the locations where help was needed.

Now, consider the same tactic applied in the political sphere—real speeches and words taken drastically out of context, coming from events that could have happened years earlier. 

One of the most notable examples involved Michigan’s recently elected Democratic Senator Abdul El-Sayed. In August, he was eviscerated by Republicans for apparently calling for Sharia law to take over in America. Social media went crazy. Was the clip real? Yes, it was from a speech he gave in 2022. But the meaning was quite different when you look at its entirety in context. So different that Politifact gave it a “pants on fire” rating for its meaning being distorted. 

In Georgia’s hotly contested Senate race, Democratic Senator Jon Ossoff ran an ad of Republican Mike Collins urging Americans to get off Social Security and return to work. But that’s not exactly how it went down. The audio came from a broader conversation about tax policy, government revenue, and workforce participation. Collins was not saying retirees should get back into the workforce and lose their benefits (Politifact rated this one as “mostly false”).

In both cases, the recordings were authentic. The misleading impressions came from stripping away the information necessary to understand what was being said, not from manipulating the original material. You may believe that this is just par for the course in a political campaign, but just because something is common does not make it safe, normal, or acceptable. 

THE IMPORTANCE OF CONTEXTUAL UNDERSTANDING

This tactic is becoming effective for two main reasons. Video is now our most prominent data source. Everything is recorded, so if someone is looking for a soundbite to make someone look bad, there is plenty of material available, if they’re willing to dig for it. The second issue is more technical. Deepfake detectors look for signs of manipulation. Authentic videos pass that test. Determining whether a clip is deliberately misleading requires a new set of inquiries, identifying when and where the video took place, what was happening around it, and what the broader context revealed.

Making such determinations traditionally has taken a lot of people a lot of time to dig through archives. Even with the deep war chests candidates possess, they’re not going to devote the human resources needed to debunk every false video, at least not in time for that work to make a dent in the video’s impact. So, campaigns take hits, and candidates can spend considerable time and energy trying to explain past comments and rebuild trust. 

WHAT’S THE FIX?

In 2026, voters and media companies are learning that two things can simultaneously be true: A clip can be legitimate while what it depicts is inaccurate. 

Voters are overwhelmed with election messages. Expecting them to research every clip they see is highly unlikely. Candidates and their backers count on this. Media companies and social platforms bear a greater responsibility, however. Fact-checking and moderation capabilities are essential to reduce the rapid spread of disinformation. Interestingly, while generative AI can be used to create convincing fakes, a different application of AI can be a powerful tool for identifying misleading content and stopping its spread.

As good as AI is getting at generating realistic videos, it’s also getting good at understanding them. Solutions that can read a video and its contextual clues can be indispensable in determining whether an authentic video is being misrepresented online. Imagine an instant army of agent fact-checkers. When AI understands video the way a human does, it recognizes not only what is said and who’s speaking, but what else is on the screen and what the setting looks like. It can also search massive archives to see if that moment has appeared anywhere else. And, at the agent layer, tech can flag suspicious content, assembling supporting evidence and escalating cases to human reviewers in minutes.

The technology that helps media companies and platforms find original footage, restore full context, and flag misleading claims will enable a faster response, not just to political ads but any sort of controversial content that might catch fire. Stopping the spread of disinformation is a perpetual problem. Thankfully, we are now building intelligent tools that can help.

Jae Lee is CEO and cofounder of TwelveLabs.
