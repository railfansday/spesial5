The AfD and neo-Nazi networks: How close are the connections?

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

The Alternative for Germany party (AfD) has become a focal point for the far right. Many of its members and staff have ties to ethnonationalist or neo-Nazi organizations.

The electoral successes of the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) have brought the party closer than ever to taking power in Germany. For the 2029 federal election, it has set itself an ambitious goal: to see its co-chair Alice Weidel to become Germany's next chancellor.

This raises an even sharper question: what political ideas does the party stand for, which has been classified by Germany's domestic intelligence agency as a "suspected right-wing extremist" organization and, in several federal states, is already considered "confirmed right-wing extremist"?

Many political scientists have observed that the AfD has become increasingly radicalized since its founding in 2013.

"Viewed as a whole, the AfD is an extreme right-wing party that also displays tendencies associated with a fascist movement," said Fabian Virchow, a researcher on right-wing extremism at Düsseldorf University of Applied Sciences, in an interview with DW.

Virchow identifies different factions and strategic tendencies within the party, some of which are at odds with one another. Party leader Alice Weidel, for example, is generally seen as a proponent of a free-market approach that seeks to keep the state as small as possible. Others, by contrast, favor a strong state shaped by an ethnically homogeneous people, among them Björn Höcke, the influential far-right AfD leader in the state of Thuringia.

Virchow sees a "normalization" of right-wing extremist positions within the AfD. In his view, this has made the party more attractive to neo-Nazi circles: "Today, many people see greater room for ethnonationalist and radical right-wing ideas."

Support for this observation comes from the latest findings of the Leipzig Authoritarianism Study, one of the most widely cited long-term studies tracking right-wing extremist attitudes in Germany. The researchers behind the study have identified a rise in right-wing extremist views within German society, including a growing acceptance of ideologies rooted in the tradition of National Socialism.

Particularly striking is the finding that around two-thirds of people with firmly entrenched far-right worldviews would vote for the AfD. According to the study, the AfD has achieved what earlier far-right parties in Germany never managed: "to unite large segments of the electorate with anti-democratic attitudes behind it."

The debate has been further fueled by several recent incidents within the party. In 2026 alone, numerous AfD politicians and staff members made headlines over alleged ties to neo-Nazi organizations:

In August 2026, police carried out a large-scale raid targeting fifteen people. Authorities suspect them of continuing the activities of the so-called "Artgemeinschaft." Before it was banned in 2023, the group was considered one of Germany's largest and most influential neo-Nazi cadre organizations. Particularly explosive was the fact that two of those targeted in the raid are sitting AfD members of the Saxony-Anhalt state parliament. Their attorneys deny the allegations.

According to reporting by the German daily, Welt, four staff members of the AfD's parliamentary group in Saxony-Anhalt are former members of the now-banned "Heimattreue Deutsche Jugend" (HDJ), a neo-Nazi youth organization rooted in National Socialist racial ideology that organized paramilitary camps for young people. Two of the staff members held leadership positions within the HDJ and now work closely with AfD politician Ulrich Siegmund, who is seeking to become the AfD's first state premier in December.

Remarkably, both organizations, the "Artgemeinschaft" and the HDJ, are on the AfD's so-called incompatibility list, which is intended to draw a line between the party and extremist organizations and movements. In principle, anyone with a history of membership in such groups is supposed to be excluded from the party.

But the list appears to be little more than a dead letter. In 2020, former AfD federal executive board member Andreas Kalbitz was expelled from the party after failing to disclose his membership in the "Heimattreue Deutsche Jugend" on his application to join. Yet according to the news magazine Spiegel, he is once again working for the AfD, this time as a staffer for Saxony-Anhalt state legislator Hans-Thomas Tillschneider, who has since become deputy speaker of the state parliament.

The question of the AfD's relationship to National Socialism has dogged the party since its founding in 2013. Even in its early years, co-founder and first party leader Bernd Lucke sparked controversy when he described democracy and parliamentary politics in Germany as "degenerate," a term the Nazis used to disparage art and culture that did not conform to their ideological worldview.

In Germany, references to the Nazi dictatorship (1933-1945) under Adolf Hitler remain politically taboo because of the country's responsibility for World War Two and the Holocaust, the systematic mass murder of Europe's Jews.

If AfD members or their staff have come under scrutiny because of past involvement in ethnonationalist or neo-Nazi organizations, they have generally sought to distance themselves from those backgrounds or from the ideologies associated with them.

But how credible are these attempts at distancing? Laura Neumann* has worked with many people seeking to leave the far-right scene. She works for "Nordverbund Ausstieg Rechts," a network of organizations that supports far-right cadres and activists as they disengage from the movement. "A successful exit is the result of a professionally guided process," she said in an interview with DW.

Neumann describes leaving the far-right scene as a challenging process of self-confrontation. "When it comes to someone leaving the movement, the first question I always ask is: How does that person talk about their past? Have they genuinely come to terms with it?"

Based on the experience of exit counselors, one principle generally holds true: the longer and more deeply a person has been immersed in far-right circles, the more difficult it becomes to distance themselves from those beliefs and affiliations.

DW requested a statement from the AfD's federal executive board regarding its handling of party members who were previously active in ethnonationalist or neo-Nazi organizations. Individual lawmakers and parliamentary staff members were also asked to comment. None responded.

The party's and its politicians' silence in the face of such allegations reflects an observation made by political scientist Fabian Virchow: "Within the AfD, there is no longer a perceived need to distance oneself from radical far-right positions. That is part of this process of normalization."

Virchow says that right-wing extremism appears to have advanced so far into the mainstream of German society that efforts to scandalize it now remain largely ineffective.
