Scientists identify two ages when children may be most vulnerable to screen time

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Screen time at certain ages may be more closely tied to children's future learning and memory than previously recognized. A new study tracking children from age 1 to 8 found that those who spent more time watching screens during infancy and around the start of elementary school tended to perform worse academically at age 9 and have weaker working memory at age 10.5.

The findings suggest that when children use screens may be just as important as how much time they spend in front of them. Two ages in particular, 1 and 6, emerged as potentially sensitive periods in childhood development.

As digital devices become an increasingly common part of childhood, understanding their potential long-term effects has become a growing priority. Recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Academy of Pediatrics generally emphasize avoiding screen exposure during the first 18 to 24 months of life and limiting it to less than one hour a day for children ages 2 to 5. However, many young children spend considerably more time using screens.

Tracking Screen Time Throughout Childhood

Despite growing concerns about children's digital habits, previous research has produced mixed findings about the relationship between screen time and cognitive development. Many earlier studies examined children at a single point in time, concentrated on children already attending school, or did not regularly track screen habits throughout early childhood.

Following the same children over several years allows researchers to investigate whether screen exposure at particular ages is more strongly associated with later outcomes. This approach can also help researchers better account for the influence of family circumstances and environmental factors.

Scientists from Inserm and the National University of Singapore addressed these gaps in a study published in the World Journal of Pediatrics. The researchers examined data from 502 children participating in the Growing Up in Singapore Towards healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) birth cohort.

Parents reported their children's screen viewing habits at six different points between infancy and middle childhood. The researchers then evaluated the children's academic achievement and working memory several years later.

Working memory is the brain's ability to temporarily hold and manipulate information. It plays an essential role in everyday learning, allowing children to follow instructions, solve math problems, understand sentences, and keep track of information while completing tasks.

The researchers found that greater screen exposure at certain stages of childhood was associated with lower academic achievement and poorer working memory later in life. These relationships were particularly consistent among children who spent more time watching screens at age 1 and around age 6, when formal schooling typically begins.

Children who accumulated more screen time throughout childhood also tended to achieve lower academic scores. However, the strength of the associations varied depending on when the exposure occurred, suggesting that some developmental stages may be more sensitive than others.

One possible explanation is that screen viewing can take time away from activities that support cognitive development, including conversations with caregivers, imaginative play, and hands-on exploration of the physical world. These experiences provide opportunities for children to develop language, attention, memory, and problem-solving skills.

"The effect sizes we saw at age 1 were the largest among all time points we examined," the authors said. "That suggests early infancy may be a window of heightened sensitivity, when the developing brain is particularly vulnerable to the displacement of learning interactions by screen time. We were also surprised to see that while screen use at age 2 and 3 did not show significant links, the associations re-emerged at age 6 -- when children enter formal schooling. So it is not just about early years; screen use later in childhood still matters."

Although the study identifies associations rather than proving that screen time directly causes poorer cognitive or academic outcomes, the results highlight the potential importance of managing children's screen habits at different ages.

Why Even Small Differences in Screen Time Matter

According to the researchers, the results reinforce the principle that "less is better" when it comes to children's screen time.

An additional hour of daily screen viewing may not produce a noticeable difference in an individual child's academic abilities. However, when similar patterns occur across a large population, even relatively small differences in average performance could increase the number of children who experience academic difficulties.

The researchers suggest that efforts to encourage healthier screen habits could begin during infancy, with renewed emphasis around the transition to elementary school. This approach recognizes that children's developmental needs continue to change well beyond their toddler years.

The authors also stress that the amount of time children spend in front of screens is only one part of a much larger picture. Future studies should investigate whether the quality of digital content, the types of devices children use, and whether parents watch and interact with their children during screen time influence developmental outcomes.

Understanding these differences could eventually help researchers develop more targeted recommendations that consider not only how much screen time children get, but also when and how they engage with digital technology.
