Seller’s choice or Compass choice? Here’s what Robert Reffkin said to his investors

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

On Compass’s Q3 earnings call, CEO Robert Reffkin framed private first marketing as a listing win and traffic driver. He cited 63% adoption in some markets and 68% year over year website traffic growth, raising questions about seller outcomes.  AI Summary

For two years, the case for private listings has come down to two words: seller’s choice. The homeowner decides. Who could argue with that? Not me. I’ve said for years that a seller who understands the trade-offs has every right to keep a sale quiet.

But here is an interesting question: When the largest brokerage in the country explains its private listing program to the people who own its stock, does it sound like a program built for sellers?

On Nov. 5, we found out. Compass CEO Robert Reffkin spent an hour with Wall Street analysts on the company’s third-quarter earnings call. It was a strong quarter. Record third-quarter revenue of $3.9 billion, and the first profitable third quarter in company history. Credit where it’s due.

Analysts don’t ask how sellers are doing. They ask how the company wins. So Reffkin told them. After reviewing the call, eight things stood out to me. I’m paraphrasing, except where you see quotation marks. Don’t take my word for any of it. The call is public. Go listen.

Reffkin introduced three-phase marketing as a listing strategy that helps Compass agents win more business. That was the stated benefit. In an hour of talking, nobody offered a single number on whether this is helpful to sellers or not, only that it is beneficial to the agents and company.

In cities with no industry marketing restrictions, he said 63% of new Compass-brand listings now launch with the three-phase plan. In some cities it topped 70%. He told investors that once the restrictions he considers artificial are gone, a good majority of listings would start as a private exclusive or a Coming Soon.

A choice is something some people make and others don’t. When seven out of 10 go the same way, maybe every one of those sellers got there on their own. Or maybe that’s what’s being pushed at the kitchen table.

He said competitors have tried to label Compass listings as hidden, and that the See It First campaign is part of a rebranding exercise to answer that. He insisted every listing is available to all agents and all buyers. Then he explained the goal in plain English: “We just want you to see it first on our site.” He called that a natural thing for a company to want.

I agree. It is natural for a company to want that. But let’s be clear – it is still hidden from all the MLSs and portals. The name of the program should be “See It First At Compass, See It Last On MLS.”

He reported that traffic to the Compass website was up 68% year over year in September, and said he expects it to keep climbing with See It First. He said the company’s sites now show 89,000 homes you can see early (which means 89,000 homes hidden or not allowed to be shown through the traditional MLS).

Reffkin said that within a year, the website of every sister brand will have the same features and the same inventory that is unique to the company. Sit with that word, unique. A home for sale was never supposed to be unique to one company’s website.

He described the pitch an agent can now make to a seller: list with me, and your home goes in front of every agent on our platform and the 225 million contacts in our system. He framed that as a way to compete against brokerages that haven’t built a platform of their own. He also said the more agents who are on the platform, the easier it gets to recruit more agents and bring over whole brokerages.

Reffkin told a story about an agent in Houston who had a client moving to Dallas. The agent searched the company platform for off-market Dallas listings, and the client made an offer on one. One agent was with Compass. The other was with Sotheby’s International Realty, now a Compass sister brand. It was presented as a win.

I’ve never had a problem with a broker selling their own listing, as long as the seller isn’t hurt. But when a home never reaches the open market, and the listing agent, the buyer’s agent, the lender and the title company can all belong to one company, who is left to tell that seller whether the price was the best one out there?

Three things belong on the record. First, Reffkin says nothing is hidden, and that every one of these listings is open to every agent and every buyer. Second, on the same call he welcomed Open Real Estate and said he sees a path to being involved. Third, he’s the CEO of a public company. Telling shareholders how the company wins is his job, and he’s very good at it.

I don’t fault him for that call. I’m grateful for it. It’s the clearest explanation of the strategy any of us have been given.

Any one of those statements is just business. Put them in a row and you see why so much of this industry is nervous.

Listen to what the listings are for. Winning the listing appointment. Driving traffic to the company website. Feeding leads to company agents. Recruiting other brokerages’ agents. Keeping the mortgage and the title in-house. Every one of those is a benefit to Compass. In a full hour, the benefit to the seller never got a number.

That’s why I’ve started calling it Compass Choice, not Seller’s Choice. Seller’s choice starts with what the homeowner needs. Compass Choice starts with what the company needs and wants, and then gets explained to the homeowner as their idea.

In my read of that call, this was never mainly about seller’s choice. It’s about earnings and the stock price. And when a listing strategy is built for the shareholder first, somebody else picks up the tab. The seller, when fewer buyers see the home. The buyer, when they see fewer homes.

For generations, the American way of selling homes has rested on one odd, beautiful habit. We compete for the listing. Then we cooperate on the sale. That habit is why a seller in this country gets every buyer in the market, not just one company’s buyers.

Compete for the listing. Compete for the traffic. Compete for the agents. Compete for the mortgage. Cooperating shows up in one place only: between Compass and its own sister brands. That isn’t cooperating. That’s a very big company talking to itself.

And here’s a principle, not an accusation. If launching homes privately truly got sellers more money, every one of us would have a duty to shut down the MLS tomorrow. Nobody on that call made that case. They made the case for market share.

There’s also a way to give a seller an exclusive without leaving the open market, but the MLSs and NAR have to wake up and get rid of the Clear Cooperation Policy and replace it with this model: the seller lists with one broker. That broker markets the home publicly and handles the showings. The listing still goes into the MLS, so every agent knows it’s for sale, any agent with a real buyer can call and ask the listing agent to co-broke the listing to their buyer.  Appraisers get the data. The seller keeps control. The market stays transparent. This is what all MLS and NAR should support.

Seller’s choice puts the homeowner at the head of the table. Compass Choice puts the shareholder there. I choose seller’s choice. What about you?
