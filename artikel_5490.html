Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s abrupt departure shakes AI industry’s foundations

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

THIS WEEK IN ENTERPRISE by Robert Hof

Honeypot or not, the Chinese executive/accused spy with whom Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang admitted an affair this week should have been a flashing red light to him. So should fraternizing with one of your own VPs — and reportedly another employee too!

In the end, all that, and maybe more to come, was just too much for Nvidia’s board to bear despite the commanding position Huang (pictured) held not only at Nvidia but in the entire artificial intelligence industry. Indeed, the speed with which he resigned — spectacularly stupid as his behavior was — still makes me wonder if there are other shoes to drop, especially on the legal or national security fronts. Market researcher SemiAnalysis thinks it came down to pressure from the U.S. intelligence community that’s very unhappy Nvidia chips have apparently been smuggled to China.

Huang was something of an AI puppet master, providing the chips and software to power the entire industry as well as seemingly unlimited cash to fund and backstop everyone from AI leaders such as OpenAI and Anthropic to innumerable cloud compute providers worldwide. No single person inside or outside Nvidia seems likely to fill that role.

However, Nvidia really is much more than Jensen Huang. Interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress helped craft the financial arrangements that in part put Nvidia at the center of AI — though for what it’s worth, Huang himself wasn’t a fan lately. Some of his direct reports such as high-performance computing chief Ian Buck seem quite capable of carrying on, if perhaps not with Huang’s panache. One company is never one person, and shouldn’t be. I expect a less centralized Nvidia going forward, whoever’s at the top.

More than that, AI is much more than Jensen Huang, and it will continue to be going forward. It’s easy to forget that although Huang brilliantly helped create this new wave, AI and its enablers also are what made him a rock star. But that time is now over. Nvidia and certainly the AI industry shouldn’t be depending on one person to keep everything afloat anyway.

The reverberations of his sudden and shocking departure will continue to echo for a long time to come across Nvidia, its investors watching hundreds of billions of market cap evaporate, legions of Jensen fanboys and the entire artificial intelligence ecosystem. A lot of people are hoping desperately that he didn’t just pop the AI bubble (though others are hoping he did). It seems unlikely this will be the pin, but we’re dealing with a lot of unlikely things in the past week or so, for better and worse, and this shock is clearly rattling the foundations.

All that managed to overshadow what otherwise would be the big news this week: Anthropic went on the road to pitch its initial public offering. It’s hoping to raise more than $64 billion and keep the AI trade humming in Nvidia’s near-term absence. Looks like next Thursday is the big day.

And it’s not the only good news in AI — that is, if the IPO is successful. After a difficult month on the stock front, uber neocloud CoreWeave provided a pleasant AI surprise with upside earnings and guidance, lifting its shares almost 25% Wednesday. AI chipmaker Cerebras’ shares rose almost 18% in late trading Thursday on positive guidance and a Microsoft deal. Cisco Systems said demand is strong for AI infrastructure, even if investors shrugged, and chipmaking equipment supplier Applied Materials said AI boosted demand as well.

Not everyone fared so well, though. Dutch neocloud Nebius saw its shares drop almost 17% Thursday as it lowered its outlook despite close to sevenfold revenue growth in the last quarter. The stock drop no doubt was partly because of Nvidia, which is, of course, an investor. CoreWeave –yes, another Nvidia investment — lost 7% Thursday.

Couple of really big outages out there: first, Microsoft Outlook, grounding a lot of office workers, then the whole damn U.S. air traffic control system, grounding thousands of flights.

Sovereignty is on many enterprise buyers’ lips these days, the notion that enterprises need a way to assert more control over their technology estates and protect their customers’ data. TheCUBE Research’s Dave Vellante and Amit Eyal Govrin just completed the first of a series of reports on how to approach sovereignty, this one focused on the hyperscale cloud providers.

The software orchestrator Kubernetes has become boring, but as SiliconANGLE’s John Furrier said at this week’s KubeCon in Salt Lake City, that means it’s a success — with lessons for AI developers. “A lot of things that are happening in cloud-native that were involved in microservices in Kubernetes world are actually the same challenges in the AI world,” he noted.

IBM has been working hard on quantum computing for a long time now, but at its Quantum Developer Conference in Chicago this week, it showed significant progress, according to Dave Vellante. Its goal now is to show actual industry impact by 2028, a laudable goal that presents a lot of challenges, Dave wrote in his Special Breaking Analysis this week.

Next week the SC26 high-performance computing conference comes to Chicago, and theCUBE and theCUBE Research will be onsite with interviews and analysis. There’s also Microsoft Ignite in San Francisco. And earnings next week include… Nvidia! Bring the popcorn.
