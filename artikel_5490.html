Ohio Becomes the Epicenter of Data Center Backlash After Tax Credits Cost State’s Schools $374 Million

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Angela Huffman, a sixth-generation farmer, spent 15 years in agriculture policy in Columbus, OH, and Washington, DC. But her focus lately has been 10 miles from her Wharton, OH, sheep farm.

On June 4, Huffman picked up her local newspaper, the Daily Chief-Union. The above-the-fold headline revealed a developer was eyeing a $900 million data center in nearby Upper Sandusky.

The headline "stopped me cold," Huffman says. The proposal would have planted a 146-acre Smartland Energy data center campus, with a natural gas power plant, on the north edge of town.

"We were already worried about a big development coming in and extracting the town's resources," Huffman tells Realtor.com®. Townsfolk were already concerned after another recent proposal for a soybean processing plant.

"What does that do to the community?" she says.

Huffman, CEO of Farm Action, says her family has owned its nearby farm for six generations. Organizing concerned neighbors, they began filling planning commission meetings and peppering officials with questions about the impact of the development. They also questioned if it should be approved on industrial zoning, as neighbors had hoped. A petition opposing the project drew 1,100 signatures.

Many residents worried about how such a major project could change their rural way of life. And Upper Sandusky wasn't alone.

Ohio was the heart of data center resistance in the midterms. Voters in 18 of its 88 counties backed 21 bans or restrictions on development of those projects. One of those was in Upper Sandusky, where voters approved a charter amendment blocking large data centers within the limits of the 6,600-person town.

"They wanted to use about a third of the water the whole town uses today," Huffman says. "I'm not against AI or development, but can a town of this size absorb a development like that?"

But the state already hosts dozens of large centers. And they already exact a cost.

A new study shows that the state's data center tax break derived its schools of almost $374 million in revenue in 2025 alone.

Republican Gov. Mike DeWine paused the tax breaks in May after the state realized developers were exempted from $2.1 billion in taxes in 2024 and 2025, far more than initally projected. Gov.-elect Amy Acton has promised further scrutiny.

Good Jobs First and Policy Matters Ohio studied where those missing tax dollars would have gone. The state's Fair School Funding Plan allocates some sales tax to schools. So by exempting data center developers from the 5.75% sales tax, those schools missed out on revenue.

“Ohio lawmakers made a choice to give data center companies a blank check, and now Ohio’s schools are paying the price,” said Hannah Halbert, Policy Matters Ohio’s executive director. “In 2025 alone, the state gave up enough revenue from this single tax break to fully update the Fair School Funding plan and close the gap.”

This comes at a time when homeowners were being asked to make up the difference. Many Ohio schools aimed to raise money through levies this year, but many were unsuccessful. The Ohio Policy Education Institute found just 24 of 66 levies on the May 2026 ballot had passed.

Upper Sandusky's school district was one of the losers—missing out on $383,000 in revenue, according to the report. While voters have approved levies for its schools in 2015, 2018, and 2023, it's still been forced to cancel classes this year because it can't install air conditioning in century-old buildings that are too expensive to rebuild.

"Our schools were struggling," Huffman says. "It tells you a lot about when these big tech companies come along, and if they are paying their fair share."

Voter anger has put data center incentives in the spotlight. NBC exit polls show 76% of voters opposed data center building in their communities, and a majority considered them "crucial" to their votes.

Polls show broad concerns that the pace of data center construction could drive up power costs, raise land prices, and create environmental problems. Recent Realtor.com research on data centers found they're increasingly moving into rural areas.

That's despite a major spending spree by the industry. Ohio was barraged with $1.8 million in data center ads in the fall, Pew Research Center found—more than Texas, Wisconsin, and other large states. AI and crypto political action committees spent an estimated $65 million backing Republican Sen. John Husted, who lost reelection to Democrat Sherrod Brown on Nov. 3.

Developers, after all, have big plans in the state, having pitched $168.7 billion in new projects.

Regulation of data center developments and AI could be a major factor for new Democratic leaders in Columbus and Washington, DC. Sober to that reality, tech stocks tanked after the election.

Acton says she supports a conditional moratorium on data centers. She wants to see them cover their utility costs, build with union labor, and abide by environmental standards. She also says she wants to see incentives "reined in" for the large developments.

Artificial intelligence companies have spent the days since the election promising they'll work with cities and residents on data centers. OpenAI published a "blueprint for responsible development" promising to slow the technology.

Meta spokesperson Andy Stone cited a commitment "to pay our own energy and water costs so they're not passed onto consumers, fund new and upgraded grid infrastructure, and support local communities."

Ohio isn't alone, either. Virginia, another data center hot spot, spent over $4 billion in data center incentives over 10 years, according to a state report. It and other hot spots like California and Texas have also moved to restrict data center developments this year.

A recent study by the Wharton School indicates that data centers have indeed increased industrial electricity prices in the nation. But it also said that residential customers absorbed relatively little of that price gain.

Ohio, in the meantime, has seen its housing situation worsen in the past few years. National Housing Conference data put the state first in the union for how quickly middle-income earners have been priced out. The number of jobs that can afford a typical home at a 10% down payment fell about 41% from 2020 to 2025. Teachers, firefighters, and retail supervisors are among those priced out of median-priced homes.

But data center advocates say the large developments are still necessary in the modern economy.

Dan Diorio, vice president for public policy at the Data Center Coalition, told the Ohio Tax Credit Authority that the developments benefit all people and businesses, not just AI companies.

"This is a far more efficient way of doing all the computing, processing, and storage that we need to facilitate our daily lives in the 21st century economy," Diorio said at the May hearing in Columbus. "The need for connectivity, for the internet, is driving the digital infrastructure that supports that."
