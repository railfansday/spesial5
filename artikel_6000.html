Democrats plot massive spending spree after midterm rout — and Trump could help them pull it off

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries addresses reporters on Capitol Hill following key Democratic victories in the 2026 midterm elections. Jeffries highlights the party's legislative agenda, promising to lower costs and protect essential programs for American families.

With a resounding Election Night victory behind them, Democrats are getting to work by plotting a massive government spending expansion paired with tax adjustments to pay for it. In this quest, they may find an unlikely ally in the White House.

Though the new crop of Democrats doesn’t take office until early next year, many are already announcing plans to aggressively increase government healthcare expenditures, cut checks directly to voters, restore housing assistance funding and pressure the federal government into repealing cuts to the bureaucracy.

Republicans, increasingly abandoning their once dogmatic opposition to increased social spending, could end up backing some of the measures Democrats seek to enact in their efforts to make life more affordable. President Donald Trump, for instance, expressed interest in cutting deals with liberal lawmakers on Election Night as it became apparent that the midterms were going to be disastrous for the GOP.

Trump and Vice President JD Vance, seen by many as the president's heir apparent, have made it clear that they do not categorically oppose the extension of Affordable Care Act subsidies, one of the marquee items on the Democratic agenda, but signaled that they would like to see increased verification requirements to crack down on alleged fraud. Both have also signaled an openness to raising taxes on high-earners to help the federal government balance its budget, though estimates regarding how much these new levies would raise generally fall short of the costs that would be incurred if Democrats succeed in actualizing their demands.

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, D-N.Y., and Senate Democrats are still firmly entrenched in their position that unless they get a deal on an extension to expiring Obamacare tax credits, they won't provide the votes to reopen the government. (Nathan Posner/Anadolu via Getty Images)

When approached by Fox News Digital, the White House kept the door open for compromise with the Democrats, so long as they don't go too far in their proposals.

"President Trump will continue to enact his commonsense, America First agenda that has delivered the most secure border ever, the largest working-class tax cut in history, the lowest murder rate since 1900, the sharpest drop in prescription drug prices in more than 60 years and a stock market that has hit record-highs," White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales told Fox News Digital.

"As the President said, he is willing to work with anyone who wants to strengthen our economy, lower costs for American families, protect our national security and improve the lives of hardworking Americans. If Democrats choose to pursue radical-left policies, they are choosing to work against the best interests of the American people," she continued.

Even with sizable Democratic majorities in the House and Senate, the party lacks the numbers necessary to override a presidential veto, meaning that liberals will need to work with either congressional Republicans or the White House if they want to enact key portions of their agenda.

"Republicans did not lose seats because of healthcare," Michael Cannon, the Cato Institute’s director of health policy studies, told Fox News Digital when asked about whether the president should compromise with Democrats by reinstating the subsidies. "Republicans did not lose seats because they let the Obamacare subsidies for the wealthy expire, because that's what those were. When we talk about enhanced subsidies, we're talking about subsidies for people making up to $600,000 per year. That's what Democrats put in place. That's what the president rightly let expire. That's not why Republicans lost seats and, so, the president should not be afraid of standing up to the industry."

The massive proposed spending hikes, if they are successful, would take place as the United States’ annual deficit continues to grow, reaching nearly $2 trillion in fiscal 2026, while the government faces down a looming financial disaster as Social Security's retirement trust fund approaches depletion and Congress confronts difficult choices over taxes and benefits.

Simply paying the interest on America’s debt cost the federal government over $1 trillion during the 2026 fiscal year.

Cannon argued that, if the government spends at unsustainable levels over a long enough time period, eventually bond markets will react negatively, reducing the supply of credit and forcing the government to make steep spending cuts, as has happened in Greece and under fiscally irresponsible nations. Some on the left, however, maintain that no such shock would occur as the wealthy can simply be taxed more to reduce the need for borrowing.

Though money is tight in Washington, Democrats have not pared back their spending ambitions.

The crown jewel of the Democratic spending agenda is healthcare, specifically restoring Medicaid funding and Affordable Care Act subsidies.

"During the first 100 days, House Democrats must act to reverse the Medicaid cuts, restore SNAP funding, extend the Affordable Care Act tax credits, eradicate the Trump tariffs and stop the reckless war of choice in Iran," House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries wrote in a manifesto published Nov. 4.

The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the healthcare cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBA) would reduce federal Medicaid spending by about $1.2 trillion between 2026 and 2035 at the cost of cutting enrollment in the program by about 13 million during that roughly 10-year period. As of publication, approximately 65.7 million people are enrolled in Medicaid.

Republicans, especially the president, may be reluctant to work with Democrats to repeal parts of the OBBA, given the sheer amount of political capital they burned to get the bill across the finish line. The extension or expansion of ACA tax credits, however, may be more fertile ground for a bipartisan agreement.

Trump, for instance, said in November 2025 that he would "rather not" extend enhanced ACA tax credits, but conceded that "some kind of extension may be necessary," signaling that he is at least willing to use the subsidies as a bargaining chip during negotiations. Vance, meanwhile, has stated that the subsidies "go to some people deservedly," but argued that they lend themselves to fraud while teasing a healthcare agreement he said would please both parties.

Extending the enhanced ACA subsidies would come with a hefty price tag. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that making the expanded tax credits permanent would add roughly $350 billion to federal deficits between 2026 and 2035, while helping approximately 3.8 million more Americans obtain health insurance in 2035.

The annual impact on the federal deficit would also climb over time, from about $23 billion in 2026 to nearly $44 billion in 2035. And because CBO's estimate only covers a 10-year period, the government would face additional costs beyond that window if the subsidies remained in place.

Some healthcare experts argue that the federal government's spending is only part of the picture. They contend that pouring more money into insurance subsidies without addressing the underlying cost of medical care can also drive up healthcare prices, potentially leaving taxpayers and consumers paying more over time.

"If you want to know why healthcare is so expensive in the United States, why we spend more than any other country, why prices are higher, look at what Democrats are doing right now," Cannon told Fox News Digital. "Government subsidies drive up prices, they drive up healthcare spending ... the most inefficient healthcare sector in the world is using Democrats in particular, but Republicans too, I don't want to be unfair ... to get the government to keep pumping more and more money into that health sector."

Economists generally hold that subsidizing demand for a good by providing subsidies, without increasing the supply of that good, will lead to increased costs across the market. Proponents of expanded government healthcare often counter that any reductions in efficiency or increases to costs, if such things really occur, are worth enduring to ensure coverage is more equitably distributed.

Progressive Democrats led by Reps. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Terri Sewell have pushed Jeffries to expand Medicare to include dental, vision and hearing benefits, set aside funds for medical debt relief and restore funding to the National Institutes of Health — proposals that could cost billions more.

Some Democratic leaders have major spending proposals extending beyond healthcare. Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker, seen by many as a top contender for the 2028 Democratic presidential nomination, released a policy document following Election Night outlining roughly 200 policy proposals that he would like to see from the newly elected Congress.

Among them are restoring government jobs at the Departments of Health and Human Services and Education, increased spending on infrastructure projects, pouring money into housing assistance programs and shoring up food assistance funding, among other priorities.

Jeffries and his congressional allies, meanwhile, also highlighted restoring SNAP funding, providing more government resources to cover childcare costs and subsidizing housing expenses. Some economists have argued that, by subsidizing demand for certain goods without stimulating increased supply, the government can inadvertently raise the cost of goods for everyone.

On the Democratic caucus's left-wing flank, independent Sen. Bernie Sanders argued that Democrats must use their resounding victory to demand a $12,000 payment to each working-class family of four, free tuition at public universities, universal free childcare, making Medicare available to 55-year-olds, increasing Social Security benefits by $2,400 a year and boosting teacher pay. While unlikely to make its way through Congress, Sanders’ agenda would cost trillions.

"The Democratic Socialists want to go much further, with universal healthcare alone, which would cost between $40 trillion and $70 trillion — roughly $300,000 to $500,000 per household," Rachel Greszler, a senior research fellow at Advancing American Freedom, a think tank affiliated with former Vice President Mike Pence, told Fox News Digital. "The entire socialist agenda calls for an estimated $71 trillion to $212 trillion in new spending over the next decade — that’s between $500,000 and $1,600,000 for every household in the US."

Paying for all of this without running up the national debt too much is also on the minds of Democrats, and represents another area where they may find common ground with the GOP.

Sen. Ron Wyden has floated taxing the unrealized gains of some billionaires; Democratic leadership has proposed increasing the corporate income tax; many Democratic senators have argued that additional taxes should be levied against those they deem to be high earners, and some have stated that taxes should be imposed on fast-growing sectors of the economy, such as data centers.

While many of these tax hikes will likely be off the table for Republicans, Trump, Vance and a handful of congressional Republicans have expressed openness to taking more money from highly productive members of society.

Vance, per reports, indicated that he would have been open to a higher tax rate for people earning more than $1 million during internal White House discussions in 2025, though the vice president has publicly expressed skepticism that taxing wealthy Americans alone could solve the country’s fiscal problems. Trump, meanwhile, has said that he would be "OK" with taxing higher earners more to assist lower- and middle-income workers — a proposal that roughly matches the Democratic policy agenda.

Democratic Sens. Chris Van Hollen and Mark Kelly have proposed exactly that through their Working Americans' Tax Cut Act, which would cap the effective tax rate for most Americans while introducing a series of surtaxes on those making over $1 million per year. The University of Pennsylvania, home to one of the best business schools in the world, estimated that the proposal would net the federal government roughly $264 billion over a decade, which is not enough to cover the extension of ACA subsidies.

The University of Pennsylvania's model did not take into account economic feedback effects, meaning that potential lost revenue from reduced investment or work was not captured in its estimate.

Republican Sens. Bernie Moreno and Josh Hawley have backed increased taxes on high earners to pay for Social Security extensions and Child Tax Credit expansion, respectively. Rep. Andy Harris, a Republican from Maryland, has also expressed openness to a higher rate or a new bracket for million-dollar earners.

Democratic Sens. Ron Wyden and Elizabeth Warren, meanwhile, recently introduced a bill that would raise the tax on corporate stock buybacks from 1% to 4%, which analysts at the Tax Foundation and the University of Pennsylvania predict would raise either $242 billion or $300 billion, respectively, over 10 years. Combined with the millionaire tax proposal, it would generate enough revenue to offset extended ACA subsidies but would not come close to generating enough revenue to match the costs of expanding Medicaid.

Wealth tax proposals from the Democrats' left-wing flank, while projected to raise far more in revenue for the government, have proven to be politically toxic and have drawn the ire of economists for potentially crippling economic growth.

A near-term tax hike, however, may not be necessary if all the Democratic talk of healthcare funding is simply political posturing, something Cannon views as likely.

"This is all kabuki here. This is not real health reform that we're talking about. All that Democrats apparently have to offer is more spending, more spending at a time when they know that existing levels of government spending on healthcare are not popular with voters," he said, pointing to the fact that voters failed to approve a billionaire tax in California to fund state healthcare. "Democrats are offering only one idea, and they know that it won't fly, but they're going through the motions because they think they can get a political advantage by saying that Republicans don't care about people."

Lofty Democratic spending ambitions are not limited to the federal government.

With state legislatures and governors’ mansions across the country falling into Democratic hands, public expenditures are likely to boom.

Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, for instance, pledged on the campaign trail to tap $17 billion from the state's rainy day fund to send $1,500 checks to every Texas household, a proposal that would require legislative approval and has since been introduced in the state Senate.

While Republicans may be eager to slam Hinojosa for pulling cash from Texas’ reserves to send payments to voters, they will be forced to grapple with the fact that Trump promised to do the same, but on a much larger scale, if the GOP came out on top during the midterms.
