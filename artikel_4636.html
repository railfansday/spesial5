Direct Your Gratitude to a Person

As someone who is not naturally grateful, I dread hearing these words at Thanksgiving meals:

“Can we go around the table and share what we are thankful for?”

People’s responses to this question generally follow the format, “I am grateful/thankful for __________.” I am grateful for good health. I am thankful for a recent promotion. I am grateful for the breathtaking nature around me. Some people might simply declare, “I am just so thankful,” without citing any reason.

The sentiment captured in these responses is a feeling of gratitude. Maybe you know folks who are bursting with thankfulness and will express feeling grateful at every opportunity. Maybe you are that person.

This kind of gratitude can be described as inward-facing. That is, what is expressed as thankfulness tends to amplify various aspects of the self by naming the things that are going well in one’s life.

Feeling grateful (and trying to increase that emotion) is one way to reap the benefits of gratitude for mental health. But it is not the only way. Outward-facing gratitude, one that is concerned with delivering a message of thanks to a specific target or recipient, should also be a part of how we practice gratitude. In the eagerness to feel gratitude within us, perhaps we have neglected action-oriented gratitude, one that directs thankfulness to a target outside of us. We need to address the imbalance.

As a bilingual person, I find it helpful to reflect on the contrast between Korean and English in the way thankfulness is expressed. In Korea, “thank you” (감사합니다 [gamsahamnida]) is reserved for when there is a clear interpersonal target. More to the point, it is unnatural to say “I feel grateful” in the Korean language; I mean, you could translate it literally, but the average Korean will find the phrasing awkward, as the expression of gratitude in Korean almost always identifies an interpersonal target.

In the individualistic culture that is the U.S., we are prone to elevating feelings, such as the affective component of gratitude. But we need to balance the affective with the behavioral when it comes to how gratitude is understood. We need to look more outside of ourselves to affirm that person who demonstrated unexpected generosity and kindness. And verbal expressions of gratitude to someone need not be dependent on feeling grateful on the inside—if a colleague surprised me with my favorite coffee, hopefully I am moved by their generosity and therefore will say thank you; but even if my heart is not filled with gratitude in that moment for various reasons, I nonetheless have a social responsibility, an interpersonal obligation, to say gamsahamnida to that person.

My research team interviewed individuals with chronic pain and their experiences with gratitude. All of the interviewees said, in effect, that they genuinely struggle with gratitude when the chronic pain and its related adverse consequences become too intense (who can blame them?). At the same time, these individuals clearly described the kind and generous actions of another person as sources of their gratitude. A courageous doctor who tried a new intervention that ended up working. A stranger who came to the rescue in an emergency situation. A supportive partner who heroically took on more responsibilities around the house.

In this holiday season and beyond, by all means, feel grateful. Name all the ways that you are thankful for the blessings in your life. But even while you look toward yourself in search of reasons to feel grateful, also be intentional about looking outward in recognizing the individuals who have shown extra kindness to you, sacrificed for you, or given generously to you.

And with each verbal expression of thanks to another person, with each handwritten or typed thank-you note delivered to another human being, you might find yourself feeling a bit more grateful.
