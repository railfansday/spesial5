4 Types of Dead-End Relationships

Sara and Keith have been married for nine years and have three children. Both work full-time, do their share of chores and parenting, and fill weekends and many evenings with soccer games, ballet lessons, or overflow work. Is it an okay life? Yes, they would agree it is okay, but in the next breath, they'll admit there’s little affection or intimacy between them.

I’ve met hundreds of couples like Sara and Keith whose relationship has been pushed to the back burner, and intimacy has essentially reached a dead end.

Here are four of the most common relationship patterns:

Child- or work-centered. This is Sara and Keith. Essentially, they stopped being a couple and instead became mom and pop or worker bees. Such couples often complain that they wish they had more time for couple time, but they're so busy with work and kids.

Roommates. If they don’t have the demands or distractions of work or children, many couples downshift to roommate status. They do spend time together, which they usually enjoy; they may even describe the other as their best friend, but the affection has long since dried up. And like roommates, there’s plenty of talk about schedules and chores.

Parallel lives. This takes the roommate scenario to another level. No best-friend status; no doing things together; each does their own thing; they essentially just share the same space.

Habit/routine-driven lifestyle. This can bleed into all the above variations, where, like Sara and Keith, they do what they do because they do it. They run on autopilot most of the time, and habits and routines kill any hope of spontaneity or anything different.

What holds all these types of couples together is that they're defined by bad solutions to other problems lying beneath. Here are some of the common sources:

Problems that are swept under the rug. If problems go unresolved, they stack up and act like a wedge pushing you apart. You create a long list of "things we don't talk about" and fill in the space with the demands and distractions of work and kids—or you devolve into parallel lives.

A power struggle. Here, problems are never solved because the individuals are unwilling to reach win-win compromises. Sometimes this attitude is fueled by anger and resentment from old wounds; sometimes it is simply a matter of personality—perhaps high anxiety they try to manage through control, self-centeredness, or a need for power. Neither is willing to bend because it feels like letting the other guy win, and coming out on top is always the priority.

Conflict avoidance. Some individuals have learned to fear conflict and others' strong emotions, often because of a childhood environment in which parents were frightening or modeled passivity and avoidance. As a result, anger is internalized or turned into self-blame, and problems are never discussed or resolved. Periodically, one person may act out or explode in anger, which only further fuels their fear and internalization.

Unhealed wounds. Past affairs and sudden, traumatic decisions, such as abruptly quitting jobs or being forced to move—painful incidents that shattered safety and trust and linger, coloring the relationship—have become a grudge that is always there; an open wound that never heals.

An expired relationship contract. Whatever Sara and Keith needed from each other nine years ago, they likely no longer need it now. Each has grown into different needs and desires, but like many couples, they’ve built a life in a box of rules and routines that no longer fit them. Some couples divorce; others, like them, downshift their relationship.

If you’re ready for a change—to get your relationship back on the front burner and bring back intimacy and affection—here are some tips to get you started:

Acknowledge the state of the relationship. In some ways, this can be the hardest step. Like "The Emperor's New Clothes," it requires breaking the code of silence, refusing to ignore reality, and having the courage to be honest. Once you say how you’ve been feeling and start that conversation, you can’t unsay it or the other can’t unhear it. You’ve opened the door to change, and that’s a good place to start.

Change the emotional climate through action. Resist the urge to think change is about getting the other person to change: this leads to nagging, manipulation, or a blink contest where each waits for the other. Instead, think about changing the overall emotional climate of the relationship, breaking ingrained habits and patterns, and stopping autopilot behaviors. The key is action: Plan a date night; snuggle together on the couch while you watch a movie; move the kid’s bedtime earlier so you have time as a couple to simply chat at the end of the day. Something, anything that is different and positive.

Repair old wounds. I’ve heard partners say they just need answers to questions that have been lingering for years—about the affair, the job, or the move. Or sometimes it is just the opportunity to get things off their chests and finally feel heard and understood, without avoidance or defensiveness. Sometimes you can do this on your own; sometimes it helps to start by writing a letter or email; sometimes you need to bring in a professional to help mediate and moderate the conversation.

Tackle unresolved problems. The best way to do this is to chip away at them in baby steps. I suggest that couples set aside a good time to talk when they’re not tired or distracted. Treat it like a staff meeting at work: Set an agenda with one or two items, focus on you, and work toward a win-win compromise while controlling your emotions. Meet for 30-45 minutes. And if it is hard to do, bring in a professional.

Resist the urge to think your feelings need to change before you can act; if you keep doing what you’re doing, you’ll keep feeling the same way. Instead, challenge yourself to break out and take action.
