All the ‘GTA’ Games Ever, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Unless you’ve been locked in a bank vault somewhere for the last 30 years, there’s a good chance that you’ve heard of Grand Theft Auto. Originally launched for PC and PlayStation in 1997, the series began as a solid commercial success for fans of edgy humor and shooters, before becoming a pop culture phenomenon in the early 2000s. Today, it’s one of the most recognizable entertainment properties on Earth, transcending the boundaries of just gaming — with its latest iteration, GTA VI, arriving this year with the highest grossing media launch ever. Day one sales of GTA VI generated over $3.79 billion, more than any other game or movie, amounting to the GDP of a small nation.

But more so than the money, GTA’s influence on gaming and culture at large is its true legacy. The open-world crime saga has informed the way video games are developed; its cinematic tone nailed an old Hollywood flavor that earlier games could only vaguely emulate; the freedom of choice in how to play inspired imitators of all kinds, from kid-friendly fare to direct copycats chasing the violent gangster gameplay.

Outside of the industry sphere, GTA became a touchstone for gaming as a concept. Think of any movie or show where characters are playing video games; it’s very likely the visual depiction will be a crudely rendered clone of GTA. A target of endless pearlclutching, politicians and advocates on both sides of the aisle have paraded the name as a leading cause of societal decay. To some, it’s a hedonistic simulacrum of sex and violence, encouraging players to indulge their darkest impulses.

And, sure, it’s a series where you can assault sex workers and commit mass shootings, but those options bely the point of Grand Theft Auto as a whole. At their best, the games serve as an intricately written parody of American culture — the greed, hypocrisy, and often downright idiocy that the country wears so proudly. In GTA, the American Dream is something to be gripped by force, and is delivered to audiences through caricature. It takes all the stories, media, and norms we love and reflects them back as satire. GTA doesn’t glorify crime, people do. It’s just laughing at us along the way.

To that end, not every GTA is so successful. The early top-down entries served more as arcade-style run-and-gunners that did little to admonish players to think beyond wanton destruction. But as technology evolved and storytelling devices matured, GTA began to better embody its role as Mean Streets meets South Park, telling exaggerated tales of criminality gone wrong to make audiences flinch — at least momentarily, before plotting their next heist.

In honor of GTA VI’s long-awaited release, Rolling Stone is ranking every Grand Theft Auto, from their humble Nineties-era origins to the modern blockbusters, both main games and expansions. Here’s every one, from worst to best.

Although the first two games in the series made appearances on the Game Boy Color, Grand Theft Auto Advance was the first original entry specifically developed for a Nintendo device. Blending the top-down overhead perspective of the OG installments with the mission structure of then-ubiquitous 3D versions, GTA Advance bridges the series’ oft-forgotten older design with more accessible gameplay.

As a prequel to 2001’s GTA III, players control a low-rent criminal named Mike whose dream of leaving Liberty City behind is blown to shreds by the mafia. Storywise, the plot comes through mostly via text and isn’t much to cling to. The handheld hardware of the Game Boy Advance doesn’t allow for much graphical fidelity, resulting in a game that looks worse than even the first PC entry, but is still commendable as a pocket-friendly way to play GTA.

To capitalize on the success of the original game, two expansions were green-lit as interim teasers before 1999’s full-fledged sequel. Grand Theft Auto: London 1969 released earlier that year as a standalone purchase, with 1961 following suit as a free download. Neither entry was groundbreaking, mostly recycling the gameplay and visuals of the base game with a Swingin’ Sixties finish. The duology is mostly fascinating for being the only GTA games set in a real city rather than fictional versions of American locales. The setting did result in one major innovation, however, with licensed music introduced to the franchise for the in-game radio stations — a feature that would become an essential part of GTA’s appeal moving forward.

Looking back, it’s hard to imagine how a game as simple as the original Grand Theft Auto kickstarted a media dynasty. Developed by Scottish studio DMA Design (now Rockstar North), the initial concept began as a simple cops and robbers sim called Race ‘n’ Chase, before being overhauled to focus on the more open-ended crime-oriented mission structure. Players choose from an assortment of unnamed protagonists to control, and must complete six linear levels spread across three cities (Liberty City, San Andreas, and Vice City), accruing a set number of points to progress.

Although seemingly basic in its top-down design, with a world composed mostly of tiny dot-like people and a handful of vehicle options, the first GTA built the foundation for everything else to come. Its six levels each serve as small sandboxes where players can choose to score cash as they see fit, and each city would later be fleshed out as fully realized urban sprawls in the 3D games. Like any of the series’ main characters, this freshman outing represents a humble start with great aspirations.

While the first batch of Game Boy Color and Advance releases proved that the series’ formula could be replicated for handheld with a few concessions, Liberty City Stories took things a step further — bringing a fully 3D GTA to the PlayStation Portable. Like GTA Advance, this all-new entry serves as a prequel to GTA III, albeit much more faithfully rendered, with graphics matching the home console version and even fully recorded voice acting for its cinematic cut scenes. In fact, it’s superior in some ways, incorporating gameplay features like changeable clothing and more intuitive camera controls. Once ported to the PlayStation 2, the game’s cracks began to show slightly; it’s a mostly inessential side quest for those who want more GTA III. But, in its initial portable incarnation, Liberty City Stories is a technical marvel.

The second spinoff developed for PlayStation Portable, Vice City Stories is yet another shining example of the impressive capabilities of Sony’s first foray into the handheld market. A prequel to 2002’s Vice City, it carries over the Eighties setting and includes an impressive voice cast for what’s essentially a mobile game, including Gary Busey, Miami Vice star Philip Michael Thomas, and even Phil Collins as himself.

Unlike its PSP predecessor, Vice City Stories does more than just replicate the console experience on-the-go, introducing some very ambitious new mechanics to the experience. The most prominent is the concept of empire building, where players must topple local local gang-affiliated businesses, then usurp control to create a network of private income sources, from brothels to protection rackets. Also like the previous game, Vice City Stories features a six-person multiplayer mode, although that feature was removed for the later PlayStation 2 rerelease.

While GTA as a series frequently pivots between settings and perspectives, Chinatown Wars marks some of the biggest departures for the franchise’s look and gameplay overall. The handheld game blends the top-down view of the early titles with a new cel-shaded aesthetic with thick character and environmental outlines reminiscent of a comic book. Initially designed for the dual-screen Nintendo DS, its gameplay features numerous touchscreen-based minigames — although both these controls and the stylized visuals were swapped out for the later PSP release.

But the most notable change in Chinatown Wars is a greater emphasis on drug-dealing, something that was controversial at release. As a Triad member, players can sell heroin, ecstasy, weed, cocaine and more — as well as strategize around different market conditions and geographical supply. It’s a murky system that goes deeper than most games in the series, which mostly ask the user to smuggle narcotics rather than peddle them directly.

At launch, GTA 2 was met with a mixed reception, mostly for failing to meaningfully innovate beyond the first game’s premise and design. But that’s not entirely true. The 1997 original did well to introduce the basics of open-ended mission structure and player choice, but its sequel perfected the formula for the pseudo-2D era.

Set in a fictional retro-futuristic city called Anywhere, U.S.A., GTA 2 sees players once again controlling a itty bitty protagonist as they conduct crime in a sequence of missions to progress through each level. This time, the setting is one large city divided by districts, and features rival gangs to work for, each at odds with each other. Balancing favor between the syndicates is key, and an increased level of environmental interactivity can lead to gang members engaging in their own immersive firefights with the police and each other. GTA 2 isn’t quite the quantum leap forward that its successor would be, but it’s a delightful piece of retro gaming nostalgia whose DNA can be seen in later indie games like Hotline Miami (2012).

Before the turn of the millennium, the GTA series had been a modest success, selling millions of units on PC and PlayStation, but it hadn’t quite hit the zeitgeist. GTA III would be the beginning of the dominant cultural force we know today. Rebooting the series’ narrative into a new continuity moving forward, it once again drops players into the New York-inspired setting of Liberty City — now rendered in full 3D — for an exponentially more cinematic experience.

For gamers, GTA III is a foundational text, popularizing many design choices and mechanics today’s player take for granted. Its urban open-world feels lived in, peppered with cars and pedestrians, each on their own loop that can be upended. Combat and gunplay, while simplistic by modern standards, are deeper than ever before thanks to the 3D leap; the ability to simultaneously drive a car and aim over-the-shoulder for a drive-by was astounding at the time. Most importantly, the first entry of this new era allowed Rockstar to fully realize the cinematic scope of what Grand Theft Auto always wanted to be. With elaborate cut scenes and voice acting from stars Frank Vincent, Debi Mazar, and Kyle MacLachlan, GTA III pioneered the blurring of lines between games and cinema. It’s basically gaming’s Mean Streets, a New Hollywood crime caper from which generations of developers would borrow from moving forward.

Much like the original game, 2008’s GTA IV received a duology of expansions that built on the base game’s foundation. But unlike the London content, The Lost and the Damned and The Ballad of Gay Tony substantially add to the narrative and mechanics of their progenitor beyond being simple reskins.

Each expansion follows characters whose stories overlap with the main plot of GTA IV: biker Johnny Klebitz in the TLATD and bodyguard Luis Fernando Lopez in TBOGT. While both play similarly, each has distinct features beyond the base game. Johnny’s motorcycle club plays a huge role in his content, allowing players to call in the gang for backup when things get hairy. Luis’ gameplay isn’t notably different, but the story in his expansion is one of the very best in the series, celebrated at the time for its depiction of LGBTQ characters.

There was a time when game development was a much speedier process, with studios rapidly churning out quality sequels near annually. Rockstar themselves had a generational run from 2001 to 2004, dominating the conversation with the trifecta of GTA III, Vice City, and San Andreas. Even more impressive is how each game substantially improved on its predecessor despite wildly shifting apart in setting and tone.

Vice City falls squarely in the middle of the pack, transposing GTA III’s groundbreaking design into a Miami Vice and Scarface-inspired Eighties crime caper, complete with a lead character voiced by actor Ray Liotta. Control-wise, it’s not a huge leap forward; players still roam an open world hopping between main missions, side quests, and mini-games. What elevates Vice City is its pitch perfect take on Eighties Miami, depicting a greasy city soaked in neon, all set to one of the greatest video game soundtracks of all time.

Until very recently, GTA V was the series’ most ambitious entry to date. For many, it’s the definitive Grand Theft Auto experience — if only by default. The sole franchise entry in 13 years, GTA V was released for PlayStation 3, 4, and 5, selling a ludicrous 230 million copies over the course of its elongated lifespan.

In many ways, GTA V plays like a greatest hits of the series’ many ideas and mechanics, while modernizing its gameplay just enough to feel like an improvement over IV. Set in the fictional state of San Andreas (although set in a different “universe” than the 2004 title of that name), the story follows three unique protagonists, all with conflicting personalities and motives. Players will bounce between bank robber Michael De Santa, gangster Franklin Clinton, and psychopathic gunrunner Trevor Philips; their interwoven narrative bringing a slightly meandering Tarantino vibe to the otherwise kinetic plot, heavily inspired by Michael Mann’s Heat. Fans would be right to argue that GTA V deserves the top spot based on popularity alone, but looking back on the actual gameplay, it did little to substantially innovate over its direct predecessor. In 2013, GTA V’s gameplay arrived feeling dated, but was bolstered significantly by its feeling of limitless freedom and well-executed campaign story.

Although the series has many flavors, there’s a semblance of homogeny among the distinct crime syndicates throughout GTA. Regardless of the time period, there’s usually a depiction of the Italian mafia, Chinese Triads, maybe some Russians — with some depiction of Cuban and Haitian gangs cropping up in Vice City. 2004’s San Andreas broke served as a massive departure, centering on SoCal gang violence inspired by the Bloods and Crips.

While so much of the franchise’s inspiration seemingly comes from TV and movies (what better societal reflection, after all), San Andreas feels more informed by actual history, weaving in elements of real-life urban crises like the Nineties crack epidemic and the 1992 L.A. riots alongside storytelling lifted from films like John Singleton’s Boyz n the Hood. Outside of its unique setting and more impactful storytelling, one of San Andreas’ greatest contributions is an increased emphasis on the slice-of-life details and mini-games that shape the player character directly — something that is heavily prevalent in this year’s new entry, GTA VI.

Following a legendary run from 2001 to 2006, wherein five 3D GTA releases dominated home consoles and handhelds alike, the expectations for the next numbered entry in the franchise hit a fever pitch in 2008. Fortunately, GTA IV delivered the goods.

Resetting the table once again with a new vision of Liberty City, GTA IV traded in the sprawling map of San Andreas for a more densely packed and lively version of the series’ original locale. The story also eschewed any direct cinematic parallels to focus on a more original story leaning deeper into the satirical commentary of U.S. culture — specifically the American Dream. Unlike the thuggish mafioso types of games prior, GTA IV’s lead, Niko Bellic, is an ex-soldier and Eastern European immigrant, arriving in the states equally interested in revenge and a fresh start in the land of opportunity. Bringing in an outsider lends the game’s tone a sharper edge; this America is an alien place filled with unserious people.

Although its 2013 follow-up would offer better graphics and more features, GTA IV is essentially a perfected vision of what the 1997 original aimed to be, and stands less on the shoulders of other media to define its voice. This is the game that gave us Grand Theft Auto as we know it today.

For all its many elements, the defining feature of Grand Theft Auto as an idea is the emphasis on player freedom. Go anywhere, do whatever you want — that’s the ultimate promise. Except, every GTA is bound by the needs of a linear narrative. There’s a story to tell, players are just a part of it. But what if the story was entirely your own?

GTA Online launched as a 30-player online sandbox that emulated the mission structure of the base game while opening the door to infinite chaos. Rather than chasing down CPU-scripted non-playable characters, users are suddenly even squared against other humans who can react strategically (or erratically), turning the meta-gameplay of GTA to a new level without the constraints of a campaign story. But storytelling is still key, and following the game’s PC launch in 2015, modders cracked open GTA Online’s world with limitless potential. Now, it’s a platform where online roleplay reigns supreme, a second-life simulator where people go about their daily routines under fictional personas.

On streaming platforms, content creators have turned GTA RP into everything from reality TV to serial killer soap operas. It’s transcended individual gameplay to become its own ever-present world of entertainment to control or passively watch. While GTA V has kept selling throughout console generations, it’s clear that GTA Online has been the lifeblood of the series in its interim time between installments, and racked up billions in revenue that’s funded the series’ next big phase.

There are certain moments in time when a game changes everything. Pong had families enraptured around their cathode ray TVs; Tetris locked people into their screens on-the-go decades before smart phones became the norm. As a series, Grand Theft Auto’s had its fair share of these moments. GTA III stunned gamers with its open world, and V made it feel like it was possible live within a movie.

GTA VI might be the most incredible feat that Rockstar’s ever executed. From its opening moments, it’s instantly clear that there’s some insane technical alchemy going on. Its characters and environments are near photorealistic, and its world brimming with life. You can touch or do just about anything. In fact, players will spend their first few hours simply making coffee and painting their house.

But beyond the flashy graphics and complex coding of NPC and environmental scripts, the most immediate difference in GTA VI is how mature it feels — and not in the adults-only content kind of way. Its dual protagonists, Jason and Lucia, aren’t jade caricatures of criminals pulled from pop media, but instead richly humanized characters with hopes and dreams. Although the game has all the hallmarks of a Grand Theft Auto underworld story, every written beat and player action serves to inform how the relationship between its leads blossoms. Arriving in 2026 to an American culture that’s well beyond parody, Rockstar’s decision to ground its story in the perspective of genuine people elevates it beyond anything the franchise was ever intended to be. GTA VI perfectly encompasses all that excess that Grand Theft Auto should have, while adding in a soul we never knew it so desperately needed.
