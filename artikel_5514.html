10 sectors where 2026 tech layoffs landed, from Oracle to Nike

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Tech has logged more than 200,000 layoffs in 2026. Here are 10 sectors, from cloud software to streaming, where cuts landed.

Picture opening your inbox before sunrise to learn that today is your last day at the company. Across the technology business in 2026, thousands of people have lived some version of that morning, and the pink slips have reached beyond the engineers who write code. Recruiters, lawyers, product managers, and human resources staff have joined the casualties. Many reports place the U.S. at the center of the downsizing. Anyone who works in tech, hires tech talent, or relies on a company that does has reason to watch where the next round falls.

Spending priorities explain much of the upheaval. Several companies have tied job reductions to heavier AI investment, and payroll has become the place where they find the money. Pushback has come from government and from workers. California Gov. Gavin Newsom unveiled a tool in June to follow how AI affects the labor force, pitching it as a way to ready the state through governance and policy. More than 4,500 Google $GOOGL staffers signed a petition seeking protections, including buyouts ahead of any mandatory layoffs and guaranteed severance. The calendar adds urgency. TrueUp counts more than 200,000 tech layoffs in 2026 with a couple of months to go, against more than 245,000 across all of 2025. By TrueUp's reading, this year's tally appears to be outpacing last year's.

Yahoo Tech maintains a rolling tracker of 2026 tech job losses, logging them company by company and month by month from March through its Nov. 4 update. Writers Katie Teague and Valentina Palladino assemble each entry from company statements, executive memos, social media posts, and outside news reports. Many entries describe announced plans or moves that other outlets reported, and several companies did not disclose how many people they let go. The tracker does not total or rank industries. This article sorts its company entries into 10 sectors, an editorial selection running from enterprise software to telecom and consumer brands. The order makes no claim about which sector shed the most jobs.

Oracle $ORCL shed 21,000 jobs over the past year, about 13% of its workforce, according to a June 23 filing. Another round followed in September, and the company never disclosed how many people it hit. Recipients reportedly opened early-morning emails on Monday, Sept. 14, telling them the day would be their last. Reports tie the September round to Oracle's effort to offset the money pouring into AI chips and data centers.

Intuit $INTU plans to lay off 3,000 employees, about 17% of its headcount. CEO Sasan Goodarzi wrote in a memo that the reductions would help the company concentrate on weaving AI throughout its services. Atlassian $TEAM dismissed some 1,600 people on March 16, around 10% of its workers. CEO Mike Cannon-Brookes told the team the cuts would let Atlassian pay for more investment in AI and enterprise sales out of its own savings while shoring up its finances. His framing casts payroll savings as the bankroll for AI, the clearest statement of the trade in this group.

Cloudflare is eliminating more than 1,100 positions in a restructuring tied to AI. Rackspace, a cloud computing services provider, let go of 750 workers, around 15% of its employees, as it pivoted toward AI. Cisco $CSCO is cutting just under 4,000 jobs, including 471 in the Bay Area.

Monday.com is dropping 20% of its people, about 630 in all, as it works to make its AI platform a core offering. Earlier in the year, the company built its main product around the idea that business customers want their teams working alongside AI agents. ClickUp CEO Zeb Evans announced a 22% cut as the company leans on AI to raise productivity. Evans insisted the move "wasn't about cutting costs" and pledged the savings would flow "directly back into the people who stay." ClickUp introduced million-dollar salary bands for employees who "create outsized impact using AI."

Salesforce $CRM axed at least 86 jobs in California. Workday $WDAY is laying off around 140 Bay Area employees to slim its tech team, part of a broader plan to pare its worldwide ranks by 2.5%. Seven of the 10 companies here tied their job losses to AI, through executive statements or press reports. In this corner of tech, AI spending competes with salaries for the same dollars.

Canva is eliminating 22% of its staff, just over 1,200 employees, as the design software company merges its product, growth, and platform teams. Reports place the overhaul in November and describe a push to reduce "subspecialisation," the habit of carving work into narrow roles. Fewer niche jobs could leave the employees who stay with a wider spread of duties.

Wix set a parallel course in May. Cofounder and CEO Avishai Abrahami announced on X $TWTR that the website builder would trim around 20% of its workforce, a reduction that should reach about 1,000 roles based on headcount. Abrahami pointed to the changes AI has set in motion and said the company needs to "become a faster, leaner, and flatter organization." He pinned part of the pressure on the exchange rate between the Israeli shekel and the U.S. dollar. Wix has its headquarters in Israel, which explains why the currency entered his reasoning.

Canva and Wix are each shedding about a fifth of their people, and both describe the goal in structural terms. Canva wants to thin out narrow specialties, while Wix wants a shallower hierarchy. Each plan redraws how the remaining staff split their work.

Webflow's reduction comes with no public headcount, though one employee called the round "a bloodbath." The word conveys how severe the layoffs felt to at least one person inside the company. A move toward AI appears to sit behind the cuts. CEO Linda Tong has said AI is rewriting how marketing teams build, test, and fine-tune digital experiences, which is the work Webflow's tools exist to support. Among the four companies here, Webflow's account alone comes from a rank-and-file worker.

Airtable let go of an undisclosed number of workers under its owner, Bending Spoons, the company that acquired Vimeo. Federico Simionato, a product leader at Bending Spoons, laid out the thinking on X as a "deep belief that small teams ship more and better." His wording lines up with Abrahami's call for a leaner company and with Canva's merger of separate teams.

Wix and Webflow both point to AI in this group, and Wix's CEO paired it with a currency squeeze. Canva and Airtable explain their cuts through team structure alone, with no mention of the technology. Half of these companies tie their layoffs to AI, and the other half treat compact teams as the goal in itself.

Epic Games announced on March 24 that it was laying off more than 1,000 workers, and CEO Tim Sweeney traced the move to a "downturn in Fortnite engagement" for its free-to-play shooter. His note to staff framed the problem as plain arithmetic. Epic was "spending significantly more than we're making," Sweeney wrote, and needed major reductions to keep the company funded. He added that AI played no part in the decision. One fading hit can destabilize a payroll built around it.

Electronic Arts $EA plans to eliminate 400 jobs, about 3% of its workforce, and the move includes closing BioWare, the studio behind the Mass Effect games. Shuttering BioWare will also cancel the next Mass Effect title. Earlier in the year, EA told investors it would rein in research and development spending as part of a push to save upwards of $700 million. A modest headcount trim can carry a franchise's next chapter out the door.

Xbox is shedding 3,200 roles through fiscal year 2027, Xbox chief Asha Sharma wrote in a post on X $TWTR. The first 1,600 people left on July 6, and the company said at the time that another 1,600 terminations would follow. In September, Xbox announced 268 more eliminations across Halo Studios, other first-party studios, and additional corners of Xbox Game Studios, all under a "reset" unveiled over the summer. The July and September rounds account for 1,868 roles, which leaves 1,332 of Sharma's total still to come. Soon after the July departures, Sharma joined a U.S. Federal Reserve task force advising on "productivity and jobs."

Sony $SONY Interactive Entertainment said in June that it is removing "a significant number of employees" from Bungie, but it gave no count. The losses reach most of the Destiny team, some members of the Marathon $MPC team, and SIE groups that support Bungie. Stripping out most of Destiny's developers leaves open how the series continues.

Ubisoft dismissed 105 employees at Red Storm Entertainment to reduce costs. Development has ended at the studio, which makes the downsizing permanent. Red Storm employed 180 people in 2022 and has weathered several earlier waves of job losses. The latest round alone equals more than half of that 2022 headcount.

Eidos-Montréal announced on March 30 that it would let go of 124 people, and studio head David Anfossi chose to depart. The studio's LinkedIn post pointed to "changing project needs" across production and support teams and said talent and performance played no role.

None of the six game companies in the tracker names AI as the trigger. The reasons they do give run to player engagement, spending, shifting projects, and a corporate reset, which ties the industry's losses to its own business pressures.

Meta $META began removing 10% of its workforce in May, about 8,000 employees, and it plans to shut 6,000 open roles to free up money for AI. The planned vacancy closures equal three-quarters of the dismissals. Meta is shrinking the payroll it carries today and the one it once meant to build.

Smaller rounds at Meta preceded the big one. The company cut about 700 people starting March 25 across Reality Labs, social media, and recruiting teams, according to reports. Reality Labs operates Horizon Worlds for the Quest VR headset, and Meta is stepping back from metaverse creation. Meta also reportedly set the end of May as the exit date for roughly 200 Bay Area workers, 124 in Burlingame and 74 in Sunnyvale. Trimming recruiters fits the move to close open positions. Both steps point to slower hiring ahead.

Microsoft $MSFT's July round hit 4,800 jobs, around 2.1% of its staff, with most of the losses in its gaming division. Buyouts form the other half of its approach. In April, the company extended its first-ever buyout to as many as 7% of employees. The one-time retirement program covered workers at the senior director level and below whose age and years of employment add up to 70 or more. A later reported offer reached 60,000 employees. Buyouts let the company lower headcount while leaving each worker to decide whether to go.

Amazon $AMZN's reductions arrived in scattered pieces. Reports put white-collar losses in its stores business at fewer than 1,000, with about 250 more in devices and an undisclosed number in its AGI group. A warehouse in Homestead, Florida, shed more than 600 positions. Amazon also eliminated roughly 30,000 people in rounds spanning October and January. The entries stretch from retail and devices to AI research and a warehouse floor, reaching corporate offices and frontline operations alike.

Apple $AAPL laid off at least 60 people in August in its Vision Group, the team behind the Vision Pro headset, and estimates put the companywide total above 200. The Intelligent Systems Experience group, which handles some AI features on Apple devices, also lost employees, reportedly because of architecture changes for the company's AI-infused Siri. An undisclosed number of Fitness+ workers departed too. The Vision Pro launched in 2024 at a starting price of $3,699, and incoming CEO John Ternus would reportedly put the category "on ice." Whether Apple exits AR and VR for good remains an open question.

Headset and metaverse teams surface in the rounds at both Meta and Apple, a sign that the two companies are cooling on mixed reality in the same year.

Visa $V is cutting about 2,600 jobs, around 7% of its workforce, and technology and product teams bear most of the losses. Efficiency drives part of the effort. In a memo, CEO Ryan McInerney wrote that Visa must "continue evolving how we work" and that "AI is also helping to accelerate this evolution and shape the way work gets done at Visa." Reports indicate that AI was one factor among several.

The card network intends to reinvest in consumer payments and value-added services such as stablecoin, according to reports. Visa is trimming technical staff while steering money toward newer payment products. The move shifts resources inside the company as much as it lowers expenses.

Coinbase is laying off 700 employees, about 14% of its headcount. CEO Brian Armstrong pointed to two pressures: the current market and AI's effect on how the company works. Coinbase stands as the lone firm in this group to name market conditions, which ties its downsizing to outside forces as well as internal strategy.

Visa's plan removes almost four times as many people as Coinbase's, yet Coinbase's share runs twice as high. The percentages put Coinbase near 5,000 people and Visa at about 37,000. At Coinbase, the cut reaches about one in seven workers.

Robinhood plans to trim its ranks by 10%, around 290 positions, as part of a restructuring it announced through the Robinhood Comms account on X $TWTR. The announcement leaves open whether AI shaped the decision. CEO Vlad Tenev said the company will turn to frontier technologies to drive its execution further. His phrasing points toward new tools without naming any.

The three companies acted in consecutive months, with Coinbase in May, Robinhood in June, and Visa in July. AI runs through the explanations from Visa and Coinbase, yet each executive paired the technology with a second motive, efficiency at Visa and the market at Coinbase. Payments companies appear to fold AI into cost calculations that predate it.

Uber $UBER announced 3,300 job cuts, about 10% of its workforce, and pointed the steepest reductions at the distance between employees and the top of the company. The plan trims staff sitting seven or more layers below the CEO by 20% and shrinks micro-teams by close to 50%. Together, the two targets describe an org chart losing its middle rungs and its smallest units at once. CEO Dara Khosrowshahi's memo on the changes made no mention of AI. Uber also reportedly cut 10% of its customer service staff in July.

Etsy followed a similar path with about 220 layoffs, 12% of its employees, to simplify its setup. Product and engineering teams took most of the losses. CEO Kruti Patel Goyal said the changes would help build "the organization we believe Etsy needs for the future." A company spokesperson said AI adoption did not drive the decision. At 12%, Etsy's reduction ran two percentage points deeper than Uber's.

Patreon let go of 93 people, 20% of its headcount, and tied the move to "changing our organizational structure and how we work." Its email to creators and team members stated that the company was "not making the above changes because we believe AI replaces humans." The same message conceded that AI has reshaped the tech industry and Patreon's own working methods. The email held both positions at once, crediting AI with changing daily work while denying it any role in who lost a job.

Groupon planned the deepest proportional downsizing among these companies, roughly 25% of its people or 400 positions, with the cuts expected by the end of the third quarter. The company set out to rebuild itself as an AI-native business. Of the six employers here, Groupon stands alone in making AI the stated purpose of its overhaul.

eBay is trimming 9% of its workforce, following a February round that eliminated about 800 positions. A company spokesperson described the move as a way to line up eBay's teams and resources behind its top strategic priorities. Zillow is laying off just over 500 people, and its CEO pointed to "a disciplined cost structure and getting more efficient" as the goal.

All six of these companies gave reasons for their cuts, and several, including Uber, Etsy, Patreon, and eBay, pinned them on how they arrange their teams while leaving AI aside. Across consumer marketplaces, reshaping the org chart drove more of the downsizing than automation did.

Netflix $NFLX plans to shed about 1,000 people, around 6% of its global workforce, in its largest round of job losses since 2022. Product, tech, legal, and human-resources teams will carry the bulk of the reduction, according to reports. The streamer has struggled to raise customer engagement and broader growth, and the downsizing lands amid that struggle. Legal and human-resources staff work far from the screen, so the reductions reach past the engineers who build the service.

Disney $DIS started its retrenchment in April, when Josh D'Amaro announced 1,000 layoffs two months after becoming CEO. Marvel followed by releasing 8% of its employees. In his memo, D'Amaro described the cuts as part of a search for ways to streamline operations across parts of the company. He argued that the fast pace of change in Disney's industries forces it to keep reassessing how to build a nimbler workforce that leans more heavily on technology.

Disney's next move could reach thousands of jobs, according to November reports describing a plan to lower annual costs by 20%. The company has not confirmed the plan. People familiar with the matter say the savings could pair staff departures with other spending trims. If Disney carries out the plan, the April layoffs would mark the first stage of a broader contraction.

NPR offered voluntary buyouts to 300 newsroom employees after losing federal funding, yet it will accept 30 of them, one in 10 of those offered. The news organization plans targeted dismissals if volunteers fall short of the target. Its approach resembles the buyouts Microsoft $MSFT extended this year. How many staffers step forward will decide whether NPR moves to forced exits.

Vimeo parted with more than 120 people, about 25% of its city staff, in its third wave of exits since Bending Spoons acquired it. Bending Spoons also owns Airtable, so one parent has pared headcount at two separate tech brands this year. Three waves under the same owner show the shrinking did not stop once the purchase closed.

LinkedIn plans to drop about 875 roles, or 5% of its personnel, to reorganize teams and steer attention toward growing areas, an anonymous person told reporters. A source said the move was "not for artificial intelligence to replace jobs at LinkedIn."

None of the five employers here named AI as the main reason for shrinking, and LinkedIn's source ruled it out. The reasons on record at Netflix, Disney, NPR, and LinkedIn run to engagement and growth struggles, cost targets, lost public money, and team reorganizations. Across streaming and media, audience and funding pressures account for the losses more than automation does.

General Motors $GM is letting go of around 500 to 600 employees in information technology, with reports describing the goal as a way to "trim costs and clear the way to bring in staff with skills in other technology areas." The automaker's rationale pairs savings with a change in expertise. GM wants room on its payroll for people who know different technical fields. The cutback shrinks the current IT group while it opens seats for new hires, making the move a swap of skills as much as a cut in headcount.

Redwood Materials, a battery recycling company, laid off 10% of its employees, or 135 people, in April, a month before the GM news broke. The two figures imply a staff of about 1,350 before the cuts. Redwood's chief HR officer wrote in an email that the company acted "to sharpen our focus, our work and the size of our teams to support the direction Redwood is going in the future." The message names no new direction and sets no savings target. It puts its weight on team size and focus, casting the layoffs as a reset of priorities.

Lucid $LCID, an EV automaker, belonged to a June wave of job losses that reached 14,000 positions across companies such as game developer Bungie and trading platform Robinhood. Lucid tied its own downsizing to an effort to "boost profitability amid growing competition." The phrase points to pressure on earnings, a different motive from GM's skills swap or Redwood's tighter priorities. No count of Lucid's departures accompanies the report. Lucid's slice of the June total remains unknown.

Each explanation in this group turns on cost, profit, or team size, and none of the three mentions AI. The timing runs in sequence, with Redwood in April, GM in May, and Lucid in June. Vehicle and battery companies kept shedding staff across three straight months without citing the AI spending that pushed cuts at several other firms.

Samsung is eliminating 179 jobs as it moves its New Jersey headquarters to Texas later this year, and estimates put the number of affected workers at around 1,000. The gap between those two figures shows how an office move spreads beyond the people it puts out of work. Many more employees face disruption than face dismissal. The plan surfaced in July, as Samsung joined the companies that cut staff after Microsoft $MSFT's round that month.

Reuters put the New Jersey count at 739 roles affected in some way. A portion of those employees received relocation offers, and others lost their jobs. Another 100 roles disappeared in Texas, most of them in the mobile division. The destination office, in other words, shed people of its own. The move falls short of a clean transfer of headcount from one state to another. The three counts track different groups, and no single total ties them together.

Intel $INTC is shrinking its Data Center Group and has disclosed no tally of the positions involved. The company described the overhaul as a way to become more focused and efficient, and it said it remained committed to its product roadmap. The roadmap pledge signals that Intel intends to keep its product plans on course while the group gets smaller. Without a number, outsiders cannot gauge how deep the unit's cuts run.

The Wall Street Journal reported in April that GoPro, the wearable camera maker, will lay off 145 employees this year under a cost-cutting restructuring plan. The reductions continue through the end of the year, which spreads the job losses across months instead of a single day. Among the three firms, GoPro states the plainest motive. Its plan aims to lower expenses.

None of the three hardware entries links its staffing moves to AI. The reasons on record are a headquarters move, a leaner data center operation, and budget control. The pattern sets this corner of tech apart from the companies that trimmed payroll to make room for AI spending.

Nike $NKE is cutting about 1,400 roles in its global operations, and most of those positions sit in technology, according to Chief Operating Officer Venkatesh Alagirisamy.

Technology staff carry the larger share of this reduction at a consumer brand. Nike's appearance in a tracker of tech job losses shows the pullback spreading past companies whose business is software. Alagirisamy's account does not say how many of the roughly 1,400 positions fall outside technology. It does establish that tech jobs make up the majority.

T-Mobile $TMUS is reportedly shedding more IT roles after a January round of layoffs. No firm count has surfaced, and reports suggest the total could run into the hundreds. The size of this second round remains an open question. Its target is not. The newer reductions land on the same function Nike singled out.

Both companies aimed their cuts at technology work. Together, they suggest internal IT teams have become a target even at telecom and consumer firms, far from the software makers that fill most of this list.
