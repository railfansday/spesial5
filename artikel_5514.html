From Honky-Tonks to High-End Estates: Inside Nashville’s Lasting Luxury Boom

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Many metros gave up most or even all of their COVID-19 pandemic-era housing market gains, but some kept on trucking, including Nashville, TN.

"Nashville is one of the pandemic boomtowns that kept its boom," writes Realtor.com® senior economist Anthony Smith in his Nashville Luxury Market Highlight report.

Nashville held on to more of its pandemic run-up than any other major Tennessee metro as of September 2026, says the report.

While the area has been hot for years, demand for its luxury homes accelerated sharply after the pandemic, resulting in a 100% increase in prices from February 2020 to October 2026. Unlike other metros that gave up most or even all of its pandemic-era gains, Nashville has retained a healthy 83.3% of them.

During the pandemic, remote work allowed many people to relocate to Music City, where they could enjoy not only the conveniences and excitement of city life, but also lower prices than wherever they were coming from.

And unlike other midsized metros that saw massive pandemic-era gains—such as Denver and Austin, TX—Nashville's prices have kept afloat. Denver, on the other hand, sits 33% below its pandemic peak, and Austin is 29% below.

Nashville's entry-luxury threshold (the top 10% of listings) now stands at $1.65 million versus $899,000 at the start of the pandemic in February 2020. This is only 8.4% below its peak of $1.80 million in April 2022.

In terms of retention, the only Tennessee city that comes out slightly ahead of Nashville is Knoxville, which pulled ahead in October with a 86.4% retention rate from its pandemic-era highs. But Nashville had to hold on to steeper gains, because its run-up was twice as large as Knoxville's.

Knoxville's entry-luxury threshold currently stands at $1.05 million, a smidge below its peak median price of $1.10 million in May 2023.

Another standout in the state is Chattanooga, which has retained nearly 64% of its pandemic gains, while Cleveland, TN, which had the highest run-up of any metro in the state with 137%, has given up all but 28.6% of that.

Nashville's share of luxury listings has also continued to rise, with million-dollar-plus homes making up 18.9% of its listings in September 2026, up from a mere 8.3% five years ago.

This is 4.6 times more luxe listings than in 2020 and is the highest percentage in the state.

Drilling down deeper, the priciest area in the metro is the new-construction haven of Williamson County. Within the county, College Grove (ZIP code 37046) has the steepest price tags, with a median list price of $3,193,165 and a luxury threshold of $7,209,900.

A full 91% of College Grove's home listings are over one million dollars, behind only Arrington at 96%.

Here, grand estates, often new, boast a median 5,304 square feet of space, significantly larger than the second-place finisher, Nashville (Oak Hill) at 4,015 square feet. It also has the highest per-square-foot price at $578.

"We've always remained in a bubble," says local real estate broker Susan Gregory. "Even when the market turned in 2008, we weren't devastated like the rest of the country. Our new construction kept selling, and we kept building."

She attributes the area's pricing resilience to an excellent public school system, as well as generous estates and sizable plots that lure top earners in numerous industries. Of course, Music City is known for its country crooning residents such as Keith Urban, Faith Hill and Tim McGraw, Jason Aldean, or the late Dolly Parton.

And don't forget those who are still migrating to the Southern state from higher-taxed pastures.

"I just sold a house to a couple from Connecticut, and they are like, 'We just got a huge raise, just in taxes,'" the agent tells Realtor.com. She says that with their move, the couple's property taxes dropped from around $30,000 a year to approximately $8,000.

Such wealthy incomers were mostly responsible for the area's run-up, says the broker.

"They were offering stupid money," she says of pandemic-era buyers who would "overbid" on properties. "Our prices doubled, tripled, and quadrupled."

Around this time, she adds, Williamson County changed its zoning so that subdivision lots had to be at least 5 acres—a common tactic employed to rein in development and protect an area's "character."

"Being on 5 acres increases values significantly," she says.

In Nashville proper, the Oak Hill neighborhood's luxury threshold of $5,815,000 is the second highest in the metro.

While the city remains famous for its music mavens, it is also increasingly a hub for nonmusic industries like healthcare, tech, and even finance.

Business behemoths such as Nissan, In-N-Out Burger, Oracle, and AllianceBernstein have been drawn to the city thanks to low or even no taxes.

High corporate earners are swarming new luxury buildings such as the Four Seasons Private Residences and the Residences at Broadwest, which sits atop the Conrad Nashville, part of the Hilton franchise. At the Four Seasons, which is sold out, price tags range from a hefty $5.16 million to $33.5 million.

In trendy, retail-friendly neighborhood The Gulch, The Residences at The Nashville Edition is being built atop the 261-room Edition hotel.

In the sleek, full-service tower, one-bedroom condos start at $1.62 million and four-bedrooms at $8.65 million.

For Corcoran Reverie agent Kris Wylder, prices like these are on par with Nashville's newish luxury market and scene.

"When I first came here, I thought, 'This city is so undervalued for what it is and what it has to offer,'" she previously told Realtor.com. "It has exploded from an amenity standpoint: the restaurants, the health and wellness options, the shopping. It's just been incredible to see.

"To me, [Nashville] is finally just being valued where it should be. I'm like, 'Oh finally, the prices caught up with everything this city has to offer.'"
