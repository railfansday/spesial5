When Your Employee Is a Worrywart

Some employees hide behind their computers or devices to avoid conversation. They fool themselves into believing that no feedback from the boss is good news. This assumption could harm their careers because they are not hearing significant criticism that could help them improve performance. And they are missing opportunities to influence the direction of their jobs by not finding ways to talk to you.

Worrywarts begin unconcerned, stoically accepting this lack of communication as a positive sign. Everything must be going great, or else you would be screaming for changes, right? Then they begin to wonder, afraid to hear a critique. The formerly happy worker bounces back and forth between unhappy and content.

I have been busy lately, but now that the merger is completed, I had better take the time to review progress and next steps with my team. Fortunately, they need little direction from me. Jim, though, is avoiding me and seems to be fluctuating like he is on an emotional elevator. I hate to confront him, but I must find out what is bothering him. I have had too many top employees leave without telling me why.

It’s been a long time since the boss has commented on my work. On one hand, no feedback probably means she is satisfied. My work is good. I do not really need feedback, or do I? But where do I stand since this merger? She gave everyone the same brief message. What if I do have a problem? What if I have been doing something wrong? Am I going to be downsized? I am afraid to ask her. I had better leave well enough alone.

Your goal is to restore the employee’s confidence and refocus their energy.

You: Jim, with the merger completed, there will be some changes. I want to discuss with you how your work may be affected. As you know, one objective is… How do you think you might help us to achieve that?

You: Sorry about that! I value open communication and want you to feel comfortable sharing your questions and concerns. Do you have something you’d like to share? Are you concerned about how I’ll react?

Jim: I’m afraid you’ll think I’m in over my head. But I’d rather hear criticism than not know how I’m doing.

Everyone needs feedback. It is your chance to encourage innovation and unleash talent. Even those who never give you a hard time need it. All workers must know that they are valued. Without feedback, some good workers can become emotional wrecks and some just go elsewhere. Feedback is especially useful when your goal is to gently draw out the uncommunicative worker.
