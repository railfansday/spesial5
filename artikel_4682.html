Caregiver Intel: Healing Resentment at the Holidays

Although Phoebe was happy to live with her mother, Martha, and care for her ever since she had the stroke, there were times when she felt resentful of her two older sisters. Generally, they were supportive and grateful for Phoebe’s sacrifices and efforts. They dropped off meals now and then, and gave Phoebe little breaks here and there. But at the holidays, things were different. Unfortunately, Phoebe’s resentment usually stirred up around Thanksgiving and tended to last through the holiday season.

Traditionally for Thanksgiving, everyone gathered at her older sister Jennifer’s house, about 20 minutes from Mom’s house. Jennifer did most of the cooking, but she expected everyone to bring a side or a dessert. Phoebe struggled to care for Martha and the old house every day and would have appreciated a small holiday from chores. Instead, she juggled her usual tasks with baking a pumpkin pie and somehow managed to get Mom ready for the day.

Since the stroke, Mom had limited use of her left leg and arm and some difficulty with language, so Phoebe had to help her in and out of the car for the drive to Jennifer’s house. She would have been glad to have one of her sisters help Mom out of the car when they arrived. Instead, Jennifer and Janine watched them from the window, smiling and waving to Mom.

Once inside Jennifer’s house, Jennifer and Janine continued their preparations for the big meal. With 12 people for dinner, there was plenty to do. However, neither her sisters nor their husbands or children offered to help with Mom and give Phoebe a break. She felt like the only reason she was there was to take care of Mom. People came over to them, but it was to talk to Mom. When Mom needed help eating, no one blinked an eye. When Mom needed the bathroom, it never occurred to anyone that they should offer to take her.

Phoebe loved her big family and wanted to enjoy her time with them. But ever since Mom had the stroke and she’d moved into her house, it seemed like the family forgot about her and saw her only as Mom’s caregiver. She didn’t want another holiday to feel so terrible. She needed a plan.

When we met with Phoebe, we offered her some strategies that would likely allow her to enjoy the holidays again, while maintaining her important role as Mom’s caregiver. Perhaps you, too, will find them helpful when negotiating caregiving during the holidays.

First, let them know that you appreciate how busy they are with the holiday preparations. Then remind them that the holiday is important to you too and that you’d love to be able to step away from Mom to enjoy time with the rest of your family. Make the task requests specific: “Would you help her cut up her food? Would you take her to the bathroom? Would you sit with her during dessert?

Then gently remind your family members about what they committed to do. Tell them you appreciate their help. It will give you a chance to talk to their children and feel like part of the festivities.

If someone cuts up Mom’s food into pieces that are too big or too small, let them work it out. If a relative isn’t quite sure how to help Mom in the bathroom, calmly explain what is needed. If they don’t do it perfectly, don’t mention it. It’s the effort they’re making to give you a little break that matters.

A little positive reinforcement can go a long way.
