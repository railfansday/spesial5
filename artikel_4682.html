Researchers Just Figured Out a Way to Detect Ovarian Cancer Earlier Than Ever

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

More than 21,000 women in the U.S. are diagnosed with ovarian cancer each year. While it’s not the most common cancer in women, ovarian cancer is considered the deadliest cancer in women worldwide because it’s often diagnosed at later stages, has a high rate of recurrence, and is difficult to treat. Right now, the five-year survival rate of ovarian cancer is just 52 percent.

As with most cancers, detecting the disease early may increase the odds of survival. Researchers recently discovered a new way to detect earlier stages of the cancer, potentially helping thousands of women. The Nature Medicine study breaks down the mechanisms of a blood-based test that can detect ovarian cancer in its precancerous stages. In theory, this could dramatically impact survival rates in women. The test is still in the research phases, but doctors agree that something like this is needed. Here’s what they want women to know.

Meet the experts: Diana Pearre, MD, gynecologic oncologist at The Roy and Patricia Disney Family Cancer Center at Providence Saint Joseph Medical Center in Burbank, CA; Abdulrahman Sinno, MD, chief of the division of gynecologic oncology with Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center, part of the University of Miami Health System

First, some background: Researchers found that women with early-stage high-grade serous ovarian cancer or serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC), which is a precursor to ovarian cancer, have higher-than-usual levels of a type of RNA called Tu-stroma circulating in their blood.

Tu-Stroma is linked with cancer’s early attacks that weaken healthy cells to make room for cancer cell growth. Spotting Tu-stroma means that doctors have an early molecular warning signal that could suggest cancer is developing before a tumor is detected.

The study itself was conducted on hundreds of women around the world with and without cancer and found that the test detected STIC in 92.3 percent of women with the condition. It also correctly identified 98.2 percent of healthy women. They also tried the test on a high-risk group and found that about 72 percent of positive results were accurate, while negative results were almost 100 percent accurate.

But this is still an early discovery, and the researchers point out in the study that more robust research is needed.

“We have very few ways of detecting ovarian cancer,” says Diana Pearre, MD, gynecologic oncologist at The Roy and Patricia Disney Family Cancer Center at Providence Saint Joseph Medical Center in Burbank, California. “There are no screening tests.” Instead, doctors rely on pelvic ultrasounds and labs to look for tumor markers.

“Unfortunately, we subject women to surgery if and when they have ovarian masses that are suspicious,” Dr. Pearre says. “Having another tool to help us discriminate which tumors are more suspicious would be very helpful in detecting cancers at an early stage.”

Abdulrahman Sinno, MD, chief of the division of gynecologic oncology with Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center, part of the University of Miami Health System, agrees. “Eighty percent of high-grade serous carcinoma is diagnosed in advanced stage with no effective screening strategy,” he says.

Previous screening strategies didn’t work because they only spotted cancer at advanced stages, Dr. Sinno says. “Before this, there was no screening test for average-risk women,” he adds.

We’re not at the stage where this can be rolled out to the general public yet. The blood test is still considered experimental and it’s not a clinically validated. Still, doctors say the news is worth following. “This is a promising discovery-stage result, not a test that is ready for prime time,” Dr. Sinno says.
