Do We Get More Generous as We Age?

Most of us are more willing to help people we feel close to. For example, we’ll give freely to a sibling, but think a little longer about a coworker, and hesitate more with someone we barely know. Psychologists call this social discounting — the value we place on another person’s welfare shrinks as social distance grows

A new study of adults in the United States and China suggests this economic phenomenon varies across age groups. For example, older adults are more generous to people with whom they have weaker social connections.

Yi Lu, an assistant professor of psychology at Western Kentucky University, set out to better understand if generosity varies across age. Lu earned her Ph.D. in developmental psychology at Cornell working with professor Corinna E. Löckenhoff, and collaborated on the study with colleagues at The Chinese University of Hong Kong and Peking University.

The research team asked nearly 1,000 adults of various ages from the U.S. and China to make a series of hypothetical decisions about how much money they would share with different people, from a close family member to a complete stranger. Participants also reported their charitable donations. The researchers then compared age groups while accounting for participants' cultural values and other background differences.

In both countries, older adults were the most generous. Compared with younger adults, they were less influenced by social distance (more willing to help acquaintances and strangers) and they gave more to charity.

Lu points out that many people assume older adults become more self-focused or simply have less to give as they age. But, she says, the data told a different story. Because the pattern appeared in two different cultural settings, it may reflect a broader pattern about how people change with age.

While both Chinese and American older adults were more likely to give than younger adults, there were some cultural differences. Regardless of age, people who placed greater value on equality and closeness with others tended to be more generous. Those who placed greater value on personal achievement and status tended to be less generous. Taken together, the findings suggest that generosity in adulthood reflects both how old we are and the values we hold.

Lu notes several limitations to this study. The questions were hypothetical, so they capture what people said they would do, not necessarily what they would do with real money in the moment; and the real charitable donations were self-reported. The study also compared people of different ages at one point in time. That shows older and younger adults differ in their generosity, but it doesn’t prove that people become more generous as they age, because generational differences may play a role.

The study also doesn’t explain why older adults give more, Lu notes. Researchers have some ideas, such as a shift toward emotionally meaningful goals later in life, but there isn’t concrete evidence that points to a reason.

“This is one of the few studies to look at how people of different ages share resources with others across social distances and across cultures,” Lu said. “In both the U.S. and China, older adults in our study were more willing to extend generosity beyond their close circle. That matters for how we think about aging. Older adults aren’t just recipients of support; they can be an important source of generosity in their communities.”

The take-home message: Older adults are more likely to give to people beyond their social circles than compared to younger adults. And no matter the age, people who value equality and closeness tend to be more generous overall.
