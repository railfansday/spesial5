A rose-colored rock found in the Sahara turned out to be a 4.1-billion-year-old piece of Mars

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

A 4.1-billion-year-old Martian meteorite found in Mali's Sahara Desert reveals clues about ancient crust, water activity and Mars' early history.

A rose-colored meteorite found in the Sahara Desert formed on Mars at least 4.1 billion years ago, a study published October 29, 2026, reports.

Named Teghaza 001 after the salt plain in Mali where it was discovered, it is one of roughly 435 known meteorites from Mars, NASA says. It is also one of the three most ancient Martian meteorites found on Earth.

Its composition, mineralogy and texture are distinct from those of other Martian meteorites.

Meteorite hunters found eight stones at the beginning of September 2022, weighing nearly 1.8 pounds (798.5 grams) in total.

The largest weighs about 15.6 ounces (442 grams), the Meteoritical Bulletin, the official record of named meteorites, shows.

The stones had no fusion crust, the glassy skin that forms during a fall. Their surfaces were sandblasted, and the largest pieces carried mineral coatings from burial in the desert.

At first, Teghaza 001 appeared to be a shergottite, the most common class of meteorite from Mars. It is about 50 percent pyroxene and 40 percent maskelynite, a glass formed when an impact shocks plagioclase feldspar.

Many of its pyroxene crystals have cores with thin layers of iron and manganese oxides, a product of alteration.

Oxygen isotope measurements at the University of New Mexico (UNM), which holds a 0.8-ounce (22-gram) type specimen, matched known Martian meteorites. Carl Agee, director and curator of UNM’s Institute of Meteoritics and a co-author of the study, classified the rock.

“This is just one single meteorite. It’s like going out back in time and chipping off a hunk of an outcrop,” Agee said.

The lead author, Yang Liu of NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, and her colleagues found tiny zircon crystals in the rock. Such crystals allow scientists to date igneous rocks with uranium-lead measurements.

A second method, in-situ rubidium-strontium dating, analyzed single mineral grains on a piece of the meteorite smaller than a fingernail. Further analysis showed when the rock formed and when changes such as water alteration occurred.

The team describes Teghaza 001 as a slowly cooled igneous rock and a sample of ancient crust. That crust formed from a geochemically distinct mantle source with a primordial composition.

“It was only after we started looking closely that we realized this meteorite is really unique,” Liu said.

Its exact origin can never be known for sure, but features of the rock link it to highlands in the southern hemisphere of Mars. Using data from NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, the researchers identified 13 locations whose mineralogy and chemistry match it.

“This is really our first piece of Martian crust from a part of Mars we’ve never had a sample of before,” said Lee Saper, the second author.

Saper did the research as a postdoctoral scholar at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and now works at NASA’s Ames Research Center.

Most Martian meteorites are roughly 200 million years old, according to UNM. NASA says the two other ancient ones give more limited glimpses of Mars’ geological history.

One is ALH84001, discovered in 1984 and composed almost entirely of one mineral.

A University of Houston team used lutetium-hafnium isotopes in 2010 to date it to 4.091 billion years, about 400 million years younger than earlier estimates. That age is similar to Teghaza 001’s.

The other, nicknamed “Black Beauty,” formed about 2.1 billion years ago and is made of small, volcanic rock fragments. Some of the minerals inside it are far older.

They include a 4.45 billion-year-old zircon. Scientists at Curtin University have reported that this grain preserves evidence of damage that only occurs during large meteorite impacts.

“I realized that this one was a little bit like Black Beauty, not exactly, but it resembled it in the oxygen isotope values,” Agee said of Teghaza 001.

Teghaza 001’s water-altered minerals and oxygen isotopes indicate that it reacted with fluids enriched in oxygen-18 before an impact blasted it into space. Some fine-grained carbonate, sulfate and clay minerals in the rock formed later, on Earth.

Martian water altered the rock sometime around 4.1 billion years ago, which suggests it formed even earlier, NASA said. Clues in its oxygen isotopes, texture and chemistry point to a much more dynamic water cycle on early Mars.

A June 2026 study of its water, led by Saper, had already reported the meteorite’s age and identified it as a gabbroic diorite.

Hydrogen isotopes in its phosphate minerals suggest that Martian water then held at least about twice as much deuterium, relative to hydrogen, as Earth’s seawater.

That minimum value supports models in which hydrogen escaped rapidly to space from Mars’ young atmosphere.

UNM ties the rock to the Pre-Noachian, the period from about 4.5 billion to 4.1 billion years ago.

In the Noachian that followed, until 3.7 billion years ago, groundwater chemically altered many rocks into clays, the European Space Agency notes.

The study was published in the journal Science.
