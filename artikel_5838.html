The Real Nvidia Scandal

How China Exploited a Massive Loophole in U.S. National Security—and How Congress Can Close It

The reinstatement of Jensen Huang as CEO of Nvidia this week added another twist to a scandal that has transfixed Washington and Silicon Valley. The stakes go beyond the swings of the stock market or the salacious details about Huang’s affairs, including with Alice Huang, a former executive at the Singaporean firm Megaspeed and a potential Chinese asset. At its core, the scandal has exposed something that matters far more than any one man or company: American export-control policy remains riddled with loopholes, hobbled by incapacity and infighting, and divorced from clear strategy. Unless Washington fixes this, it risks imperiling the United States’ hard-won advantage in artificial intelligence.

This export policy failure is more than the sum of specific deficiencies. It stems from the Trump administration’s broader posture on China. Washington has embraced unfettered AI acceleration at the same time as it has struck a more accommodating stance toward Beijing. That combination set the stage for the explosive Megaspeed scandal—and perhaps others that have yet to emerge. As a result, China has been able to access an alarming number of advanced chips that have enabled it to narrow the AI gap with the United States and power its AI-driven military modernization. According to the AI research institute Epoch AI, up to a third of China’s AI computing power may come from smuggled chips. China’s top AI models are now just months behind the U.S. frontier. The narrow gap reflects China’s genuine innovation and access to abundant energy resources as well as its relentless efforts to circumvent U.S. controls.

Huang’s return to Nvidia at the behest of President Donald Trump suggests that both the company and the White House seem utterly uninterested in keeping powerful AI chips from China. It will instead fall to Congress to ensure that the United States leverages its compute advantage and maintains its AI edge. The midterm elections and mounting bipartisan concerns over the Megaspeed debacle give Congress new momentum to correct course. Its near-term agenda should include closing known loopholes that played a key role in allowing Megaspeed to supply advanced chips to China, stepping up enforcement of existing controls, and strengthening oversight of the administration’s export-control decisions.

Over the medium term, Congress must also tackle the broader question of how to balance the strategic benefits of exporting U.S. technology to build the world’s AI infrastructure with robust controls to prevent its leakage to China. The long-standing debate over which American AI chips and capabilities Washington should permit to diffuse globally and under what conditions has finally boiled over into the mainstream—and, in a perverse way, the national security community can thank Alice Huang for forcing the issue.

The issue before Congress, however, is much broader than addressing the specifics of the Nvidia scandal. Congress must also contend with the underlying impulses driving the administration’s China policy and install guardrails to avoid the erosion of U.S. export controls. This means that after Congress passes near-term bills to mop up the Megaspeed mess, it must then take the reins on setting long-term export-control strategy.

Congress cannot stand by idly as AI blazes forward. OpenAI’s top model can now perform professional tasks as well as or better than humans 85 percent of the time, up from just 14 percent in 2024. The time an AI model can independently work on a task is doubling every four months. Last week, a researcher at Anthropic announced that AI helped him untangle the Riemann hypothesis, one of the most famous problems in pure mathematics.

As capabilities have grown, so have risks. Frontier models can now complete simulated cyberattacks on networks on their own, and hackers are using AI models to partially automate their attacks. In the July incident involving the AI platform Hugging Face, rogue AI agents escaped their testing environment and plotted to evade human oversight, underscoring the difficulty of aligning model behavior with human intent. The growing power of AI and the risks from misuse or loss of control make U.S. leadership a national security imperative.

At its core, the U.S.-Chinese AI competition boils down to which country can deploy the best chips in the highest quantities to train and adopt advanced AI models. The Biden administration’s controls slowed Chinese AI developers by leaving them with fewer, worse chips. Nvidia’s top Blackwell chips are nearly seven times better than Huawei’s Ascend equivalents. By the end of 2026, Huawei will likely have produced less than four percent of the compute that Nvidia will have over the same time. As the founder and CEO of DeepSeek, one of China’s top AI companies, admitted in 2024, “Our problem has never been money—it’s the embargo on high-end chips.”

To circumvent Biden-era restrictions, elaborate networks for smuggling and transshipment emerged to divert chips to China from other countries that could legally acquire them, and Chinese companies leveraged export-control loopholes to remotely access U.S.-powered compute through data centers abroad. Chinese firms funded companies such as Megaspeed to facilitate these workarounds. Backed by the Chinese tech giant Alibaba, Megaspeed has purchased $4 billion worth of Nvidia chips since 2023, prompting an investigation from the U.S. government. Chinese companies also used a technique known as adversarial distillation—training their models on the outputs of top U.S. models at a fraction of the compute. All of this needlessly shrank the United States’ AI lead over China, even after the imposition of export controls.

These developments should have spurred the Trump administration to better resource, enforce, and update the export controls when it came into office. It did the opposite. In May 2025, the administration suspended the Biden administration’s AI Diffusion Rule, which had established rules for which countries could access advanced chips and in what quantities. Then, after initially prohibiting sales to China of Nvidia’s H20 chip, which was custom-built as a less powerful product for the Chinese market, the Trump administration reversed course in December 2025 and greenlighted the sale of Nvidia’s H200 chip. Although the H200 is two generations behind top U.S. chips, it remains superior to anything China can produce. An investigation from the House Foreign Affairs Committee in November revealed that between December 2025 and May 2026, the Trump administration approved the sale of more than 800,000 of Nvidia’s H200 chips and AMD’s MI325X equivalent, including to companies that the Pentagon has designated as linked to the Chinese military. This week, under pressure from Congress, the administration added Megaspeed to the entity list, which blocks those listed from buying U.S. products without a license—but before that, the administration had not added a single new individual or business to the list in 2026.

This regulatory vacuum and policy incoherence created the perfect conditions for Megaspeed. The Singaporean firm at the center of the Nvidia scandal seems tailor-made to operate in the seams of U.S. export controls. In 2024, the Commerce Department initiated a formal investigation of the company, which could have led to sanctions such as being placed on the entity list. But the Trump administration dragged its feet in pursuing the investigation. In the meantime, Megaspeed stocked Southeast Asian data centers with thousands of U.S. chips, enabling remote access for Chinese firms. This remote access loophole in U.S. export controls was a well-known and increasingly popular workaround for China-linked firms, but the Trump administration left it open for nearly two years and closed it only last week amid pressure from the Nvidia scandal. Regardless of the Megaspeed specifics, the net result of the Trump administration’s actions and inaction is that China has enjoyed access to far more computing power than it should have during a vital period of the AI competition.

The administration’s long-awaited closure of the remote access loophole is a welcome step. The rule requires foreign data centers to secure a license before offering significant computing power to China-based customers. It also requires a license to export advanced chips to places that could facilitate remote access and chip diversion, including Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. This rule will make it harder for firms such as Megaspeed to import chips in the first place, or to use them to serve China-based customers. But the rule will be difficult to enforce because the U.S. Commerce Department does not have the legal authority under export controls to directly regulate services such as cloud computing. More important, the administration’s lax enforcement of past controls—and internal divisions over the new ones—raise serious questions about its willingness to follow through. Huang’s reinstatement as CEO, with Trump’s backing, will make it harder to vigorously enforce these new controls and move toward a tougher posture on China.

Since Congress cannot rely on the administration to fix export controls and protect the United States’ AI lead, lawmakers should urgently advance bipartisan measures to address the underlying conditions that gave rise to entities such as Megaspeed. As a starting point, Congress should pass the Remote Access Security Act, which would give the administration clear legal authority for controlling remote access to data centers powered by U.S. compute. While implementation will require close congressional oversight, getting the right legal structure in place is an important first step. Next, it should work with industry and technical experts to fund and fast-track “on-chip” technologies—basically, features that can verify a chip’s location and counter smuggling and diversion. Beyond sorting out the technical feasibilities, Congress will need to work with industry to understand the implications of on-chip security mechanisms for global competitiveness and the potential surveillance risks should China develop similar controls since it produces much of the world’s legacy chips.

Along with these technical fixes, Congress should seize on the Megaspeed scandal to strengthen oversight. The AI Overwatch Act, which may be included in the annual defense bill, would codify existing export controls on advanced chips into law, preventing the current or a future administration from reversing them. It would also empower Congress to review and block advanced chips sales to U.S. adversaries. Congress can go even further by establishing a new investigative committee or inspector general to strengthen oversight of sensitive chip export-control policies. Together, these actions would go a long way toward combating the circumvention that let firms such as Megaspeed thrive.

At the same time, closing loopholes and codifying rules is insufficient to fix the underlying issues that led to the Megaspeed scandal in the first place. This mess is directly downstream from Trump’s dovish shift on China that has seen the administration take a softer line on Beijing just as the AI race accelerated. The administration’s own overreach forced Washington into a conciliatory posture. Administration officials failed to realize that China had learned its lesson from the first Trump administration’s trade wars and was poised to retaliate hard in the second term. After China responded to Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs and export controls with its own punishing controls on rare earths, the Trump administration found its way into a vaguely defined and fragile truce. This has severely constrained U.S. policymaking, including related to compute controls.

Indeed, Trump became an easy sell for advocates of aggressive AI promotion such as Jensen Huang. This camp argues that U.S. leadership in the global AI race requires pushing American AI technologies to as many parts of the globe as possible—including China. They claim that keeping China “addicted” to the U.S. AI stack gives Washington leverage and outweighs the national security concerns that could come with helping China improve its AI capabilities. Huang even pushed demonstrably false narratives that China’s military was not interested in Nvidia’s chips and labeled his critics as “unpatriotic” for not wanting U.S. chips spread as far and wide as possible and therefore depriving American tech firms of global market share.

Trump, who was interested in stabilizing the relationship with China and prioritizing near-term, transactional victories on trade, bought it. He also broke with decades of U.S. foreign policy precedent to negotiate export controls with China. In a series of “do nothing” leader-level meetings since, the president has emphasized summitry optics over pushing China on any sensitive issues. And Huang’s restoration at Nvidia ensures that the president’s worst impulses on China will continue to be egged on by his favored tech CEO, to the detriment of U.S. national security.

A core part of Congress’s longer-term agenda should be outlining the basic principles to determine who can import or remotely access advanced AI chips and under what conditions. Administrations have gone back and forth over this line-drawing exercise. The Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule would have imposed a global licensing regime that controlled not just the export of chips but also the aggregate U.S.-powered compute that any country could receive. Under Trump, only a handful of countries face restrictions. A smarter approach would condition advanced chip sales on the buyer’s ability to protect them from diversion and guard against misuse by malign actors looking to skirt safety and security rules. Allied and partner governments could demonstrate this by developing robust economic security policies to prevent chip diversion or remote access. Foreign companies would have to show strong compliance and security commitments. In practice, this would mean having internal procedures to ensure that chips are not diverted and maintaining cooperation with Commerce Department officials to verify that such procedures were working.

Congress can also help rebuild allies’ confidence in U.S. export-control policy. There was hard-won, if imperfect, progress on allied alignment in key areas of export controls under the Biden administration, notably with the Dutch and the Japanese on controls of semiconductor manufacturing equipment. The painstaking work needed to get allies on board can be agonizingly slow, but once achieved, it is far more likely to endure than if the United States imposes extraterritorial controls over their objections. The MATCH Act, one of the bills revived following the Megaspeed scandal, requires controls on allied-produced tools if Washington is unable to negotiate allied alignment. It is a rather rude approach at a moment when U.S. export controls seem to be on shaky footing, U.S. tariffs against allies have pushed relations to a near breaking point, and allies rightly question whether the United States still views China as a strategic threat. Instead, Congress should support efforts to create durable frameworks for allied alignment, such as economic security agreements in key strategic sectors.

A longer-term agenda must include fixing the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security, the main agency responsible for administering and enforcing export controls. The bureau has been gutted by dismissals and low morale during the Trump administration, but it has been underresourced for far longer. Congress should increase funding and require modernization of the bureau’s antiquated IT system. Just as important, Congress needs to help insulate policymaking decisions from corporate influence, and it can help by insisting that future Commerce Department officials commit to independent and risk-based decision-making to earn confirmation.

None of these steps will be easy. The president’s support of Huang’s restoration shows that stock market fluctuations weigh more heavily in his decision-making than national security, and the administration is unlikely to welcome increased congressional oversight. The Constitution gives the executive wide deference on matters of foreign policy and national security. But Congress has a role in ensuring that the president faithfully executes the laws and is supported by the right resources, oversight, and strategic priorities. The United States’ AI lead may hinge on whether it meets the moment.
