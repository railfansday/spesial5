An Olympian’s Incline Treadmill Workout That Tracks Your Fitness Gains All Winter Long

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Break up indoor miles while measuring offseason progress.

When winter weather pushes your runs indoors, using the treadmill as a training tool can make it easier to keep up with your routine. But logging miles in the same spot can get tedious, especially when you’re used to the fresh air and changing scenery.

So how can you make indoor miles more exciting? Find a workout that keeps you engaged and helps you make progress until it’s time to head back outside.

Enter: pro runner Joe Klecker’s incline tempo workout. The Olympian returns to this treadmill workout throughout the winter, using it to challenge himself and check in on his fitness as the season progresses.

Add it to your repertoire and you’ll have a workout you can come back to throughout your own offseason—plus a method for measuring how much stronger you’re getting before your next training block.

Klecker’s workout has roots in another runner’s winter training: his mother, 1992 Olympic marathoner Janis Klecker. She used the session while preparing for the marathon in Minnesota, he previously told Runner’s World.

The workout calls for a sustained effort with a change in incline every quarter mile. These alternating stretches break up a longer tempo run into smaller segments, which can make the time on the treadmill feel more manageable. They also give you a chance to practice holding a steady effort while the grade changes.

Klecker explains that he sometimes alternates between marathon and half marathon pace because the incline portions make the tempo workout significantly harder.

Keep in mind that Klecker’s full session is a big workout: With the warmup and cooldown, it adds up to eight to 10 miles. Adjust the distance and your effort according to your current fitness level and training capacity.

One of the biggest benefits of this workout comes when you return to it multiple times in an offseason. Klecker told Runner’s World if he completes the six tempo miles comfortably once, he may pick up the pace slightly the next time he does the workout. But if he struggles getting through just five miles, he keeps the pace where it is until he’s fit enough to complete all six.

This gives him a way to compare his fitness across different points throughout the winter. You can use the same approach: After each session, record your pace and how the effort felt in your training log. When you repeat the workout, use those notes as a useful comparison.

Also, it’s important to remember that fatigue can affect how you feel on a given day. Look at your progress across several repetitions of this workout, and don’t rush into faster paces until your workouts consistently indicate you’re ready.

Coming back to a familiar workout not only lets you track your progress, but it can also help you build toward faster running. Regularly practicing a challenging effort gives your body a training stimulus to adapt to, and gradually adding to that stimulus—by adding time or increasing speed—as you improve supports continued progress.

Exercise physiologist Greg Grosicki, PhD, previously suggested to Runner’s World to start with an easy speed session like four reps of 800-meter repeats with two minutes of easy jogging or walking between each to measure speed gains. Each time you revisit the workout, see if you can finish it faster and, if you can comfortably do so, add another interval or increase the distance. If not, focus on endurance for a bit before redoing the workout.

You can use a similar strategy with the above tempo workout: Start at just four miles at marathon pace (or even three). If you feel like the workout was too easy the first time, bump up the speed when you do the workout again in a couple weeks. If you struggled to finish it? Try again after a few weeks of training to see if you can complete it. The results in either case signal whether your training is moving in the right direction.
