How many times in your life have you walked into the room of someone you barely know, stripped down to your underwear or less, and revealed intimate details about your life and personal habits, all within the space of 10 minutes?

This scenario highlights how much courage is required to attend a routine doctor’s appointment. We expect our patients to tell the truth and to reveal what’s going on underneath their symptoms.

They may have to talk about drinking, eating, sex, bowel movements, loneliness, fear, the medication they haven’t even opened, the symptoms they’ve Googled at three in the morning, or their worry that something is seriously wrong. Picture yourself doing all that while a virtual stranger takes notes, looks at their screen, and surreptitiously glances at the clock when they think you’re not looking.

We expect people to share so many intimate details when they barely know us, and I’ve lost count of how many times a patient has turned to me as they’re about to leave my consultation room, their hand hovering above the door handle. They might say, “There’s one more thing.” Or, “Could I ask another question?” Then the real consultation begins.

Am I contributing to this delayed trust? And how can I help the people in my care feel safer sooner? I know how I act when I’m running late because my wife and family are excellent mirrors. I fidget in my chair and start looking at the door, mentally preparing my "to do before lunch" list. The professional version of me may still be listening, sprinting down the diagnostic tree, faithful to my training, while the personal me is already wondering how on earth I will get everything done and still show up for the kids’ concert.

Hubris might tell me I’m concealing this rather well, but I’m probably not. People notice when our hand moves to the keyboard too quickly or when we fill a silence that, if left untended, might otherwise have allowed something difficult to emerge. They are aware when we’re not with them and monitor us for evidence: Is it safe to trust? I've become much more curious about why we as humans are so sensitive to these signals.

Attachment theory gives us a lens that might sharpen our understanding. As children, we discover early what happens after we cry, get scared, make mistakes, or express our needs. If someone responds consistently, we may come to expect that it’s safe to show our distress or discomfort and that help will be there when we need it. When that response is less reliable, different ways of protecting ourselves may come to the fore. One of those lessons: When help doesn’t come, we had better fend for ourselves.

That might explain why a patient who has spent 50 years trying not to inconvenience anyone wouldn’t abandon that habit just because she has walked into my consulting room.

Amy Edmondson’s research on psychological safety shows the importance of speaking up without fear of humiliation or punishment. There is something useful here for medicine to explore, too.

Some of my patients remember surprisingly little of the specific information I’ve given them during a consultation, particularly if their condition is considered serious. What they do report back, often years later, is how they felt while they took it in. I like to think we have some control over that environment.

Medicine has changed enormously during my career and will no doubt continue to develop. Artificial intelligence is already becoming useful in diagnosis, while wearable devices can accurately track our health whether we’re snoozing or exercising. As a young doctor, the possibilities of precision medicine would have blown my mind. These days, I would add a note of caution: The increasing sophistication of our tools doesn’t make the old-fashioned business of paying attention obsolete.

The most useful thing I learn in a consultation rarely comes from a scan or blood test. It comes from waiting long enough for someone to say, "There's one more thing." I still don’t always get this right. I interrupt, and I get distracted. Undoubtedly, there is more than one patient who never got to say what they wanted to say to me. But I’ve become much more interested in that hovering hand. Now, whenever I hear “There’s one more thing,” I sit back down.
