Conformity, Individuality, and the Lawns of Levittown

Just a few miles from my home is historic Levittown, Long Island, where immediately after WWII, 4,000 acres of potato fields were plowed under to mass-produce more than 17,000 detached houses; many of these four-and-a-half-room homes were constructed in about two days.

Selling for less than $8,000 ($115,000 in 2024 dollars), each house came with a TV set and hi-fi. Buyers could choose a red, yellow, or blue house in one of three styles, in neighborhoods largely devoid of trees. This first modern suburb prompted historian Lewis Mumford to describe Levittown as “a uniform environment from which escape is impossible.”

A popular folksong tagged Levittown and other such developments as places of little boxes “made out of ticky tacky/And they all look the same.” No longer.

Drive through the area today, and it is nearly impossible to imagine the architectural conformity that it once was. What is striking about Levittown is that as houses aged, they became distinctive from one another and went decidedly upscale. They expanded upward and sideways. These four-and-a-half-room homes now are four- or five-bedroom houses with two baths. Barren landscapes are long gone. In 2024, Levittown houses were listed in the $700,000 range.

The sterile 1950s transformed into the turbulent '60s, with subsequent decades bringing tremendous social change in their wake. New generations moved in with new ideas; they had more money than did the returning war veterans. With time, Levittown’s appearance reflected the tastes of new homeowners.

Levittown reflects two contradictory impulses in human nature: The individuality impulse on the one hand, and conformity on the other. It is the universal desire to be recognized as a unique individual but at the same time desiring to be part of a group. The distinctiveness of the home displays this while adhering to the idealized standard of a suburban front lawn. You can drive for miles through Levittown—and almost every suburban neighborhood—and find that while the houses are no longer alike, not a single house has dispensed with grass lawns.

This would be a landscaping choice if it didn’t also mean that, to maintain the carpet-like appearance, the air is polluted with the fumes of mowers, the groundwater is poisoned with fertilizers (Long Island depends exclusively on its aquifer for its drinking water), and a din created by leaf blowers makes a quiet summer day around the house a rare event. Dispensing with lawns will probably have to be a policy decision, as there is only so much individuality that a person can express without feeling like an outsider. The change will have to come as an imposed municipal edict; not having a manicured lawn becomes a new enforced norm, where conformity becomes not having a grassy lawn.
