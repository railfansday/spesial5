The Unproven Economics of the Weekend Cruise

Cruise lines are putting some of their biggest ships on shorter trips, but the financial evidence that those voyages generate better returns remains thin.

Cruise lines are moving some of their biggest ships onto three- and four-night trips, betting that shorter vacations will bring customers back more often. But more passengers are not the same as more profit. Norwegian shortened its average cruise this year, carried 24% more guests, and earned less revenue and gross margin per passenger-day. That does not prove short cruises are bad business, but it does mean the weekend math needs more scrutiny.

For decades, the industry sold seven-night vacations. As ships grew larger and added water parks, more restaurants, and bigger shows, short sailings went to older, smaller ships that sold for less.

Disney was already doing this with the Wish in 2022, and Virgin Voyages launched its first ship on four- and five-night Caribbean itineraries in 2021.

Cruise lines are putting their newest and largest ships on three- and four-night itineraries, a break from the seven-night template the industry built its economics around. Royal Caribbean started it at megaship scale with Utopia of the Seas from Port Canaveral in 2024, and Carnival will follow with Mardi Gras on four- and five-day Bahamas sailings from the same port in May 2027. The logic is frequency: a ship running alternating short trips serves two sets of guests a week instead of one, doubling embarkations and the chance to sell drink packages, specialty dining, and excursions. Royal Caribbean Group CEO Jason Liberty said on the October 27 earnings call that half of the group's guests are millennials or younger, that they spend about as much on a short trip as on a seven-night cruise, and that onboard spending has beaten expectations. The filings are harder to read. Royal Caribbean's revenue per passenger-day rose only to about $320 from $315 in the first nine months of 2026, with ticket revenue per passenger-day flat, and its average voyage barely shortened, from 6.2 days to 6.1. Norwegian, which has cut its average cruise from about 8.6 days to 7.5, carried 24% more passengers but saw revenue per passenger-day fall from $401 to $385 and gross margin per passenger-day drop nearly 11%. Neither company reports results by cruise length, so the weekend model's profitability cannot be verified from public disclosures.
