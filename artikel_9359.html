The decision between writing your own will and hiring an attorney is not about cost — it is about complexity. A DIY will is perfectly appropriate for a large percentage of Americans, but certain situations demand professional legal counsel.

If your situation falls into the DIY-appropriate category — which covers roughly 70-80% of American households — you can create a legally valid will for free or under $200 in about 30 minutes.

The online legal services market has matured significantly, and several platforms now produce wills that are functionally equivalent to what a basic estate planning attorney would draft. Here is a detailed comparison of the leading options:

Our recommendation for most FI-focused households: Trust & Will offers the best balance of comprehensiveness and value. The $159 individual plan ($259 for couples) includes your will, healthcare directive, and power of attorney — the three essential documents every adult needs. FreeWill is excellent if you want a basic will at zero cost and do not need power of attorney or trust options.

All of these services work the same way: you answer a series of guided questions about your family, assets, beneficiaries, and wishes. The platform generates a state-specific legal document that you print, sign in front of witnesses, and store. The questionnaire approach actually reduces errors compared to attorney-drafted wills because the software forces you to address every required element.

A legally valid will must contain specific elements. Missing any of them can result in your will being thrown out by a probate court, which means your assets are distributed according to your state's default intestacy laws — not your wishes.

Your will does not control assets with named beneficiaries. Life insurance proceeds, 401(k) and IRA accounts, joint bank accounts, and property held in joint tenancy all pass directly to the named beneficiary or surviving co-owner regardless of what your will says. This is the single most important point that DIY will makers must understand. If your 401(k) beneficiary form names your ex-spouse, your ex-spouse gets the money — even if your will says otherwise. Review ALL beneficiary designations as part of your estate planning process.

The number one estate planning mistake for people building wealth is outdated beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance policies. Your will does NOT override these. If you changed jobs, got married, divorced, or had children since you last updated your 401(k) and IRA beneficiary forms, update them today. This single action prevents more estate planning disasters than any other — and it costs nothing.

Holographic wills are recognized in about 25 states including California, Texas, Virginia, and North Carolina. They must be entirely in your handwriting, signed, and dated. However, they are far more likely to be challenged in probate because they lack witnesses and often contain vague or incomplete instructions. A typed, witnessed will from an online service is always more reliable than a handwritten one, even in states that accept holographic wills.

No. You do not file or register your will while you are alive. The will is submitted to probate court after your death by your executor. During your lifetime, store the original in a secure, accessible location and tell your executor where to find it. Some states allow you to file the will with the court for safekeeping, but this is optional and not required for validity.

Review your will every 3-5 years and update it after any major life event: marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary or executor, significant change in assets, or moving to a new state. A codicil (amendment) can handle minor changes, but for major revisions, creating an entirely new will is cleaner and reduces the risk of confusion or legal challenge.

Yes. All major online will makers include guardian nomination for minor children. This is one of the most important reasons to have a will — without one, a court will decide who raises your children based on state law, which may not align with your wishes. Name a primary guardian and an alternate, and discuss your decision with the chosen guardians before finalizing the document.

If you die without a will (intestate), your state laws determine who inherits your assets. Typically, everything goes to your spouse and children in proportions set by state statute — which may not match your wishes. Unmarried partners receive nothing. Close friends and favorite charities receive nothing. The court appoints an administrator (who may not be the person you would choose) and the probate process takes longer and costs more. For parents of minor children, the court also decides guardianship.

Writing a will without a lawyer is straightforward, affordable, and legally valid for the majority of American households. Online will makers like Trust & Will ($159) and FreeWill (free) produce state-specific documents in 15-30 minutes that cover everything a basic estate needs. The key to a valid DIY will is proper witnessing, specific beneficiary language, and a residuary clause. Just as importantly, review and update the beneficiary designations on all your retirement accounts and life insurance policies — these override your will and are the most common source of estate planning failures. The only thing worse than a bad will is no will at all. Do it this month.
