Think Your Pack Is Heavy? This Hiker Carries 220 Pounds For Fun

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

On a Saturday in Slovakia’s Tatra Mountains, Regan Bridge stood in a line of tourists at the foot of a cliff studded with ladders and chains. Strapped to his back was his own 120-liter army pack, stuffed with 132 pounds of supplies. A carry strap ran across his forehead. “It was ugly,” he says.

He couldn’t get the pack up the ladder, so Bridge did what nobody was supposed to do and climbed the chains on the path designated for the descent, in full view of the local porters, who are famous for hauling extraordinary weights.

When he hikes back home in New Zealand, 54-year-old Bridge’s setup looks like a stunt. Toolboxes stacked high, a ladder, and up to 220 pounds adorn the back of the log truck driver. Up close, he is the opposite of a stuntman: He has no social media and makes no money from his hobby.

I write about ultralight gear, so on paper Bridge and I don’t have much in common. I carry light, while he hauls as much as he can. He gets jokes from passersby about bringing the whole house, and I get asked whether my boyfriend is carrying my sleeping bag. But we’ve both learned how easily others can misconstrue the way we pack, and how little that has to do with why we do it.

Having previously been involved in stone lifting, Bridge was no stranger to unusual tests of strength. His interest in carrying heavy loads in the backcountry began when he volunteered to transport 15 predator traps for a New Zealand conservation project. Typically, workers were only allowed to haul two at a time. Bridge asked for the rule to change; the organization waived its weight limit for him, and he cleared them all in two trips. The experience expanded his sense of what the human body could manage. “It grew my mindset,” he says, and he began looking for new ways to test it.

Since then, Bridge has slowly piled on more weight. His setup includes duplicate hiking gear, even multiple pairs of footwear, to maximize the load. The toolboxes hold water bottles, which he empties at the summit, roughly halving his load for the descent. He uses the ladder to get the pack on and off and to rest along the way; once it exceeds 110 pounds (50 kilograms), he can no longer lift it off the ground himself. Bridge admits he now favors steep, demanding dayhikes for the adrenaline rush; longer, multiday trips have lost their appeal.

After watching a documentary about Slovakia’s nosiči, the mountain porters who carry supplies to remote huts, Bridge traveled there hoping to experience it for himself. His wife of 25 years found a place that offered tourists the chance to carry supplies between huts in exchange for a free coffee. The suggested load was 44 pounds, or 20 kilos. “I looked at that and went, I ain’t carrying no 20 kilos.”

Instead, Bridge carried double the suggested weight up to the top hut on the first day, and was rewarded with coffee and rum, which, he says, “made that trip down really fun.” The following day his climb up the chains caught the porters’ attention. They offered him a turn carrying their traditional frame, laden with 110 pounds.

“They’re probably stepping out on a limb to give me a go,” he reflects. When he told them he did this for fun back home, they thought he was “a little bit crazy.” But among the porters, he was no oddity. He was “a dime a dozen.”

People can be quick to judge. Other hikers tell him it’s a waste of time; others warn his knees won’t last. “And I’m looking at them going, well, you’re tramping [hiking]. That’s a waste of time too.”

“I want to live. I want to do stuff,” he says. “If I have to get a knee done when I’m 60, I’ll get a knee done.”

He likes the history behind the porters’ work, but says, “I also like the space it puts me in while I’m doing it.” Climbing is his favorite part. “The climb just clears my brain a bit more, but I still have the chance to think about where I am, have a look at the view.” Descents are different; he’s too focused on not getting hurt. I’d pictured a stoic, silent grind; instead, he hauls to a soundtrack ranging from AC/DC to ’90s dance music. Asked what keeps him coming back, Bridge describes it as “just feeding the soul.”

Next, he wants to take 220 pounds (100 kilograms) up the 2,200 steps of the Sealy Tarns Track in Aoraki National Park. But his carefully balanced setup makes exposed climbs tricky.:“You put wind in there, it’s diabolical.”

Before we hung up, Regan pulled out the questions I’d sent ahead of our interview, which he’d printed, and went through them to ensure we’d covered everything. He wanted the facts right, because through translation, things can change.

“All of a sudden, I’m carrying 120 kilos (264 pounds),” he says. “I don’t want people to think that I’ve done something that I haven’t.”

He has no sponsors and no followers, yet he still wanted every number right—no flattering angle, no bigger feat, just a man who didn’t want credit for a kilo he hadn’t carried.

When the call ended, Bridge was off to spend an hour training, as he does six days a week. I’d asked him earlier whether there would ever be a peak, a weight where he’d say, “that’s enough”.

“One day I’m going to wake up and go, yep, I’m there. I’m happy,” he says. “And until then, it’s still going to continue to be fun.”
