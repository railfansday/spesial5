Opinion: Maybe in-person four-year institutions aren’t the right approach to medical education

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Serafini is currently a fourth-year medical student at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai.

This article is part of a new First Opinion series on redesigning medical school.

Over eight years as an M.D./Ph.D. student, I have watched medical education change considerably. Preclinical curricula have become shorter, lectures have moved online, virtual reality tools have entered anatomy education, and licensing examinations have repeatedly changed.

The pandemic pushed this experiment even further, demonstrating that students can learn substantial amounts of medical content remotely. Additionally, many students already supplement, or sometimes essentially replace, institutional lectures with standardized third-party resources and question banks. According to the 2022 AAMC Year 2 survey, approximately 70% of students used non-institution-created online content daily or weekly.

If students across the country are increasingly learning the same material from the same resources, it seems reasonable to ask whether we need hundreds of institutions independently building parallel versions of the same curriculum prior to embarking on clinical rotations, known as the “pre-clerkship” phase of medical school.

I realize that proposing a major restructuring of medical school is likely to provoke skepticism. Medical education is deliberately demanding, and for good reason. Patients should expect physicians to undergo rigorous training. Schools also do much more than deliver lectures. They provide mentorship, community, research opportunities, clinical skills training, and professional development.

I am not arguing that we should abandon those functions or make becoming a physician easier. I am asking whether every part of that infrastructure needs to be bundled into the same four-year institution.

One alternative might be what I call a centralized “core-to-clinic” model. In this model, students may enroll in a centralized, digital pre-clerkship curriculum after meeting basic criteria, such as a minimum MCAT score.

This curriculum would include all technical knowledge needed for U.S. Medical Licensing Examination Step 1 and should utilize a multifaceted approach to learning, including video instruction, practice questions, established medical education resources, and AI-based assistance. Students would also be required to attend occasional in-person workshops at local teaching centers that include standardized patient experiences or laboratory exercises. In order to proceed through the curriculum, students would need to take subject examinations at licensed testing centers.

Upon completion of Step 1, they would be eligible to apply (or possibly match) to a clinical site for clerkship training.

Imagine that any undergraduate student who meets predefined admissions criteria could enroll in a standardized, largely remote preclinical curriculum administered by a central organization, such as the National Board of Medical Examiners (NBME). Rather than paying traditional medical school tuition from day one, students might pay a substantially lower subscription-based fee.

This would not mean learning medicine entirely from a laptop. Anatomy exposure, standardized patient encounters, physical examination training, group cases, and other hands-on activities would still occur in person at participating medical schools, hospitals, and community sites.

Students would periodically report to these regional centers as they progressed through the centralized curriculum, with timing and location logistics handled by the central, managing organization.

A reasonable objection is that this sounds like replacing medical school with a question bank or commercial lecture resource. That is not what I am proposing. Medical knowledge is only one component of becoming a physician. But it is a component that is unusually amenable to standardization, and we already standardize much of its assessment.

Under a core-to-clinic system, anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, pharmacology, pathology, microbiology, and organ-system modules could culminate in externally administered examinations analogous to the NBME shelf exams used during clerkships. Students would need to demonstrate competency before progressing. These examinations could also identify struggling students early and provide a common measure of knowledge across the preclinical student population.

After completing the preclinical curriculum and passing Step 1, students would apply to institutions for the clinical phase of their education. At that point, schools would know much more about applicants than they do during traditional medical-school admissions. Instead of relying heavily on undergraduate GPA and MCAT performance, clinical programs could consider demonstrated performance across multiple standardized medical subject exams and in-person learning experience scores.

This structure could also make it easier to expand clinical education beyond traditional academic medical centers. Community hospitals, rural health systems, and federally qualified health centers could become dedicated clerkship sites without having to reproduce the expensive preclinical infrastructure of a medical school. That could increase the number and geographic distribution of training opportunities while creating pipelines between students and communities that need physicians.

There is another feature of this proposal that I think deserves consideration, which is an off-ramp. Students who complete the preclinical curriculum and Step 1 but decide that clinical medicine is not for them could receive a master’s degree in medicine. Someone who has demonstrated competency in medical science should not necessarily leave two years of education with substantial debt and no credential. Such graduates could pursue careers in biotechnology, consulting, regulatory affairs, medical communications, or other fields where medical knowledge is valuable. Furthermore, a three- to five-year reentry period could allow some graduates to return to clinical training later, enabling protected experimentation with other career paths.

Of course, there are good reasons to be cautious. Remote education can be isolating. Several survey studies have found that few students feel connected to their medical school or classmates during remote learning. Medical school is also where students begin developing professional identities and relationships with mentors and peers. A centralized system would have to deliberately create community rather than assume that occasional in-person sessions could replace it.

Increasingly distributed clerkship education accentuates another problem that already exists today: standardization across institutions with variable clerkship management experience. Patient populations, teaching quality, resources, and evaluations may vary considerably across clinical sites. Accreditation bodies would reasonably demand evidence that a student training at a community hospital receives an education comparable to someone at a major academic center.

From a financial perspective, a subscription model sounds cheaper, but that does not make it economically viable. Substantial costs are associated with the development of the new curriculum, administration of examinations, student support, technology maintenance, and clinical training site oversight. Before claiming meaningful savings, the model would need real cost analyses using institutional data.

In order to de-risk this transition, a small number of medical schools and community clinical sites could pilot centralized preclinical modules alongside existing curricula. Standardized preclinical examinations could be validated against Step 1 pass rates, as well as subsequent clerkship and Step 2 performance. Researchers could compare costs, student well-being, attrition, academic outcomes, and feelings of social connection. Accreditation organizations could then determine whether educational outcomes are comparable.

But the current system should not be exempt from the same scrutiny simply because it is familiar to us. Medical students assume extraordinary financial and professional commitments before they have ever cared for a patient. At the same time, technology has fundamentally changed how we acquire medical knowledge.

The question, then, is not whether medical education should remain rigorous. It clearly should. The more interesting question is whether rigor requires preserving every structural feature of the medical school we inherited. I am increasingly unconvinced that it does.

Alex Serafini, Ph.D., M.H.A., is currently a fourth-year medical student at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai with research interests in medical technology and therapeutic development, health care access, and medical education. The views here are his own and do not represent those of his institution.
