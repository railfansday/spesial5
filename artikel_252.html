Before Clavicular and other manosphere streamers were introducing young guys to the concept of breaking their facial bones to become more attractive, there was an older generation of creators building massive followings with content focused on chemical biohacking. Were it not for steroid-obsessed influencers like Anthony “Tony Huge” Hughes launching YouTube channels over a decade ago, looksmaxxing might not have become such a prominent part of the digital media landscape in 2026. These creators became internet famous by presenting themselves as peak male specimens. But their larger-than-life online personas often concealed the truth about how sinister and toxic this kind of influencing can really be.

Hughes is the gravitational center of Get Huge: Looksmaxxing to Death — Hulu’s new three-part docuseries from director Stacey Lee. The series is both a deep dive into Hughes’ strange life and a fascinating exploration of how perilous the creator economy can be in the age of manosphere and looksmaxxing influencers. Through Hughes’ ever-expanding orbit of creators, Lee is able to highlight how lonely, impressionable men are all too easily radicalized by what they see and post online. And it makes clear that all these men are trapped in a deadly psychological prison of their own making.

Get Huge takes some time to touch on Hughes’ former life as a bankruptcy lawyer and supplement salesman who was investigated by the FDA for hawking unapproved performance-enhancing drugs. But most of the series takes place in Pattaya, Thailand, where Hughes spends his days producing content showcasing his extravagant life of nonstop partying and injecting substances that swell his muscles to huge proportions. Hughes takes pride in his image as a man with access to a harem of women, and the fact that his money gives him power over many of the people around him. He presents himself as the ruler of a small kingdom — and his entire brand is built on the idea that he can show people how to become more like him.

The men caught in Hughes’ aspirational fantasy aren’t just stateside fans who’ll never reach his muscle-y heights. He is also marketing himself to other established bodybuilding influencers who might be interested in joining his inner circle by following him to Thailand. These include a menagerie of biohacking / manosphere influencers who became vital parts of Hughes’ operation over the years. Leo Longevity (legal name Laith Abdallah Algaz), Elon Muscular (his legal name isn’t revealed), and Connor Murphy all built sizable social media presences of their own. But they were each compelled to move to Pattaya and join Hughes’ entourage by a powerful desire to feel connected to something larger and more fulfilling than their individual followings.

As Get Huge features clips of Hughes’ videos — the bulk of which are now only featured on Rumble and X following multiple account bans on YouTube and TikTok — you can see that he’s trying to sell his audience on his highly specific way of life. Hughes’ own videos make it seem like he’s living his dream surrounded by people who revere him. But with each interview, Get Huge makes it clear that for all of his posturing, Hughes has been living in something of a nightmare — one where many of his disciples have died under mysterious circumstances.

As these men explain why they were drawn to Hughes, you get the sense that looksmaxxing culture is a direct response to the widespread disaffection that followed the last decade of interconnected societal shifts, from the #MeToo movement to Donald Trump’s presidencies. Hughes seems very much like an entrepreneur profiting from men’s loneliness by convincing them that their lives would be better if they were part of his community. As Get Huge first maps out Hughes’ friendships, they all appear to be anchored by an odd, sometimes envious, but genuine kind of intimacy. But as the documentary digs deeper into its subjects’ lives, it becomes hard to see them as anything but emotionally stunted men who perform cartoonish masculinity for one another in ways that are psychologically unhealthy and, at times, physically dangerous.

Part of what makes Get Huge such an unsettling watch is how relatively calm and collected Hughes and the other interviewees are as they begin to discuss how many people in their circle have died in recent years. Hughes and Murphy grow closer as friends following Algaz’s death in 2023, but they seem to bond by making Algaz the butt of the joke in content produced after his death. Simply by letting these men speak frankly about their social dynamics, Get Huge reveals the manosphere as a space in which connections built on mogging and the objectification of women can quickly deteriorate into infighting and petty jealousy. Elon Muscular readily speculates that Murphy might have had something to do with Algaz’s death because Murphy openly jokes about it. And the entire situation becomes that much stranger and more grim when Get Huge reveals that Murphy drowned shortly after the series initially wrapped filming.

Though the deaths in Hughes’ circle bring a dark whodunit energy to Get Huge, the documentary emphasizes how much peril comes as a part of existing in the looksmaxxing space. Rather than trying to solve the mystery of how Algaz died, the docuseries presents his story as a cautionary tale. He’s an object lesson in how this lifestyle can warp a person’s perception of reality in a way that ultimately cuts them off from the kind of help they really need.

What might be most disturbing about Get Huge is the way it ends. The series follows Hughes to a youth bodybuilding event where he talks up his less-than-legal products to a new group of boys — some as young as 13. To keep his momentum going, Hughes has to appeal to a new, younger audience that doesn’t understand how everything about looksmaxxing is a performance. Though the docuseries gives you the distinct impression that people like Murphy and Algaz felt a true belonging with Hughes, its closing shots call into question how genuine any of his connections with other bodybuilders really are. By juxtaposing the deaths of Hughes’ adult acolytes with his focus on turning children into fans, Get Huge paints a clear picture of how the manosphere’s promise of emotional connection often ends in a twisted kind of exploitation. And that extractive energy feels foundational to a culture that runs on people turning every aspect of their lives into content.

All three episodes of Get Huge: Looksmaxxing to Death are now streaming on Hulu and Disney Plus.
