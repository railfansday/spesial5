From 3DGS to printable meshes: a multi-model case study

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Affiliation and writing disclosure: This case study is shared on behalf of the Mugen3D team and is based on the team's original write-up, input images, and recorded model demonstrations. AI tools assisted the English translation, editing, and adaptation.

A 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) asset can look complete in a viewer while still requiring substantial work before it becomes a physical model. The useful distinction is between a representation that produces rendered images and a geometric surface that meets printing requirements.

This case study follows a mesh-based workflow across animals, a portrait bust, and a stylized character. The sequence is 3DGS → mesh conversion → surface inspection and repair → appearance preparation → full-color printing.

Other fabrication routes exist. For example, print3dgs describes placing color within transparent resin, and DreamPrinting studies the mapping from radiance representations to voxel-level printing materials. The mesh conversion described here belongs to this particular workflow; it is not a requirement for every way of printing a radiance-based asset.

Animals, a portrait bust, and a stylized character as full-color physical prints.

The examples include animals, portrait busts, and stylized characters. They differ in silhouette, local structure, and color, making it useful to examine the same workflow across several kinds of input.

Starting with image-derived 3DGS assets can reduce the work involved in building an entire subject and recreating its details by hand. It still leaves decisions about geometry, repair, color, and printing. The input image remains a reference for the visual features that need to carry through to the result.

For the animals, the features to examine include the lion's layered mane and the horse's flowing mane, outward-projecting tips, and thin, sheet-like sections. These are structures whose shapes and gaps can change during surface extraction, repair, or mesh decimation.

The portrait bust brings geometry and appearance into the same comparison. Floral hair ornaments, the bun, and collar edges have physical contours. The red-and-gold patterns on the collar and the lip color involve the correspondence of surface color and texture as well.

The stylized character provides another set of checks: the teeth along the dinosaur hood, the white locks of hair, eye colors, and green clothing. Keeping these structural and color features recognizable matters alongside the overall silhouette.

These are qualitative visual comparisons. Visible detail in a GIF does not establish a numerical manufacturing tolerance or submillimeter accuracy, and the examples do not provide a controlled benchmark against other systems.

A conventional mesh explicitly represents a surface with vertices, edges, and faces. 3DGS renders Gaussian primitives with properties such as position, shape, orientation, and color. Those primitives do not directly form a connected, closed surface.

After conversion, an untextured mesh may look different from the rendered asset. It may contain cracks, holes, overly thin sheet-like structures, unwanted connections between nearby parts, or lost surface detail. Surface extraction remains a research topic; SuGaR is one example of work on that problem, rather than a specification of the conversion implementation used in this case study.

A single input image also leaves the back and occluded areas unseen. Those parts of the generated result are inferred. Checking side and rear views can reveal whether the completed model is coherent, but it does not turn inferred information into captured ground truth.

After surface extraction, inspect the overall structure and local surfaces for cracks, holes, thin sheets, and unwanted connections. The model must be checked against the relevant printing requirements.

Address defects introduced during conversion, using the original image and the 3DGS asset as references for the features that should remain recognizable. The priorities vary by subject: repairing a thin or protruding element may also change its shape, so structural repair and detail preservation need to be considered together.

Once the geometry has been processed, preparation includes mesh decimation, color and texture processing, and baking as required by the print workflow. Appearance preparation and geometric repair address different problems: matching a color pattern does not close a hole or restore the contour of a thin structure.

The practical takeaway is to review the transition between representations explicitly: what looks complete in a renderer, what survives as geometry, what remains in the appearance data, and what is visible in the physical print. Those checks are especially useful when the input is a single image and some viewpoints must be inferred.

The distinction you draw is the whole story: a 3DGS render is a picture of a surface, not a surface. The lion's mane and the horse's flowing mane are brutal test cases for exactly that reason — thin, layered, high-frequency geometry is where surface extraction invents cracks and holes. What I'm most curious about is the occluded side: a single input image leaves the entire back of the subject unseen, so the mesh there has to come from somewhere. Do you hallucinate it with a multi-view diffusion pass, mirror the visible side, or hand-sculpt it during the repair stage? And after the first physical print, how often does the turntable inspection send a model back for a second repair pass — is the rework rate meaningfully different for the bust versus the animals?
