WhatsApp doesn't translate calls. Here's what does, and where your audio goes

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Posted on Nov 26 • Originally published at ai-translator.dudko.dev

If you're trying to talk to a partner's parents, or your own, on WhatsApp and you don't share a language, the short answer is that WhatsApp won't help with the call itself. It translates messages. Calls happen in whatever language people speak.

There are ways around that, and they differ more than the vendor pages make it look. The thing that differs most is what the person on the other end has to do, and the thing almost nobody mentions is where the call audio ends up.

No. WhatsApp's own announcement from September 23, 2025 covers messages: long-press, tap Translate, and it works "for 1:1 chats, groups, and Channel updates too". Android can also translate a whole chat automatically. The translation runs on your phone, "where WhatsApp cannot see them". Calls aren't in the post, and as of this week WhatsApp hasn't announced call translation anywhere we could find.

So everything below is something you add on top.

Most of the tools fall into one of three designs. The design decides more than the brand does.

1. Captions on your screen. You take the call in WhatsApp on your computer, and an app listens to it and shows a live translation as text. JotMe's guide (August 2026) works this way, as does SonicMeet, which shows bilingual captions for WhatsApp calls on Windows and Mac. You read; the other person hears your voice as it is. JotMe says its free plan "typically includes 20 minutes of monthly translation", and it describes sharing translation minutes with a code so guests can view the translations too.

Good for: you understand them, and you can answer in their language, or someone on their side can.

2. A link that moves the call. You send a link in the WhatsApp chat, and both of you join a translated call in the browser instead. That's Bridgecall's approach ("Keep the chat in WhatsApp. Move the call to a link that translates.") and AI Call's Call Links. The other person installs nothing, but they do leave WhatsApp and open a link. Whisperr mixes the two: it listens on your device, and a "Broadcast Mode" room link lets the other person read or hear your words in their language in a browser.

Good for: an older relative who will tap a link in a chat but won't install anything, and doesn't mind it not being a normal WhatsApp call.

3. A translator under the call. The call stays a normal WhatsApp call, and the translation happens between the call and your ears and mouth. The other person doesn't open or install anything. On a phone that's Samsung's Live Translate; on a Mac it's what AI Translator does. More on both below.

We aren't comparing translation quality between any of these. None of them publishes a number you could hold against another, and we haven't measured them. A five-minute test call with someone patient will tell you more than any page will.

If your phone is a recent Samsung Galaxy, start here. Samsung's support page says Live Translate works on select phones and tablets with Android 14 and One UI 6.1 or higher, and that it's available in "some third-party apps, such as WhatsApp, Facebook Messenger, Instagram, Signal", among others.

The path from that page: Settings > Galaxy AI > Call assist > Live translate in other apps, turn on WhatsApp, then during a call swipe down to Quick settings and tap Live translate. You'll see the translation on screen and hear it, and you can pick the languages and voice for yourself and the other person.

Samsung adds one warning worth keeping: "Only one person should use Live Translate at a time, otherwise translation errors will occur." So if both of you have Galaxy phones, decide who runs it.

On an iPhone, Apple documents Live Translation for the Phone app, FaceTime and Messages. We found nothing from Apple or WhatsApp saying it reaches WhatsApp calls, so don't count on it there.

If you take your calls in the WhatsApp app for Mac, a translator can sit in the audio path. AI Translator installs two virtual audio devices, AI Translator Speaker and AI Translator Microphone. WhatsApp sees them as an ordinary speaker and mic, the same way Zoom, FaceTime or Teams would.

What we'd pick for talking to family: the on-device engine, if your Mac is on macOS 26 and your language pair is covered. It's free, needs no key, and it's turn-by-turn, which suits a conversation where people take turns anyway. If you need it to keep pace with someone who talks fast and long, use a cloud engine, knowing the audio goes to OpenAI or Google under your own key.

The site gives language counts, not a list, so check your exact pair in the app before the call that matters.

WhatsApp says its Mac app keeps your calls private "with end-to-end encryption". That's true, and it's about the trip between devices. To play the call to you, your device has to decrypt it. Whatever translates the call works on that decrypted audio, and then it's wherever the translator runs.
