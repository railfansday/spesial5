Condensed Milk Bread Rolls

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Recipe by Kallee Krong-McCreery, Escondido, California

These milk bread rolls are enriched with condensed milk, which makes them fluffy and tender with a touch of sweetness.

Baking bread is one of my biggest culinary joys. It feels magical that a handful of pantry ingredients can create a supple dough, which then transforms into tender, browned bread just begging for a slathering of butter. Whether I’m making sourdough French bread or homemade dinner rolls, the process is mostly the same, but a few small ingredient changes can give a recipe a distinct identity. In these condensed milk bread rolls, that small change is a can of sweetened condensed milk.

This recipe was inspired by Brazilian milk buns (pão de leite condensado) and Portuguese sweet bread, which have sweetened condensed milk added to the dough for flavor and a sugary edge. Most of these recipes use a starter technique called tangzhong, where a cooked flour paste is kneaded into the dough to create a fluffy, cloud-like texture similar to that of Japanese milk bread. We simplified the process for our buns, skipping the starter so they come together without advanced planning.

In a small bowl, dissolve the yeast in warm water. In a large bowl, combine the milk, eggs, butter, sugar, vanilla, salt, yeast mixture and 1-1/2 cups of flour. Beat the mixture on medium speed for three minutes until it’s smooth. Stir in enough of the remaining flour to form a soft dough.

Turn the dough onto a floured surface and knead it until it’s smooth and elastic, six to eight minutes. Place the dough in a greased bowl, turning once to grease the top. Cover the bowl and let the dough rise in a warm place until it has doubled in size, about one hour.

Editor’s Tip: When kneading, start with a light dusting of flour, adding more only as needed. It’s normal for dough enriched with eggs and butter to be tacky initially (it eases as you knead and develop gluten), but adding too much flour will make the bread dry.

Punch down the dough. Turn it out onto a lightly floured surface, then divide and shape it into 12 balls. Place the dough balls in a greased 13×9-inch baking pan. Preheat the oven to 350°. Meanwhile, cover the pan with a kitchen towel and let the rolls rise in a warm place until they have almost doubled in size, 45 to 60 minutes longer.

Editor’s Tip: For evenly-sized rolls, divide the dough by weight using a kitchen scale. The best kitchen scales offer measurements in grams and ounces, so you can divvy up the dough easily no matter which system you prefer.

Bake the rolls until they’re lightly browned, 15 to 20 minutes. Cover the pan loosely with foil if the tops brown too quickly.

Since these rolls are yeast-based and require two rises, you have to make them at least a few hours ahead of time, but you can stretch that to the day before. Make the dough as directed, but cover and refrigerate them after shaping and placing them in the baking dish. The next day, let the rolls warm and rise at room temperature before baking them as directed.

You can freeze baked condensed milk bread rolls for up to three months in an airtight, freezer-proof food storage container. Thaw them in the refrigerator, then warm them in a low oven, around 300°, until they’re soft.

Dense or dry rolls are usually the result of underproofing, overbaking or adding too much flour. Proofing gives the yeast time to lift the heavy dough, weaving air into it for a light texture. If the dough doesn’t rise enough, the lack of air produces rolls with a tight, firm crumb. Use the time cues in this recipe as a guide—proofing may take longer in a chilly kitchen, or go faster on a hot day.

Overbaked rolls can be dry or hard because the extended oven time pulls out their moisture. Since ovens vary, set a timer and check them a few minutes early—even three to four extra minutes can make a big difference. They could also be dry if you knead in too much flour, or if you didn’t measure your flour correctly from the start. Extra flour will hydrate as it proofs, sucking up the liquid that would otherwise contribute to tender rolls.

You can tell yeast is active when a creamy, foamy layer forms on the water’s surface a few minutes after mixing it with warm water. It looks like frothy, milky coffee! Although you don’t always have to proof yeast depending on the type and recipe, proofing active dry yeast gives you visual confirmation that it’s viable. Sometimes the yeast can die off even before the expiration date; proofing checks for that. Once the dough is mixed, you’ll see active yeast at work as it starts to expand, rise and relax the dough.

These lightly sweetened rolls are versatile and can be served for breakfast or brunch with your favorite frittata, crispy bacon and a bowl of fruit salad, or with afternoon tea or dinner. The flavor of the condensed milk—especially as it caramelizes on the roll’s tops—would pair especially well with pork chop dinners, which often feature a sweet ingredient. I also want to try them as a twist on croissant ice cream sandwiches in place of the flaky French pastry.

This recipe doesn’t use a whole can of sweetened condensed milk, but don’t toss the rest. Put it to work in other sweetened condensed milk recipes, or try something new and shake up a Vietnamese coffee martini. Better yet, serve condensed milk rolls with the martini for a happy hour to remember.
