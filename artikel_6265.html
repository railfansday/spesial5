Garages and unfinished basements are often poorly insulated, making them prone to extreme cold in fall and winter. A garage heater can turn this typically frigid space into a warm, practical area for woodworking projects, vehicle maintenance, and more. If you plan to spend time in your garage this winter tackling projects at your workbench, raising the ambient air temperature is a must.

Garage heaters come in various styles, though the most popular models are electric and propane. Electric heaters are convenient and easy to install if a power outlet is nearby. Propane heaters are more portable and can cover more surface area. Natural gas garage heaters are also powerful options for larger garages and can offer long-term heating solutions.

Heaters can benefit not only comfort but also safety. Cold temps can cause condensation to collect on tools and stored items, which may lead to rust and other damage. Wintry environments can also cause car engines and batteries to wear out faster. A well-heated garage protects your investments and provides a warmer, safer place to work.

There’s a lot to consider when using a heater to keep your garage safe and comfortable. So we picked the brain of Pat Porzio, a mechanical engineer and multi-licensed tradesman (electrical, plumbing, HVAC). He’s also the HVAC manager for Russo Brothers in East Hanover, New Jersey. We also spoke with David Shapiro, an electrician, author, and expert on old house wiring who contributes to electrical product and installation standards.

Both agree on one thing: Your overarching concern in heating a garage is safety. The first words out of both their mouths were identical: “Pull a permit.” That is, if you plan to use a permanently installed electric or gas-fired garage heater, you need to file a building permit for the work—whether you do the work yourself or hire a contractor. Permitted installations are inspected by the town or other authority with jurisdiction over this work where you live, which increases the likelihood of a safe installation.

Aside from pulling a building permit for the electrical work, Shapiro steers homeowners away from any heater without a UL (or other safety-related U.S. testing agency) listing.

Using a 120-volt space heater is simpler; same goes for small propane-fired and kerosene heaters. You don’t need a building permit to use one of these in a garage. Still, there are safe and unsafe ways to use these heaters. For more on that topic, check out our garage heater safety tips below.

As we mentioned earlier, there are a couple of different types of garage heaters. Electric heaters are easier to install, don't require a vent, and are generally safer. But depending on your local electricity rates, they can be more expensive to run. Gas heaters use natural gas or propane, and they can be a more cost-effective option for heating larger garages. They require ventilation, which can be a pain if you don't already have the right setup.

Infrared heaters heat objects rather than the air, so they can be a good companion for localized projects at your workbench. Forced-air heaters use a fan to distribute heat quickly and work well for large spaces, but they require good insulation to run efficiently and may kick up dust around your workshop.

For most garages, an electric heater is often the most viable option because it's easy to install, safe, and effective. But if you have a larger or poorly insulated garage, a gas heater might be the way to go.

“A bigger heater is not necessarily better,” Porzio says. “An oversized heater will short cycle. That is, it will turn on and off too frequently. It satisfies heating demand very quickly and abruptly turns off. A heater that is too small will do just the opposite. It will run and run and never fulfill the heating demand, eventually wearing itself out.”

Before contractors install a boiler or furnace, they perform a series of calculations, called a Manual J, to determine the correct size. While homeowners can get a rough estimate by multiplying the area's square footage by 50 to get the required BTUs, Porzio recommends using simplified Manual J forms online or a sizing tool like those on King-Electric.com and Newair.com.

For reference, a two-car garage will need a heater ranging from 30,000-50,000 BTUs to reach a consistent, comfortable temperature.

Whether you attempt the electrical work or hire it out, Shapiro has two more recommendations. First, don’t install the heater so that a power cord leading to a machine or tool could be draped directly in front of it. The heat can damage the cord.

Second, consider installing a CO alarm in the garage. A CO alarm can make the garage a safer place to work, especially if you’re heating it with gas, and it can also protect the occupants of the house.
