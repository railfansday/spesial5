At the Broad, Six Decades of Art Question the Myth of Progress

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Two weeks after the 1992 Los Angeles uprising, Los Angeles Times reporter Janny Scott published an essay confronting her own complacency toward the city’s stark inequalities. For years, she’d witnessed the chasm widen between women carrying $3,500 handbags and those wearing trash bags; from her “nice neighborhood where the helicopters don’t come,” she did little to address the disparity. Still, the unrest that followed the acquittal of four LAPD officers charged with beating Rodney King seemed to have broken through the collective apathy. Angelenos, she wrote, were suddenly talking about “things that matter”: race, guilt, poverty, urban policy, to name a few. More than three decades later, artist Guadalupe Rosales found a copy of the newspaper containing Scott’s article on eBay. The headlines “Seeing Ourselves” and “Has the Myth Been Shattered?” registered the same discrepancy she sensed between what she remembered as a child growing up in East LA and how the press perpetuated myths about the city’s seeming collectivity. Rosales was 11 years old in 1992, and she has a distinct memory of her mother rushing the family home from school as smoke stained the usually sun-drenched California sky. As an adult, Rosales has amassed an archive of clippings, photographs, and other ephemera of Chicanx youth culture—much of it crowdsourced through social media—that serves as both a bulwark against that dissonance and a counter to historical erasure and amnesia. “I’m not looking for answers,” she told ARTnews. “But for some kind of physical proof that things really happened, that they’re not just in our memories.”

Rosales’s 2024 sculpture Has the myth been shattered encases that infamous yellowed newspaper in a nearly six-foot-tall aluminum frame fitted with a two-way mirror, illuminated by colorful LED lights. Though it resembles a doorway, the work offers no passage; instead, viewers face their own reflections superimposed over the 1992 text, along with photographs of palm trees and police. The temporal distance between then and now collapses onto a single plane, leaving Scott’s question perpetually suspended in the present.

The work also lends its title to a just-opened exhibition at the Broad in downtown LA. Organized by assistant curator Jen Vanegas Rocha, the presentation brings together works by more than a dozen artists spanning six decades. Recent acquisitions by Rosales and Hank Willis Thomas appear alongside art by Andy Warhol, Barbara Kruger, Glenn Ligon, and Robert Longo, among others. Across prints, paintings, drawings, sculpture, and tapestry, artists appropriate text and images from various forms of public record, drawing attention to the ways media shapes perception of major sociopolitical events. Seen together, the works undermine the assumption that exposing injustice inevitably leads to redress. “There are echoes of the present in the past,” Willis Thomas said. “So is the past present or is the present past?”

The Broad’s founding director, Joanne Heyler, sees that unease as central to the exhibition’s timeliness. “The notion of a linear progression of change unfolding in a dependable, escalating way,” she said, is “becoming harder and harder to believe in.”

Consider the oldest work in the show, Andy Warhol’s Race Riot (1963), in which police dogs attack Black civil-rights demonstrators in Birmingham, Alabama. In 2026, the decades-old image calls to mind the police response to the 2020 protests following the murder of George Floyd, as well as the more recent pro-Palestine and anti-ICE demonstrations nationwide, in which protestors were often battered and tear-gassed. Warhol lifted the photograph directly from a visual essay by Charles Moore in Life magazine, translating the confrontation into a monochromatic silkscreen. Adopting the incendiary rhetoric of contemporary news coverage, he labeled the peaceful demonstrators “rioters.” Race Riot reflects the artist’s own reckoning with the contradictions at the heart of American life: His obsession with celebrities and mass consumption give way to the sensationalized images of death and violence that belie it, like the mangled cars and electric chairs that also feature in his “Death and Disaster” series. Viewers are left to consider whether the relentless circulation of violence sharpens public outrage or gradually dulls our empathy.

For Willis Thomas, Warhol’s Birmingham silkscreen paintings function as “postcards to the future.” By transforming them from documentation to works of art, Warhol has made a case for their continued relevance. Nearby, Willis Thomas’s photo-based Black Survival Guide, or How to Live Through a Police Riot (2018) revisits the same era to recover another history largely omitted from public memory. While researching the aftermath of Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination, he discovered a typewritten pamphlet offering practical advice to Black residents of Wilmington, Delaware, during the nine-month National Guard occupation in 1968. Onto retroreflective vinyl, Willis Thomas printed excerpts from the pamphlet—advice on how to staunch bleeding or communicate during a power outage, for example—atop archival News Journal photographs of confrontations between armed soldiers and the city’s Black communities. Installed in a darkened gallery, the black-and-white images are invisible until a flash of light illuminates them. Rather than merely encountering the past, the audience actively aids in its recovery. “In some ways the observers become the researchers,” he said.

Histories of state-sanctioned violence and resistance movements similarly converge across a nearly eleven-foot-wide canvas in Mickalene Thomas’s 2021 mixed-media painting The Charnel House (Resist #5). Reworking Picasso’s 1944–45 masterpiece of corpses heaped beneath a table made in response to news footage of liberated Nazi concentration camps, Thomas collages photographs and placards from the Civil Rights and Black Lives Matter movements with the modernist’s fragmented anatomical forms. A poster advertising the Black Panther Party’s free breakfast program appears beside a portrait of Angela Davis and the names of Freddie Gray and Alton Sterling, both killed during encounters with police. Here, grief and trauma are paired with resistance and community building.

The language of dissent assumes a subtler register in Ligon’s Narratives (1993), a suite of nine sepia-toned etchings that mimic the ornate typography and florid prose of 19th-century slave narratives. Ligon replaces accounts of enslavement and escape with details from his early education at a predominantly white private school, his desire for other men, and the circumstances of his career as an artist. In his hands, a literary form that freed Black people used to demonstrate their humanity becomes a medium for contemporary self-portraiture and self-determination.

Museums, of course, too, play a role in shaping public records. Over the past five years, the Broad has acquired work by 26 artists previously unrepresented in its collection, many of whom are of a younger generation and, like Rosales, actively working in LA. Heyler sees these acquisitions as part of an ongoing effort to better reflect the multiplicity of the communities that surround the institution. “We’re not telling one story but many,” she said. “We’re always striving to strengthen the connective tissues between them.”

The exhibition offers an opportunity to showcase many of the newly acquired artists and to extend an art-historical conversation that Heyler traces to exhibitions like “A Forest of Signs: Art in the Crisis of Representation,” a landmark 1989 survey at the Museum of Contemporary Art, just across the street. Bringing together works by 30 American artists, from Jeff Koons to Jenny Holzer, that show examined the unstable boundaries between reality and representation in an increasingly media-saturated society. But the terrain has evolved, Heyler noted. Where artists of that period often exposed the ideologies concealed by authoritative and commercial imagery, audiences today, immersed in social media and generative AI, navigate what she called a “stew of falsehoods and truths.”

Even so, curator Vanegas Rocha hopes her exhibition inspires something more generative than cynicism, in which artists can be seen as the “architects of better futures.” She locates the potential for systemic change in the power of an individual aesthetic encounter. “It can teach you about the world, about others, about yourself,” she said. “That single moment can have a ripple effect, leading to a collective difference.” Rosales, likewise, hasn’t relinquished hope. Citing José Esteban Muñoz’s 2009 queer theory book Cruising Utopia, she described hope as an ever-receding horizon that we must continue to endeavor toward. “We are a people that don’t give up,” she said, noting that her mother also took her to protests from an early age. “Standing up for each other, even when you don’t see it right away, does pay off.” Progress, she suggests, may not be linear or inevitable, but that doesn’t make it an illusion. In her mirror, the unresolved question—“Has the Myth Been Shattered?”—is cast back onto the people who might yet determine the answer.
