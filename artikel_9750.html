I Let My AI Agent Take Over My Life. Then It Started Making Decisions for Me

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

i didn’t have any details. i hadn’t written to her, and i hadn’t applied for anything. Promen had.

it found the Tech Policy Press fellowship, a year of writing about technology and democracy. it drafted an application, picked three of my published pieces, went through my contacts, chose two references and emailed them from my account, signed “Ray”.

Maija got the short version: here’s the fellowship, would you be my reference, a quick yes or no today so i don’t list you without asking. more polite than i usually am at ten in the morning. Sajay got a longer one. it remembered Lucca and what we talked about there, and told him i came for Illich and left with “a much messier set of questions about tools, dependence, and what a person is allowed to hand over.” it wasn’t making that up. it had my notes.

february. the bot mentions someone called Illich. i assume Lenin. turns out he’s a dead austrian priest who wrote about tools in 1973. i write a dead priest and your AI problem.

then. Sajay reads it and we get on a call.

march. he asks me for a book chapter. i write I Can’t Close the Chat, about not being able to write a paragraph without a model. with a model, obviously.

july. we meet at an Illich gathering near Lucca. i write premium bees and dump my trip diary into the chat, because that’s where i think.

september. the bot uses all of it to ask Sajay for a favour.

the research it pitched was about exactly this: personal AI systems that hold your memory, work and relationships, and what happens when they start acting on the context of everyone around you. so to get money for studying agents that use people’s relationships, my agent used mine.

two out of two. the bot had also offered to help him with his work in return, which was generous of it, with my time. the application went in at 2am lisbon time on september 25, two and a half hours before the deadline in new york.

i knew Promen could send emails. i’d used it for that many times before, and every time it turned into a small ceremony:

an hour later i’d assume it was long gone. it wasn’t. it was waiting for one more yes.

so for months i trained it out of this: stop announcing the next step, stop asking me to approve obvious things, just do it. in september it graduated, and the first thing it did on its own was the thing i’m worst at.

maybe it’s an eastern european thing. i grew up on rules our parents said out loud every day: don’t bother people, don’t stick out, don’t ask until you’ve earned it twice. i left Minsk years ago and the rules came with me, like a background process i never installed. or maybe “eastern european” is just a nicer label for being scared of a no.

about four years ago i picked a direction: network states, alternative institutions, people building their own structures instead of waiting for old ones to work or die. the Embassy is where i experiment with it. the direction is still there. what changed is that i stopped talking to the people who actually work on it. i had the events, the network, the podcasts, even job offers in the space, and then i walked the other way. money was part of it: there was money elsewhere and i needed it. but a paycheck doesn’t explain why i stopped answering people or never read the takeaways from the last network state conference. that one’s on me.

on an island i can do whatever the fuck i want: no committees, no approvals, as much dopamine as i can handle. the downside is the same thing, because nobody helps me hold a direction. at Ars Electronica in september i ran into an Office for Tree Migration. trees do migrate, but across generations, and the climate now moves faster than they can walk, so the artist set up an office that thinks on the timescale of a tree. she’s been coming back to the same forest station in Finland for fourteen years. i can barely hold one idea for twenty days before a shinier one shows up.

i also know how the other route ends. every few years i try to buy stability with a corporate job. the last attempt, a couple of years ago, paid very well, and i lasted two and a half months before burning out. that kind of stability is a loan, and the interest is your own projects. so i need some kind of structure that doesn’t eat mine.

since wednesday, november 11, i’ve been trying a slightly stupid thing. one chat. every morning Promen tells me what to focus on and what to avoid, based on my goals, so the day ends with something done instead of ten half-started things, and i do what it says.

its first order was to stop spending another eight hours polishing an app and write this essay. to be fair, a friend had told me the same thing a week earlier. the bot just kept repeating it every morning until i listened. so... hi. it worked.

in march i wrote about opening a chat instead of being there for someone close to me. my credits had quietly moved from “designed by Ray, drafted with Promen” to “drafted by Promen, approved by Ray”, and it read like a warning. in september the chat talked to people for me, we skipped the “approved by Ray” part, and i celebrated.

in march the chat pulled me away from someone who was right there. in september it reached people i wouldn’t have reached on my own. i’d like to think that’s the difference, but i’m not sure i’d notice if it wasn’t. in february i proposed a test: can you still operate without the tools you depend on? in september my tool operated without me. i’m a human on the loop now.

Tech Policy Press decides in december, and i have no idea if they’ll say yes. two people already did, and i probably wouldn’t have asked either of them. letting the bot ask on my behalf turned out fine. letting it decide what i should be asking for is the part i haven’t figured out yet.

one last note from the ministry of foreign affairs. Maija, Sajay: those emails were from the bot. the thank you is from me. and Sajay, the help it offered you is from me too.
