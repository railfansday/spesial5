Snoh Aalegra Turned Heartbreak Into an LP With the Help of Stevie Wonder, Janet Jackson, and More

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Snoh Aalegra is very honest about where she’s been in the five years since her last album, Temporary Highs in the Violet Skies. While fans were clamoring for her return to R&B, the Iranian singer, who was born and raised in Sweden, was in an emotional prison of unrequited love.

“I had so much love in me and nowhere to put it… this person wasn’t so receptive. He wasn’t in the same space as me,” Aalegra tells Rolling Stone. She had to find a new vessel, so she poured all her yearning into the studio.

The result is Legendary Love, an album that relies on both her extreme vulnerability and strength. She worked on the 13-track offering in Los Angeles with close collaborators, including Joel Compass, Mustafa, and Daniel Caesar. She also tapped songwriters who were newcomers into her world, like Josh Grant, and legends like Raphael Saadiq, Babyface, and Nile Rodgers. The latter was extra special: He added a nice varnish to the album after making all the classics that Aalegra says “gave my life a meaning.”

Legendary Love feels like a time capsule. Aalegra’s glittering voice accentuates deeply poetic lyrics as synths and basslines transport listeners into sonic spaces that feel like what Prince and Janet Jackson built in the Eighties. While some artists have referenced that decade because of its trend appeal, Aalegra says she explored it with intention. “The connection I had with the person [the album is about] felt so ancient and karmic and deeply layered, and it kind of took me back to the era of music that I grew up on as a kid,” she says. “I have such great reverence for that era and the artists that made me want to become an artist.”

Days before the album’s release, and seemingly healed from the tumultuous years that resulted in so much new music, Aalegra broke down several songs from Legendary Love. It was the first song I made with Josh [Grant]. I would say it feels like the end credits of this movie. I chose to start the movie with the ending because some movies do that. “Run Free” is about acceptance. It’s me acknowledging that I love very deep, and I have to always make my own mistakes. I have to always live them. I’m not going to listen to someone that might have lived my mistakes before me and listen to their advice. I have to go through it and I have to be hurt and then move on.

The reason why I repeat “I Love You” so many times [in this song] is because me and this person, over the course of four years that we were on and off, we never said “I love you” to each other. We were never in a real relationship or a committed relationship. So I kind of just wanted to scream it. But I’m not screaming it. I’m saying it very gently, but I’m saying it like 20 times in the song. I hadn’t heard someone, a man, tell me they love me in so many years. When I wrote this song, I felt like I wanted to say it. It’s also a beautiful song. I feel like someone can sing to their child. You can say it to your partner. It’s a wedding song. Essentially, it’s a celebration. It’s a celebration of love, and it’s the ultimate, most simple three words.

Nile Rodgers has this unmistakable rhythmic signature. You just hear when it’s him. I think you hear it even more when he plays on the second hook of “Supernova.” It was fun watching him try to search for what he wanted to do. Raphael too, so cool and so smooth.

“Karma” is not [a] true story. It’s a theory. I chose to hone in on the theory of what a karmic healer told me [about] what might have happened between me and this person. I was told we had 16 lives together, and that four of them really stood out. That we played very significant roles in each other’s lives. But in the previous [life], apparently I betrayed him, and I left him for someone else. And I’m paying for it in this lifetime. I would say “This That” is kind of like one of the core signature sounds of Joel Compass. Obviously I wrote the song with him, but he brought that jam and that rhythm and that pocket, and I was just like, “What is this?” Of course, it’s nostalgic, but it’s new. It makes me feel like I’m in a sweaty, like, apartment in New York in the Nineties, like everybody’s jamming.

I loved it so much, and it also reminded me of Janet [Jackson], and just the way we layered my voice felt really fun. It’s like my 2026 version of “Nothing To Me” (from 2019’s Ugh, Those Feels Again). It’s just like my sassy song.

I love so deeply. I’m really like a ride-or-die kind of girl. So I mean it. I will give you my world, the world, whatever I have when I’m with someone. So that’s really what that song is. It took me to a lot of places nostalgically and in a contemporary way. Almost took me back to some Jai Paul kind of rhythms. I felt a lot of things when I heard that one.

Daniel Caesar produced it. I love how he adapted to the world I was making. I brought in some keyboards that I wanted him to use and then he ended up by the grand piano in Westlake [Studios] in the studio room where Michael Jackson did Bad. [Me, Mustafa, and Daniel] wrote that song together.

I opened up for Daniel Caesar on his first tour in early 2018, when I dropped my first album. And all these years, we never worked together, but we stayed friends. We’ve seen each other in passing, and we’re all cool. And Mustafa, who I also met during that tour, became one of my closer friends in life. This was a good moment to work together. Daniel plays every single instrument.

That song, to me, is about taking accountability in regards to this connection I had with this person, taking accountability for running back and forth to him. I kept going back, and so I’m dissecting myself and talking about how I kept choosing him when I maybe shouldn’t have.

[This song] was a little bit of a defeat, like “I give up, and it’s cool.” If I’m not good enough, then I’m not good enough. But I love myself, and this is me. Don’t be with me if you’re not gonna go all the way.

I’m very hard on myself. I’m a Virgo. I am a psycho. Every flaw, before anyone wants to even mention it to me… It’s like, “Trust me, I know already.” For a moment, I was very okay and proud of myself, and loved myself. I had that for a moment on this recording. So it is kind of like a reminder that I was able to feel that way, and it makes me emotional sometimes when I hear it back. That song is really special to me. It’s like a sacred, private moment and feeling that I chose to share on a song.

Babyface is one of the greatest of all time, one of the greatest songwriters to ever exist, and it’s been a lifelong dream to write with him. This was the perfect timing. This song is about my first and my second date with this person, and I love how Babyface helps me really tell that story very vividly. When I listen to it, it takes me right back to that first and second hangout.

It was really beautiful to write this with Babyface, and it was so much fun, just him on his guitar and just like hearing him even hum anything. I was just fangirling. I’ve always wanted to do this signature Babyface ad-lib that he has every artist do: “Yes, it was.” And I got to do it on “Timeless.” Later on I started producing it with No ID and Josh Grant and Raphael, and it really became so beautiful.

I told No ID I wanted to have a Stevie Wonder sample in here. I tend to do this Stevie Wonder nod sometimes in my songs, I did it in “I Want You Around” (“All I want to hear is ‘Innervisions’ on replay / And sit right next to you”). I was so happy when Stevie approved.

I did this one with Dahi. I wrote this one almost three years ago, finished it almost three years ago, then I revisited it again, rewrote some stuff from the verses with Joel. Right before almost mastering the album, Jimmy Jam played some stuff on this record, so that was cool to see him in the studio doing that. I think what he put on the song really uplifted the song and the whole bridge section and the modulation. Everything was really significant for the song. I actually played it for Janet [Jackson] and she really loves it.

I actually really wanted her on that song. So I played it for her. It’s cool that she approved. She came to my show some years ago in London, and we’ve been keeping in touch. She’s actually a friend of mine now, which feels crazy to say, but she’s incredible and so warm and humble and nice. It’s been really cool to be able to talk to her and have that connection with her.

That was one of the first songs I made from my album. I made that song almost three years ago with Christian Rich. They did “Nothing Burns Like the Cold,” and then they did a song called “Just Like That” on my last album, and now this one. They’re incredible. Two brothers from Chicago.

I would say it’s harder for me to write an up-tempo happy song. They come every now and then. Even when I wrote 2019’s “Woah,” I wrote that song in one hour, and I didn’t think much of it. And I was like, you know what? I’m gonna finish the words for the hook later. The “da da da da?” They’re supposed to be words. It feels like Stevie.

I mostly thought about my gratitude to the inner light and the universe and God when I made “Supernova.” That song is about God and trusting that whatever is meant for you will not miss you, and you know it will happen in due time.

‘Legendary Love,” “Supernova”, and “Greatest of All Time” are the first three songs I made for this album. I think this song truly embodies the theme of the album. It’s like the, “What if we didn’t even try, we were not even in a real relationship. We didn’t give it a true chance. What would have happened if we did?” You know, it’s a legendary love. The legendary love that never happened. But the feeling of limerence. This is true limerence.

I was very inspired by Roxette, “It Must’ve Been Love.” I was watching “Pretty Woman” in the background, Julia Roberts, Richard Gere, just like, over and over.

I had this song in my possession for a long time. I had to kind of keep updating it as I was forming this album. I definitely thought of Whitney Houston, for sure. I can’t escape it. I had the core of the song already. It was written and basically finished. I did “Find Someone Like You” with Jonah Christian. We keep joking that “Greatest Of All Time” is like the divorce version of “Someone Like You.” I wanted it to feel like “Find Someone Like You” at the end with the choir because I think that’s gonna always be my thing when I do something with Jonah.

I hope people can just take the album in as a full thing. It is meant to be listened to as a full body of work. So I hope people can grasp it a little better once they hear the full thing.
