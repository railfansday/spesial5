'Star Trek: Deep Space Nine’s Greatest Quote Still Hasn't Been Beaten

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

Star Trek: Deep Space Nine is the black sheep of the Star Trek franchise, a truth that has divided opinions ever since its debut in 1993. It eschews the utopian future that was Gene Roddenberry's vision, with its enlightenment, moral fortitude, and the wonder of exploration, summed up in the "to boldly go where no man has gone before" phrase from its iconic opening narration. In its place is a gritty, darker series that takes place on a stationary outpost, where the ideal is constantly tested by moral ambiguity, subterfuge, and conflict. Nowhere is that challenge more evident than in Star Trek: Deep Space Nine's greatest quote, one made 33 years ago that began as a statement of belief in moral righteousness, only to become one of dark irony as the series progresses.

The quote comes in the fourth episode of Star Trek: Deep Space Nine's first season: "A Man Alone." Constable Odo (René Auberjonois), Deep Space Nine's security chief, is wary of the influx of characters boarding the station via the recently discovered wormhole. He takes particular offense to one, Ibudan (Stephen James Carver), who had been a smuggler of goods to Bajorans during the Cardassian Occupation. The popular opinion is that Ibudan is a hero; only Odo doesn't share that opinion. He tells Commander Sisko (Avery Brooks), after getting into a fight with Ibudan, that Ibudan had, after a young girl's parents were unable to pay for his smuggled goods, callously let her die. Strike one, in Odo's mind, and strike two came when Ibudan killed a Cardassian officer.

That intense dislike doesn't bode well for Odo after Ibudan is found dead in one of Quark's (Armin Shimerman) holosuites. Odo is now the prime suspect, prompting Sisko to warn him that he is not to breach protocol or take the law into his own hands. Odo retorts with the series' greatest quote:

"Laws change, depending on who's making them — Cardassians one day, Federation the next. But, justice is justice."

In context, Deep Space Nine had only recently come under joint Federation-Bajoran administration following the extended, harsh occupation by the Cardassians. Odo's role, however, has not changed under the new regime: he was the enforcer of the law under the Cardassians, and now he is under the joint administration. As one would expect, the written laws under Cardassian rule were far more Draconian than those of Starfleet, so the idea of what is right is fluid, depending on who's in charge. Odo, by his very nature, sees justice as an absolute principle, a standard of morality that is true and right regardless of what the powers-that-be dictate. Or, as he so elegantly says it, "Justice is justice."

History is littered with examples of government edicts that contradict what is morally right – look no further than Nazi Germany as a perfect example – so Odo is not wrong. But as the series progresses, Odo's strict adherence to order and fairness is challenged regularly, forcing him to see that his black-and-white thinking is more complicated in this gray environment. Order is only maintained by obeying the law, where justice often means breaking it: the pursuit of both is an exercise in futility.

And then things take a darkly ironic turn when Odo, a Changeling, discovers his origins in the Season 3 premiere two-parter, "The Search, Part I" and "The Search, Part II." That part of Odo that sees order as an absolute is, indeed, an innate part of his people, a fundamental principle they cling to. The part of Odo that seeks justice, however, is learned. Where Odo sees justice as an absolute, the Changelings see control as an absolute instead, and couldn't care less about justice. The quote now, then, takes on a tragic irony where, in his heritage, justice isn't justice: justice is control. And it's that learned justice that forces Odo to turn against the Changelings and return to Deep Space Nine.

The quote doesn't just apply to Odo's arc, but to the series as a whole. Laws don't apply in the face of the need for survival or to do what is just. The best example comes from Captain Sisko in "In the Pale Moonlight." In his pursuit of the greater good, he lies and schemes in order to bring the Romulans into the Dominion War. That pursuit inadvertently brings about the assassination of a Romulan senator, leaving Sisko to have to rectify how his actions brought it about and accept it, a casualty in the chase for justice. It's these types of complex narratives that tie directly, or indirectly, into Odo's quote that make Star Trek: Deep Space Nine a fuller, more mature entry in the Star Trek franchise.
