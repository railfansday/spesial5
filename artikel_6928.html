This Wood Finish Was Once Seen as “Outdated.” But Designers Now Say It's Coming Back.

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

You might regret ripping out those kitchen cabinets.

For a period of time in the late 1980s to early 2000s, it seemed virtually impossible to enter a house and not come across honey oak. That bright, orange-toned wood finish covered kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities, even baseboard trim and furniture around the house.

“You’d see it everywhere—cabinetry, trim, stair railings, and floors. It was the go-to wood tone for builders and homeowners alike,” Cathleen Gruver of Gruver Cooley tells House Beautiful. “But by the early 2000s, it started to fall out of favor as people shifted toward cooler, more neutral interiors.”

However, there’s been a noticeable shift in the way people are wanting their interiors to look, and warmer spaces are definitely back in style. With this evolution, can we expect to see a revival of honey oak, as well? We asked a handful of designers if they think this golden wood finish is back, and how they would style it for modern-day interiors.

Elizabeth Bennett and Mallory Robins, the founders of interior design studio Kobel + Co, believe honey oak is back for two reasons: The first is that warm tones throughout the entirety of the home are on the rise; the second is that honey oak has a lot of nostalgic associations for people who grew up in the '80s and '90s and are now homeowners.

“We're seeing a resurgence of styles from this era not only in homes, but in fashion as well, as we harken back to a pre-digital era,” Bennett explains.

“It’s not necessarily a full-blown comeback, but I’ve definitely noticed a soft return—particularly in cabinetry, millwork details, and furniture," Gruver says. “Clients aren’t always asking for 'honey oak' by name, but they are gravitating toward natural wood tones that offer warmth and character.”

Before you take a sledgehammer to those original honey oak cabinets, reconsider this tone by styling it in more modern ways. Keep reading to see fresh ways that designers are working with honey oak in their new projects.

As homeowners gravitate more toward lived-in, inviting interiors, honey oak is a wonderful way to add warmth. Leave it to the professionals to find stylish ways to make this once-outdated wood tone look cool again.

Plenty of designers will tell you to mix wood tones in your home to prevent it from feeling one-note, and the same is encouraged when honey oak makes a big presence. When designer Adina Hall took on the project above, there was too much honey oak in the space for her team to replace or refinish it all, so they embraced it instead.

“We developed a custom stain for the new wood floors, a whitewashed French oak that created a neutral canvas for the structure without competing with the existing honey tone,” Hall says. “We added custom cabinetry finished in a taupe-tone ash veneer, again a neutral tone, and we leaned into crisp whites and some cool tones like sage green and indigo blue as accents to create a balanced palette.”

Heavy amounts of warm woods can overwhelm the eye; it’s important to build a restrained palette and introduce interest with cooler tones, wood stains, sculptural furniture, and contrasting materials. “The key is to give the honey oak a contemporary context in order to make it feel like an intentional material that delivers a punch of warmth in an otherwise cool and architecturally bold yet restrained palette.”

Whether you’re adding in fresh honey oak or you’re trying to work around what’s already in your home, one of the best ways to ensure it feels modern and not like a ‘90s Tuscan-inspired home is choosing an earthy color palette. Muted greens, warm whites, greiges, taupe, light blues—these are all beautiful hues that won’t add even more orange-ish warmth to your space.

“Instead of making honey oak the focus of the room, I let the beauty of the wood shine through the use of clean lines, black matte touches, natural stones, and modern lighting,” designer Nancy Dow of Sierra Living Concepts says. She’s fond of using this wood, and uses these shades to create a modern and fresh appearance.

Rather than “wood drenching” a home in a single tone (as was often seen in the early aughts), Robins explains that current homeowners are exercising more restraint with their finishes. “Rather than the primary finish for a house on trim, floors, and cabinetry, it's being used in more subtle ways—a cased good piece or a kitchen island alone,” she says.

One of the reasons honey oak fell out of style is because it is a very warm tone, and in the world of “millennial gray,” an orange-tinted kitchen or bathroom isn’t very appealing. However, designer Marli Jones of Rebel House design studio believes this can be fixed simply by creating a contrasting palette.

In the kitchen space above, Jones used only honey oak cabinetry in the scullery to contrast the light, bright main kitchen. “It adds a coziness to the scullery and envelops the space in warm, natural tones,” she says. “That same honey color travels across the countertop stone and into the tile backsplash.” The two rooms are connected by the wall of glass and steel, but the change in materiality from the white cabinets to the honey oak creates a dynamic transition. “Warm wood always adds a grounding layer.”

When adding a honey oak piece into your home, do so intentionally. Opt for a “stunning built-in, a custom island, or as an accent detail that brings texture and warmth,” Gruver says. To emphasize the styling, pair the honey oak piece with contemporary hardware or painted elements to help it feel fresh and curated.

While honey oak can work in any interior aesthetic, there are a few styles it looks best in, according to Gruver: updated farmhouse and cottagecore. Basically, “any space that leans into character, craft, and comfort,” she explains. However, she's also seen the tone used successfully in more modern, organic spaces where there's a large emphasis on natural materials and textures. It all depends on how it's balanced, which holds true for any wood tone. She adds, “When styled right, honey oak can absolutely feel current again.”
