From raw oysters to leftover rice: 10 foods tied to foodborne illness

Tanggal: 2026-11-06

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These 10 foods that cause food poisoning, from raw oysters to leftover rice, carry specific bacteria and viruses, and simple handling habits lower the risk

A bout of food poisoning can usually be traced to a particular germ that found its way into a particular food. Salmonella travels with poultry and eggs. Listeria survives the cold of a refrigerator. Vibrio bacteria collect in oysters that filter seawater. Knowing which pairing is at work explains why some foods call for more care than others.

Foodborne illness is the broad term for sickness caused by bacteria, viruses, parasites or toxins in what people eat and drink. The foods linked to it tend to share a few traits. Some come from animals that carry bacteria in their gut or environment. Some are eaten raw, which removes the cooking step that would otherwise kill whatever is present. Others become a problem after cooking, through the way they are cooled and stored.

That last group shows that safe ingredients are only part of the picture. A pot of rice left on the counter can let bacterial spores that survived cooking germinate and produce toxins. Here, the risk comes from handling rather than from the raw ingredient.

The list below covers 10 foods that cause food poisoning when something goes wrong in how they are produced, prepared or stored. It is a selection, not a ranking, and the order carries no meaning. It spans animal products such as chicken, eggs, ground beef, raw milk, deli meats and oysters. It also includes raw produce such as leafy greens, sprouts and cantaloupe, along with one common leftover.

Home food safety comes down to four steps: clean, separate, cook and chill. Clean means washing hands, utensils and surfaces. Separate means keeping raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs away from foods that will be eaten without cooking. Cook means heating food to a safe internal temperature. Chill means getting perishable food into the refrigerator promptly.

Each slide explains the pathogen tied to one food, how contamination happens and the handling habits that lower the risk for that item. Pregnant women, older adults, young children and people with weakened immune systems face higher stakes from several of these foods. For them, the details on each slide carry extra weight.

Raw or undercooked chicken is a common route to food poisoning because poultry often carries Salmonella and Campylobacter. U.S. government guidance is to cook all poultry to an internal temperature of 165 degrees Fahrenheit. That temperature kills both bacteria.

Both organisms live in the intestines of healthy birds. They can reach the meat during slaughter and processing. A chicken breast can look clean and smell fresh while still carrying bacteria on its surface. The same applies to turkey, duck and other poultry.

Thorough cooking is the step that makes poultry safe to eat. A food thermometer shows when the meat has reached that temperature. For a whole bird, check the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing and the thickest part of the breast. Keep the probe away from the bone. Touching bone can produce an inaccurate reading.

Ground chicken and ground turkey follow the same 165-degree target as whole cuts. So does stuffing cooked inside a bird. Stuffing absorbs raw juices from the cavity and needs to reach the same temperature as the meat around it.

Washing raw chicken before cooking adds risk rather than removing it. Rinsing under the tap does not kill Salmonella or Campylobacter. Water splashing off the meat can carry bacteria onto the sink, nearby counters, dish towels and any food sitting close by. Cooking to 165 degrees Fahrenheit already destroys the bacteria. A rinse adds nothing to that protection.

Raw poultry juices create a second route for illness through cross-contamination. A cutting board used for raw chicken can pass bacteria to salad vegetables or bread chopped on it afterward. Knives, plates, tongs and hands can do the same. Separate boards for raw meat and ready-to-eat foods reduce that risk. Hot, soapy water cleans boards and utensils between uses. Hands need washing with soap after touching raw poultry.

Refrigerator placement matters too. Raw chicken kept on a lower shelf, sealed in a container or set on a plate, cannot drip onto foods below it. Marinade that held raw poultry should not go on cooked food as a sauce unless it is boiled first.

Salmonella can be present inside an intact egg, not only on its shell. Raw or runny egg dishes can cause food poisoning even when the egg looks clean and uncracked.

The obvious place to look for contamination is the outside of the egg, where dirt or droppings might touch the shell. Shell $SHEL contamination does happen. Salmonella can also reach the yolk or white before the shell forms, when a hen carries the bacteria in her reproductive tract. A spotless egg straight from the carton can still hold the bacteria inside.

Thorough cooking kills Salmonella. The concern centers on foods in which the egg is eaten raw or only lightly heated. Homemade mayonnaise is a common example. It is made by whisking raw yolks with oil, with no cooking step at all.

Pasteurized eggs offer a practical swap for recipes that call for raw egg. In-shell pasteurized eggs are heated in a warm water bath long enough to kill bacteria without cooking the egg. Pasteurized liquid egg products sold in cartons serve the same purpose in dressings, sauces and desserts.

For eggs eaten on their own, cooking until both the yolk and the white are firm kills Salmonella. Sunny-side-up, poached and soft-boiled eggs leave the yolk runny. That leaves more room for any bacteria inside to survive. Cracked eggs give bacteria an easier way in and are safer thrown away. Storing eggs in their carton on a refrigerator shelf, rather than in the door, keeps them colder and more consistent in temperature.

Ground beef should be cooked to an internal temperature of 160 degrees Fahrenheit under U.S. guidance, because grinding spreads surface bacteria through the whole piece of meat. E. coli O157:H7 is the pathogen most associated with undercooked burgers.

These bacteria live in the intestines of cattle. During slaughter and processing, they can end up on the outer surface of a cut of beef.

On a whole steak or roast, that outer layer is where the bacteria stay. The dense interior of an intact cut is generally protected. Searing the outside exposes surface bacteria to direct heat. For that reason, a rare steak carries a different risk than a rare burger.

Grinding removes that protection. The process takes whatever sat on the surface and mixes it through the entire batch. One batch of ground beef can also combine trimmings from more than one animal. Bacteria from a single source can then end up in many patties.

The center of a burger is no safer than its edge. A patty with a dark, seared crust can still hold live bacteria in an undercooked middle.

A food thermometer settles the question. For a burger, push the probe in through the side of the patty until the tip reaches the thickest part. A reading taken from the top of a thin patty can put the tip too close to the hot surface. That can overstate the temperature at the center.

E. coli O157:H7 produces a toxin known as Shiga toxin. In serious cases, an infection can damage the kidneys. Young children and older adults face a greater risk of these complications.

The 160-degree target covers every dish built on ground beef, not only burgers. Meatloaf, meatballs, chili and stuffed peppers all start from the same mixed meat. U.S. guidance sets the same temperature for ground pork, veal and lamb. Raw preparations such as steak tartare, made from chopped or minced beef, skip the cooking step entirely.

At restaurants, a burger served rare or medium-rare has not reached the temperature U.S. guidance sets for ground beef. Diners who want to meet that standard can ask for a burger cooked to 160 degrees.

Raw oysters can concentrate Vibrio bacteria and norovirus from the water they grow in. Eating them uncooked means swallowing whatever the oyster has collected, with no heat step to kill it.

The risk starts with how oysters feed. They are filter feeders that pump large volumes of seawater across their gills to strain out plankton and other particles. Bacteria and viruses in that water can build up inside the animal. Clams and mussels feed the same way. The concern therefore extends to other bivalve shellfish served raw.

An oyster is also eaten whole, gut included. A fish fillet is different. Its digestive organs are removed before it reaches the plate.

Vibrio bacteria occur naturally in coastal salt water and brackish water. Their presence is not a sign of sewage or pollution. Warmer water favors their growth. An oyster from a clean, well-managed bed can still carry them.

One species, Vibrio vulnificus, is a particular danger for people with liver disease or weakened immunity. In those groups an infection can turn severe. People with a chronic liver condition should avoid raw oysters entirely. The same caution applies to anyone whose immune system is suppressed by illness or medical treatment.

Norovirus reaches shellfish by a different route. It enters growing waters through human waste, from sources such as sewage overflows or boats discharging untreated waste. It is a common cause of vomiting and diarrhea. It also spreads easily from person to person once someone is infected.

Popular toppings offer no protection. Lemon juice and hot sauce do not kill the bacteria. A fresh smell and a clean, glossy appearance cannot reveal whether an oyster carries Vibrio or norovirus.

Heat is what lowers the risk. Fried oysters, oyster stew, grilled oysters and steamed mussels carry less risk than the same shellfish served raw on ice. When steaming clams, mussels or oysters in the shell, throw out any that stay closed after cooking. Shucked oysters should be cooked through until the flesh is firm.

Raw milk, meaning milk that has not been pasteurized, can carry Listeria, Salmonella, E. coli and Campylobacter. Pasteurization kills these organisms. That heating step is the main safeguard between a glass of milk or a wedge of soft cheese and a possible infection.

Bacteria can enter milk at several points before it reaches a bottle. An infected udder can shed germs directly into the milk. Traces of manure on the animal's skin or on milking equipment can add more. A clean-looking farm and a healthy-looking animal offer no guarantee. These organisms are invisible, and they do not change the taste or smell of the milk.

Pasteurization addresses that problem with heat. Milk is warmed to a set temperature, held there for a set time, then cooled quickly. The process takes its name from Louis Pasteur, the 19th-century French scientist whose work showed that heating could kill microbes in wine and beer. Pasteurization does not sterilize milk. Pasteurized milk still needs refrigeration, and it will still spoil over time.

Soft cheeses made from raw milk carry the same risk in another form. Styles such as brie, camembert and queso fresco are higher in moisture and lower in acid than many hard cheeses. When they are made from raw milk, bacteria in the milk can carry through into the finished cheese. Fresh Latin-style cheeses such as queso fresco are sometimes sold informally, outside regular stores. That makes their origin and production method harder to check.

Listeria is the main concern with these cheeses. Pregnant women, older adults and young children face greater risk from it than other people. In pregnancy, a Listeria infection can pass to the baby, even when the mother feels only mildly unwell.

The practical step is reading the label. In the U.S., look for the word "pasteurized" on milk and cheese packaging. Rules on selling raw milk differ from state to state. As a result, what shoppers find on shelves varies by location. At restaurants, farmers markets and when traveling abroad, asking whether a soft cheese is made from pasteurized milk is the most direct check.

Deli meats and hot dogs can carry Listeria monocytogenes, a bacterium that survives refrigeration. These foods are sold ready to eat, so there is often no cooking step at home to kill it.

Cold slows most foodborne bacteria sharply. Listeria behaves differently. It can keep growing slowly at refrigerator temperatures. A package of sliced turkey or a pack of franks can become riskier the longer it sits in the fridge.

Contamination usually happens after the meat has been cooked. Ham, bologna, salami and roast beef are processed, then sliced, handled and packaged. Listeria can reach the meat during those later steps, from equipment or surfaces in a processing plant. Meat sliced to order at a deli counter passes through one more machine before it reaches the customer.

Hot dogs follow a similar path. They are fully cooked before packaging, and many people eat them straight from the package. That habit skips the one step that would destroy bacteria picked up after the factory cooking.

Listeria poses a particular risk during pregnancy. An infection can pass to the fetus, even when the mother has mild symptoms or none at all. U.S. food safety guidance advises pregnant people to heat deli meats and hot dogs until steaming hot before eating them. The same guidance covers older adults and people with weakened immune systems. Thorough heating kills Listeria. A quick warm-up that leaves the center cool does not.

A refrigerator set at 40 degrees Fahrenheit or below keeps many bacteria in check. A refrigerator thermometer confirms that setting. Many refrigerator dials use numbered settings rather than actual degrees.

Cold storage alone does not stop Listeria, though. Eating opened packages of deli meat promptly limits the time the bacteria have to multiply. Keeping the liquid from hot dog packages away from other foods and kitchen surfaces also matters. That fluid can carry Listeria too.

Romaine lettuce, spinach and other leafy greens can carry E. coli and other bacteria straight to the plate. They are usually eaten raw, so no cooking step stands between a contaminated leaf and the person eating it.

That habit sets greens apart from many foods linked to foodborne illness. A chicken breast or a burger patty gets heated to a temperature that kills bacteria. A salad does not. Whatever reaches a leaf in the field or the packing shed can still be on it at dinner.

E. coli outbreaks in the U.S. have been traced to leafy greens, and romaine and spinach have both been involved. The strains behind these outbreaks produce Shiga toxin. They can cause serious stomach illness. In some people, especially young children, the infection can lead to kidney damage.

Contamination can start long before harvest. Irrigation water, runoff from nearby livestock operations and animal droppings in fields can carry bacteria onto growing plants. Harvesting, chopping and packing give those bacteria more chances to spread. Cutting damages the leaf, and bacteria can lodge in its torn edges and crevices.

Bagged salads go through a commercial wash, but a pre-washed label does not mean the contents are sterile. Bagged greens still need to stay refrigerated. They should be used before the date printed on the package.

Whole heads and loose bunches call for a few steps at home. Remove the outer leaves first. Rinse the remaining leaves under running water just before eating, rather than soaking them in a sink of standing water. Soap and bleach are not meant for produce and should not be used on it.

Rinsing removes some dirt and germs, but it cannot remove every bacterium clinging to a leaf. Greens that are damaged, slimy or wilted belong in the trash. Refrigerating cut or chopped greens promptly limits how much any bacteria present can grow.

Heat changes the picture. Spinach, kale and other sturdy greens cooked until fully wilted, as in a sauté or soup, no longer carry the same raw-produce risk.

Raw sprouts carry a food poisoning risk that begins before they ever reach a store shelf. The warm, humid conditions used to sprout alfalfa, mung bean and clover seeds also let bacteria multiply quickly.

Sprouting depends on moisture and warmth. Growers soak the seeds in water, then keep them damp and warm for several days while they germinate. Salmonella, E. coli and Listeria grow well in those same conditions. A small number of bacteria on a seed can become a much larger population by the time the sprouts are harvested.

The seed itself is often the source. Bacteria can sit on the seed coat, lodge in its cracks or get inside the seed. From there they travel into the finished sprout. That sets sprouts apart from many other raw vegetables. The contamination does not have to come from a dirty knife or a careless shopper — it can be part of the product from the start.

Rinsing does little to solve the problem. Water can wash some bacteria off the surface. It cannot reach organisms that have grown inside the sprout. A thorough wash at the kitchen sink does not make raw sprouts safe.

Growing sprouts at home does not remove the hazard either. A sprouting jar on a countertop recreates the same warm, wet environment a commercial grower uses. Contaminated seeds bring their bacteria into the jar with them. Home-grown sprouts can carry the same risk as store-bought ones.

The way people eat sprouts adds to the concern. Alfalfa and clover sprouts usually go raw onto sandwiches, wraps and salads. Mung bean sprouts often arrive as a fresh garnish on dishes such as pho or pad thai. They are served raw on the side or scattered on top.

Cooking is the step that lowers the risk. Stir-frying bean sprouts, or simmering them in soup until they are steaming hot, reaches bacteria that rinsing leaves behind. When ordering at a restaurant, diners can ask for sprouts to be cooked or left off the plate.

The stakes are higher for some people. Listeria poses a greater danger to pregnant women, older adults and young children. People in those groups, and anyone with a weakened immune system, can choose cooked sprouts instead of raw ones.

Cooked rice left at room temperature is a common source of food poisoning from Bacillus cereus. The bacterium's spores survive cooking, then germinate as the rice cools and produce toxins that reheating may not destroy.

Bacillus cereus lives in soil and dust. It can reach rice grains in the field, and dry, uncooked rice can carry its spores into a home kitchen. A spore is a dormant, hardy form of the bacterium. Boiling or steaming kills active bacteria, but spores can come through the pot intact.

The danger builds after the meal is served. A full pot left on the stove or counter cools slowly. On the way down, it passes through temperatures where the spores can become active again. Once they germinate, the bacteria multiply and release toxins directly into the rice.

One of those toxins causes vomiting. It withstands heat, so warming leftover rice does not necessarily make it safe to eat. A microwave or a hot wok can kill the bacteria while leaving the toxin behind. Bacillus cereus can also cause a separate, diarrheal form of illness.

This pattern is sometimes called fried rice syndrome. The name reflects a familiar sequence in restaurant and home kitchens. Rice is cooked in a large batch, held at room temperature, then stir-fried later. Fast, high-heat frying warms the grains. It cannot be relied on to break down toxin that has already formed.

Speed is the main defense. A deep pot keeps its center warm for longer, so cooked rice should not be left to cool in the pot it was made in. Spreading it in a shallow container lets heat escape faster. Then it should go into the refrigerator promptly.

Time limits apply as well. Perishable food, including cooked rice, should not sit out for more than two hours. When the air is above 90 degrees Fahrenheit, such as at a summer picnic, the limit drops to one hour. Rice that has passed those limits belongs in the trash, not the microwave.

The same habits apply to takeout, buffet trays and rice cooked ahead for weekday lunches. The risk is not limited to rice. Other cooked starches, such as pasta, can also carry Bacillus cereus and need the same prompt cooling.

A cantaloupe's rough, netted rind can trap bacteria, and a knife passing through it can carry them into the flesh. Listeria and Salmonella have both been linked to cantaloupe. The rind needs cleaning even though nobody eats it.

The netting on a cantaloupe is a web of raised ridges and shallow crevices. The fruit grows in contact with the ground. Soil, irrigation water and handling all leave material on the surface before a melon reaches a kitchen. Bacteria can settle into those grooves, where a quick splash of water may not reach them.

The main hazard comes during preparation. A blade enters at the rind and travels straight into the edible flesh. Anything sitting on the outside can ride along on the knife edge. The same transfer happens when cut slices are pushed across a cutting board that the unwashed fruit touched moments earlier.

Once inside, bacteria land on a moist, sweet and low-acid surface. Those conditions do little to slow bacterial growth. U.S. food safety rules for restaurants and stores treat cut melon as a perishable food that must be kept cold. A whole, uncut melon does not fall under that requirement.

Scrubbing the rind before cutting is the standard first step. A clean produce brush under cool running water can work into the netting better than fingers alone. U.S. food safety guidance calls for plain water rather than soap, detergent or bleach when washing produce. Patting the melon dry with a clean towel removes more of the loosened material.

Smooth-skinned honeydew and watermelon call for the same routine. Their rinds have fewer crevices to hide bacteria. They still grew in a field and passed through many hands on the way to a shopping cart.

After cutting, the pieces belong in a covered container in the refrigerator. A platter of sliced melon left sitting on a picnic or buffet table loses that protection. Pre-cut melon from a grocery store should come from a refrigerated case and go straight into the fridge at home.

The stakes are higher for groups more vulnerable to Listeria, including pregnant people and older adults. Washing and cutting a whole melon at home lets them know how the fruit was handled and how long ago it was cut. Pre-cut cubes of unknown age offer no such record.
