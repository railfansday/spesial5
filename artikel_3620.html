Get Huge: Looksmaxxing to Death Is a Documentary About the Shocking Deaths of Two Biohacking Influencers

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

If you’re familiar with the term “looksmaxxing,” which refers to an online trend dedicated to optimizing one’s life by way of physical improvement through steroids and peptides made popular in the manosphere, then you’ve inevitably come across Clavicular. The 20-year-old influencer, born Braden Peters, has become the (often bone-smashed) face of this male-dominated internet subculture. Famous for viral stunts that often leave him bruised, battered, or, in at least one instance, hospitalized, Peters has faced a slew of legal battles while documenting his physical transformation for his more than 2 million followers.

“It was actually never a Clavicular thing,” says director Stacey Lee, the filmmaker behind Get Huge: Looksmaxxing to Death, a three-part docuseries about manosphere biohackers that begins streaming on Hulu November 10. News that the online looksmaxxing influencer Clavicular would appear in a documentary “got picked up and swept into places that weren’t what we were up to,” Lee tells Vanity Fair in her first interview about the project.

“Look, when you’re making a documentary about influencers, it’s very hard for the documentary itself not to become caught up in that [world],” she says, clarifying that Clavicular was never the documentary’s focus. “We were looking to spend time and actually understand these guys—not just as outrageous human beings, but what is driving them? Where are they coming from?” says Lee.

Following influencers like Clavicular may help illuminate what motivates an increasingly popular, exceedingly online idea of modern masculinity. A generation of boys who were socialized behind screens, and sold steroids as a cure-all for their societal woes, came of age in the two years that Lee and her crew spent shadowing various manosphere influencers, starting in 2023. “When we came in, this was niche,” says Lee, “and even after the first [day of] shooting, it was front and center, mainstream cultural conversation—politics, RFK, all these things coming into play as we were making this documentary. Simultaneously, all of the documentary’s main looksmaxxers “escaped to Thailand because America doesn’t offer them enough,” Lee says, shrugging. “So there’s a bit of contradiction in that.”

The resort city of Pattaya, Thailand, is where Lee was introduced to Tony Huge, born Anthony Hughes, an “overstuffed Marvel character,” a biohacking influencer who predated Andrew Tate, Bryan Johnson, and Clavicular, says Lee. “He became famous for taking DNP, which is a fat burner. Essentially you take one pill and lose a bunch of weight, you take two pills and…people [could] die,” she explains. “He was willing to go on camera and do things that a lot of people at the time were not. In the world of bodybuilding and fitness culture, so many men are taking a bunch of drugs but not really talking about it, whereas Tony took a different approach. And that created this brand, maybe an illusion for him, of radical transparency. But when you are like, you can see everything, it actually makes you more on guard like, what are we not seeing?”

Tony Huge, whose online mantra to his 250,000 followers across social media became “a day natty [short for natural] is a day wasted,” became a steroid-injecting pioneer for a group of vulnerable men, such as documentary participant Elon Muscular, a then-suicidal 19-year-old who messaged Hughes on Facebook and received an invite to his hero’s Thailand compound. “Young men were drawn into this world because they were disillusioned with the modern medical kind of environment of being told what not to do,” says Lee. “They were also feeling the statistics of loneliness and depression amongst men that are just becoming increasingly worse.”

Muscular found community in Hughes’s admittedly “cult-like” living situation, one where multiple men manipulated each other’s brain chemistry—and made social media content about it—all in the name of masculinity. “Most men come to Thailand because they’re mentally unstable,” Hughes says in the docuseries. Among his rotating circle of sidekicks was online influencer Leo Longevity, a fellow biohacker and self-described penis-enlargement expert who died in January 2023 inside the home that he shared with Hughes. “Obviously, a penis influencer dying within this manospheric space was already a really absurd story,” says Lee. “But we started to see that this wasn’t just a one-off situation, but a larger reoccurring issue within this world, and certainly people that are part of this specific community. Then it became a little bit more urgent, the story.”

“Why Is Everyone Around Tony Huge Dying?” reads a YouTube comment under one of Hughes’s videos after the death of Longevity, real name Laith Abdallah Algaz, which is included in the documentary. That is a central question that also emerges in Looksmaxxing, as the series uncovers the string of sudden and strange deaths of Hughes’s closest confidants, including Nick Weisser (in 2018) and Josh Brooks (in 2021), and the apparent disappearance of Coach Trevor in 2022. “Throughout the process, I was very straight up with Tony, ‘I’m going to ask you all the things that everyone’s already talking about behind your back. And I think it’s really important that you have to answer them,’” Lee recalls. “He’s never fazed by that, to be honest, which doesn’t feel like a normal response. Tony said in the beginning, ‘I don’t care if I’m a villain or hero. I don’t know which one it is,’” says the director. “He gets censored so much that he’s just willing to put his story out and see.”

Lee’s crew began filming with Hughes following Algaz’s death. “Everyone was presenting a united front on camera, and behind the scenes there was a lot of conversation that we started to become aware of about how they were being portrayed or what they were going to say,” she says, even pitching ideas to the director. “Actually, no, that’s for your channel. Just be yourself and let’s see how that feels,” Lee told them. “These guys don't do therapy. We did offer them mental health services many times. But they exist in this matrix, so to speak. They don’t trust medical professionals, [preferring to rely on] their own best sense of judgment.”

Nevertheless, Lee was welcomed into Hughes’s scarcely female circle of trust. “I was in their life so much, it was like they forgot that I was a woman a lot of times. After we’d wrapped something, Tony would be like, ‘Oh, what did you think of that?’” she says. “It’s almost like he remembered that they were talking about Red Pill philosophy or whatever it was in front of me. I remember a conversation very early on talking about how women can’t be successful in careers and should never be a leader. And yet here I was, sitting here directing a team of mostly men in a documentary.”

Gender dynamics were harder to ignore when documenting the multiple women who live with Hughes in Thailand. “A man should only have as many girlfriends as he can leg press,” Hughes says in one video that’s in the docuseries. “In regards to the women, yeah, that was one of the hardest parts to witness,” says Lee. “That’s where the inherent contradiction of who he is comes in. He proposes freedom, yet he’s got these women that are forced to live with him. Well, they can leave at any point in time. But there’s power dynamics at play, money at play. He genuinely believes he’s helping, improving them. It’s a very complex situation to say the least,” the filmmaker continues. “It was important to give those women, where we could, the voice to speak for themselves, but also to showcase what biohacking looks like for them within that space.”

Lee and her crew also built trust with Hughes’s closest allies, including Connor Murphy, a fitness-influencer-meets-performance artist whose online presence was predicated on pranks and then, while living with Hughes, on being the influencer’s “art project,” Murphy says in the doc. “My parents are extremely worried because a lot of people in the atmosphere around you-know-who seem to be dying,” Murphy tells Lee in the documentary. “At first it frightened me, but now it kind of enlivens me. I almost feel like a wizard. The boy who lived—for now,” he adds, raising his eyebrows in a smirk.

“As we got to know the characters deeper, they weren’t just one lump unit, but their own tectonic plates that were constantly realigning,” says Lee. “And that’s symptomatic of the larger manosphere itself. Everyone’s climbing a totem pole. They were, in a way, competing over who gets to tell the story.”

Things came to a sudden halt on July 7, when Murphy died at the age of 32 in what authorities have ruled an apparent drowning. “Production had finished [four weeks earlier] when he passed. I was on the phone with these guys all the time talking to them about what’s going on and where they’re at,” says Lee, who grows visibly emotional at every mention of Murphy. “His death was absolutely shocking. When you spend two years filming with them and getting to know them just as a regular human being, it affected me deeply.”

Hughes subsequently made a video saying that Murphy drowned in his backyard lake during an erratic drug-induced episode. (Investigators reportedly found two unused syringes and several white pills in a bag inside Murphy’s car.) “His passing reinforced why we were making this documentary. He wasn’t just somebody who died,” says Lee. “Being able to show that complicated side of who he was and not just his death defining his life was really, really important for us.”

As the mother of a nine-year-old son, Lee worries about the dearth of positive male examples in online spaces. “With these influencers—the Tates, the Fuentes, all these guys who are performing masculinity, it was so apparent to me that we are lacking really good male role models, where men have each other’s backs, where they’re looking out for the best in each other, where they’re lifting one another up,” says Lee. The next generation of men are “looking at these guys and seeing it as a blueprint for their life. When you call yourself an influencer, there is a level of responsibility that needs to be taken for what you say and do,” she says. “And in this world, because they want to be anarchist and free and don’t take responsibility for anyone, that is what creates these tragedies.”

While more daring online personalities infiltrate the manosphere, “there is a sense of Tony losing relevancy,” Lee says of Hughes, who is father to three daughters and a son on the way with his live-in girlfriends. “When you lose relevancy,” she adds, “you try and control what’s closest to you. When your identity is built around pushing boundaries, what happens when the next generation’s going further than you? And how do you know when it’s enough?”

As the younger generation turns to new influencers in the biohacking space, Lee says there’s plenty of evidence to suggest looksmaxxers—and the appetite for their content—aren’t going anywhere. “What we are witnessing is nihilism within the next generation where it’s about living for today. I need to look as hot as I can today,” she tells VF. “We are being trained to find the easy, quick solution and those solutions are never going to fix these emotionally complex layers of confidence and purpose and true connection. Those aren’t going to be found online.”
