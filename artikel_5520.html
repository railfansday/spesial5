Stock markets celebrate a ‘stunning rebuke’: Trump says he will accept midterm losses—but don’t expect defeat to change him

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

It was a “stunning rebuke” to President Trump. That’s not Fortune’s opinion, by the way. The words “stunning rebuke”—and variations on that theme—were the choice of dozens of front pages reporting on the midterm election results today.

“Democrats Take Congress in Stunning Rebuke of Trump,” the WSJ said. “Party Flips Senate and House as Voters Deliver Stunning Rebuke to Trump,” the NYT declared. “Americans deliver stunning rebuke to Donald Trump as blue wave sweeps country,” the FT wrote. “Texas Turns Blue in Stunning Rebuke of Donald Trump, Elon Musk and GOP,” Bloomberg said.

France’s Le Monde originally headlined its story as a “stunning rebuke for Trump” but, perhaps sensing that the term was already overused, later changed it to “spectacular rejection” (you can see the original in the URL).

The results were so sweeping—the Senate stood at 54-44 in favor of the Democrats this morning, with the House at 228-129—that even Trump appears to accept that reality has changed around him. He told Fox News last night, “I think the Democrats did a good job, I really do, I think they did a good job. They fought hard.”

Crucially, Trump also said he would accept the results. He told the NYT he was “absolutely willing to accept those results” and would work with Democrats. “We’ll, you know, look forward to working with them … We’ll probably work something out.”

But don’t mistake this for a sign that Trump will be chastened and change course on the issues that cost him the election, such as the war, tariffs, and the price of gas. Chris Ruddy, the president’s friend and CEO of Newsmax, told the BBC this morning that although the results were “horrendous” for Trump, “he’s not a man that reflects. He’s got sort of two drives, fast and faster, in his gearbox. I think he’s going to continue to pursue his policies by executive order and there will be a lot of battles.”

"The president likes to respond usually with brute force," Ruddy said.

It was as if the recent carnage—with the S&P 500 down a grueling 2.64% over the past month—never happened. All of a sudden, the global markets are in a buoyant mood. The major indexes in Europe and Asia were up across the board this morning (with the sole exception of Japan’s Nikkei). U.S. futures were up before the open in New York. Even the price of oil declined.

No prizes for guessing why: The midterm results promise deadlock between the White House and the U.S. Congress—and markets love the certainty of knowing that there will be few legislative surprises over the next couple of years.

Midterms are good for stocks, in the sense that they tend to mark the low point of the year for equities, and once the results are known a huge amount of uncertainty is removed from the market, according to Jim Reid at Deutsche Bank.

The “S&P 500 has risen after every U.S. midterm election since 1998, with average annual gains of nearly 12%,” according to Chris Beauchamp at IG.

In fact, the historic track record is even better than that, Deutsche’s Reid told clients: “The S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after the election-day close following every midterm from 1946 to 2022. The average gain is +14.4% and the median +13.7%.”

Suppliers of plywood are doing a roaring trade in Paris right now. All over the French capital, shop owners and restaurateurs have boarded up their windows against les manifestations—protests over a lack of education funding which have frequently devolved into rioting. Shops that aren't boarded up sometimes have cracked windows, where the looters have failed to gain entry.

Fixed to the boards are paper signs, saying “Nous sommes ouverts!” (We are open!) Business hasn’t stopped just because of politics.

In fact, as Fortune discovered on a visit to Paris recently, the capital is not—as social media would have you believe—a disaster zone of barricades and flying bottles. Quite the opposite. If you hadn’t read the news, you wouldn’t even know there were protests going on. Yes, there were 770 arrests nationwide on Saturday and Molotov cocktails were thrown at the police. But I took an Uber twice through la Place de la République—that’s the square with a big statue that demonstrators often climb onto—and didn’t see anyone there.

The city was not, as some Wall Street analysts have suggested, on the brink of civil breakdown.

The best example of this phenomenon—that the reality of the French protests is in fact much more boring than the viral videos suggest—was captured by this Instagram account, which intercuts scenes of chaos from the TV news with humdrum images of Parisiennes picking through street markets and drinking coffee in the autumn sunshine.

Yes, there is a beefed-up police presence. Les flics could be seen on many street corners, wearing armored vests. There were, perhaps, a few more sirens than normal. But nothing that would make a New Yorker blink.

After the interior minister admitted in October that the police had “lost control” of the protests, the French government is simply not sitting still for any more trouble.

With the street protests contained, that pushes the economic issue—the protesters want more spending and fewer cuts, which the bond market is unlikely to tolerate—into next April’s elections. Neither of the two most popular candidates, Marine Le Pen on the right and Jean-Luc Mélenchon on the left, has produced spending plans that the market takes seriously.

France is thus playing a game of chicken with the European Central Bank. If it fails to get its fiscal house in order, a debt crisis will emerge. At that point, the ECB has two choices: a) Buy French bonds in a bailout, devaluing the euro, and importing inflation throughout Europe. b) Do what the ECB did to Greece in 2015, when it forced Athens to accept austerity measures in return for a bailout. That program reduced Greek GDP by 25% over the following years. Unemployment hit 28%.

In both cases, the cure is worse than France’s current sickness. The protesters and their politicians might want to heal themselves before Frankfurt steps in.

A video of a prankster faking a theft from a Red Baron Pizza worker as he stocked a grocery store freezer has made the prankster an online pariah. It has also turned the worker, a Michigan father of four identified only as Walt Z., into the beneficiary of a GoFundMe campaign that had raised $761,514.

The video shows the cameraman asking Walt for the time. When Walt pulls out his phone, the prankster holds up his own device, plays a sound mimicking an Apple Pay transaction, and claims he just took $10.

“It’s not a joke to me,” Walt says. “I don’t have time for this crap in my life. My life is hard enough as it is.” He explains that his kids went to bed without him, that he was supposed to be home an hour earlier, and that he has to be up at 5 a.m. to get to 10 more stores.

The video angered so many people who saw it that donations began pouring in to a GoFundMe to support Walt. Red Baron stood behind Walt, too. “He’s got a fan club. Including us,” the company said in a social media post, saying that employee-of-the-month nominations were closed. “You know why.”
