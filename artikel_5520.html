Opinion: Washington and Beijing are building walls around biotechnology. That’s the wrong approach

Tanggal: 2026-11-27

We ranked the 25 most valuable publicly traded Chinese drugmakers

Kinch is a professor of pharmacology at Stony Brook University and the author of six books on pharmaceutical innovation.

For more than a decade, my colleagues and I at the Center for Research Innovation in Biotechnology have tracked every experimental medicine that enters human testing anywhere in the world. Last December, I wrote in STAT that China had reached parity with the United States in terms of the absolute number of new drugs introduced into clinical trials each year. Starting in 2018, the rate of U.S. contributions began to stagnate. Meanwhile, in 2013, Chinese organizations had introduced about 30 new experimental drugs. A decade later, the number topped 400. And there is no reason to expect the momentum in either country is changing.

Over the past month, both governments have seemingly been moving to wall themselves off. On Oct. 16, Beijing proposed barring foreign acquisition of its companies’ proprietary cell and gene platforms. Within a week, the U.S. Department of the Treasury proposed restrictions that would bar Americans from taking equity stakes, forming joint ventures, or making limited-partner investments in Chinese biotech companies and their equipment suppliers.

The proposals from Beijing and Washington do not impact licensing. American drugmakers can keep licensing Chinese molecules, and Chinese companies may develop new medicines to sell to the U.S. and European companies.

That open door matters more than either wall. Data show that the U.S.-China contest in biotechnology is not one race but several. Restricting investment will not help America win the races it is already losing. The bigger threat to U.S. leadership is not Chinese capital. It is the erosion, at home, of the research enterprise that built America’s lead.

The Center for Research Innovation in Biotechnology’s newest analysis, published in September in Drug Discovery Today, emphasizes the importance of these continued engagements. My colleagues and I evaluated the 17,000 new drugs that first entered human trials between 2000 and 2024, separating them into eight different modalities. These ranged from conventional pills to gene therapies. The convergence in the numbers of overall active ingredients that we reported last year turned out to be anything but uniform when looking at the details.

The growth by China was concentrated in a key subset of biologics. By the late 2010s, Chinese sponsors often had introduced more, and more novel, cell therapies annually than American sponsors. By the early 2020s, China had matched or exceeded the U.S. in the introduction of new monoclonal antibodies, a commercially important class of biologic drugs. China took a commanding lead in certain categories of monoclonals, such as multispecific antibodies and drug conjugates.

The U.S. still holds a larger share in two categories: nucleic acid drugs and gene therapy. Dominance in both technologies reflects tools built over decades of Western public and private investment. Examples include lipid nanoparticles that conveyed SARS-CoV2 vaccines, viral vectors, and CRISPR gene editing.

Those differences help explain why the two governments are building different walls. Beijing’s proposal shields cell and gene therapy from outside entities, areas where they have a demonstrated or aspirational determination to lead the world. Platforms must remain domiciled in China while individual products can be marketed abroad. That is a coherent industrial strategy, and it is paying off well. Chinese financial regulators report that foreign deals for Chinese biotech assets reached $120 billion through August 2026, up more than a third from the year before.

Washington’s proposal works differently. The Treasury Department proposal prevents American investors from punting on a piece of Chinese biotech and yet still allows American companies to pay for Chinese molecules. In areas where China now leads, the combined effect would seem at best to be a division of labor. As my co-author and I pointed out in our earlier STAT article, China increasingly invents and owns while American firms are more prone to take licenses, complete late stage development, and pay royalties.

Nonetheless, a decision to cut off scientific and/or economic engagement would seem detrimental to both nations.

Restriction is a defensive move. It does not put a single new medicine into the clinic. It treats “biotech” — which comprises drugs, devices, diagnostics, ag-tech, and many other areas — as one thing. Each of these competitive races is being run at different speeds and with entirely different players, outcomes, and pole positions.

The fundamental problem is the one described in our December 2025 article. American output of new experimental drugs has been flat since 2018. In our earlier study, China’s public sector, including universities and state-linked enterprises, contributed more than twice as many new drugs in 2024 as their American counterparts. All the while, the U.S. universities that built our lead in nucleic acids and gene therapy face shrinking federal research support as has been highlighted by STAT.

A competitor lacking fuel will lose the race even if regulators try to slow the opposition.

Three steps would accomplish more than walls ever can.

First, subject matter experts should take advantage of the public comment period the Treasury Department has opened through Dec. 7.

Those comments should press Treasury on several points. If it does impose restrictions, and it is not obvious that it should, they should be aimed narrowly at transfers of platforms and capabilities rather than at all investment. More clarity is also needed to address “NewCo” deals, in which a Chinese licensor takes a stake in a new U.S. company. Importantly, the regulator should set a date and objective criteria to review whether any new rules are working as intended.

Second, policymakers should prioritize and incentivize investment in areas where America leads or could lead. Nucleic acid and gene therapies depend on academic discovery and specialized manufacturing. Stable funding for proven models such as translational research by the National Institutes of Health, ARPA-H, and domestic manufacturing is the most obvious and reliable way to protect a declining margin. In cell therapy, policymakers should ask candidly why so many early trials now start in China (regulation, investment, technology, or combinations thereof), and how we might alleviate domestic hurdles.

Third, Washington should measure the race in a more nuanced way, such as by drug or disease type. The totals of new experimental drugs, such as those we have previously emphasized, can hide more meaningful long-term outcomes, such as FDA approvals as well as market and public health improvements. Federal agencies could start by tracking first-in-human trials by drug type and sponsor country. As this will take time, it should become a long-term indicator, ignoring annual variations. Policy aimed at the aggregate and/or based solely on short term measures will miss key details.

Walls are what you construct when you think you are under attack or are losing. In the technologies that will define the next generation of medicines, from RNA drugs to gene editing, American institutions still lead. The biggest threat does not come from abroad but from changes in domestic policy, such as cutting biomedical research. Our current lead in these fields was built with reliable investments by the public and private sectors, not barriers.

Michael S. Kinch is a professor of pharmacology at Stony Brook University and the author of six books on pharmaceutical innovation.
