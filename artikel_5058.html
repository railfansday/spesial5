Could Watching Sports Boost Your Mood? This Specific Group May Get the Biggest Benefit

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

There are so many reasons to watch sports, from the obvious excitement that comes with game day to joining a built-in community of fans. But now, new research suggests it may also be linked to fewer symptoms of depression for one group of adults.

The study focused on people with congenital heart disease (which is a range of structural abnormalities of the heart that someone is born with; an estimated 1.4 million adults in the U.S. live with the condition). But the findings are also in line with many other health benefits of watching sports, experts point out.

While many forms of congenital heart disease can be treated, symptoms of depression are more common in this group compared to others without the disease. The new study found a clear link between sports fans with congenital heart disease and those with the disease who didn't watch sports. The findings don't suggest that people should skip proven treatment options for depression in favor of watching games, but they do hint that having a sports-watching habit could give some patients an edge in the mental health department.

Experts also say watching sports is linked to many positive health outcomes—not just for those with congenital heart disease. Here’s what they say.

Meet the experts: Thea Gallagher, PsyD, clinical associate professor at NYU Langone Health and co-host of the Mind in View podcast; Jason Andrus, MD, chair of psychiatry, Northwell’s Northern Westchester Hospital; and Navjot Kaur Sobti, MD, interventional cardiologist and women’s health expert, Northwell’s Northern Westchester Hospital

The study, which was published in the European Journal of Cardiovascular Nursing, recruited 108 adults with congenital heart disease and asked them whether they watched sports and how often. Researchers also gave participants a standardized questionnaire to look for symptoms of depression.

The researchers found that participants who attended a sports game in person at least once in the previous year had fewer symptoms of depression than those who didn't attend any sporting events. But those who watched sports on TV or online at least once a week also had fewer symptoms of depression compared to those who didn't watch sports.

The study didn't prove that watching sports actually lowered the risk of depression—instead, it found a link between people watching sports and fewer symptoms of depression. Still, doctors say there's likely something here.

"One possible explanation is that sports offer connection, enjoyment, and something to look forward to," says Thea Gallagher, PsyD, clinical associate professor at NYU Langone Health and co-host of the Mind in View podcast. "Following a team can give people a shared identity and an easy reason to call a friend, get together, or feel part of a larger community."

For people with regular medical appointments or physical limitations, it can also provide them with an interest and identity beyond being a patient, Gallagher says. "From a behavioral perspective, depression often pulls people away from rewarding activities, which can deepen isolation and low mood," she says. "Watching a sport you enjoy could be an accessible way to reconnect with pleasure and other people."

Watching sports "doesn't replace staying physically active, but it’s a great, socially-connected way to enjoy some downtime," says Jason Andrus, MD, chair of psychiatry, Northwell’s Northern Westchester Hospital. "A loss of pleasure can be a major factor in depression and perhaps the thrills of victory, or even the agonies of defeat, experienced vicariously while spectating sports, can help break out of the 'blahs' zone," he adds.

People with depression often struggle with low energy, feelings of hopelessness, and trouble concentrating—and that can interfere with taking medications consistently, attending appointments, and maintaining routines around sleep and physical activity, Gallagher says. "Depression can also cause people to withdraw from relationships that provide emotional and practical support, just when they may need that support most," she adds.

The relationship between congenital heart disease and depression can go both ways, Gallagher says. "Living with a lifelong heart condition, medical uncertainty, and physical limitations can contribute to depression," she points out.

That's why cardiologists have depression on their minds, too. "We routinely screen our patients for depression as well as other mental health conditions as part of holistic and effective cardiac care," says Navjot Kaur Sobti, MD, interventional cardiologist and women’s health expert, Northwell’s Northern Westchester Hospital.

It makes sense that playing sports could have serious benefits for cardiovascular health, strength training, community-building, and boosting longevity...but experts point out that anyone can reap the social and health benefits that come with spectating. "The strongest case is for psychological and social benefits," Gallagher says. She notes that people who attend live sports events often feel less lonely and have a greater sense that life is worthwhile.

"Watching sports can also create opportunities for shared enjoyment, conversation, and belonging, although the experience will depend on the person and how they engage with it," she says.

Overall, Gallagher says the findings are a reminder to ask people what they enjoy and what helps them to feel connected. "For someone who loves sports, following a team may be an accessible way to keep those experiences in their life, particularly when illness makes other activities harder," she says.
