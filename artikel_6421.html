Restaurant cooking is becoming a surprisingly major source of air pollution

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Restaurant cooking is emerging as a major source of urban air pollution, sometimes exceeding vehicle exhaust emissions during peak dining hours.

The smell of food drifting out of a restaurant rarely makes people think of air pollution.

Yet new research shows that in parts of central London and Beijing, cooking releases more fine particles than vehicle exhaust during lunch and dinner hours.

Cleaner vehicles and tighter exhaust rules appear to have helped change that balance. Kitchens have faced less attention: UK and European rules require them to extract fumes, but do not require filters.

Ben Langford, an atmospheric scientist at the UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology, led the research.

His team measured emissions from one central neighborhood each in London, Beijing and Delhi, three cities with more than 10 million people.

“Most people smell cooking but never consider it as a pollutant, yet cooking can release large amounts of fine particulate matter into the atmosphere,” Langford said.

The team placed instruments on London’s BT Tower, 623 feet (190 meters) above the street, and on a Beijing tower at the Institute of Atmospheric Physics, 335 feet (102 meters) up.

In Old Delhi, they worked 108 feet (33 meters) above ground at Indira Gandhi Delhi Technical University for Women.

Above the rooftops, a wind sensor and an instrument that identifies chemicals in particles took readings five times a second.

Matching the air’s upward and downward movements with what it carried showed how much pollution came from the surrounding neighborhood.

Frying oil, car exhaust and coal fires produce different chemical mixtures, which helped the team trace particles to their sources.

To measure pollution moving through the air, the researchers separated their readings into upward and downward air movements.

These tiny particles can travel deep into the lungs, and some may enter the bloodstream.

In London, cooking produced up to 83% of the measured particle emissions at midday and 87% in the early evening. In Beijing, those shares reached 61% and 76%.

Both towers overlooked areas with many restaurants, and the researchers think commercial frying produced most of the cooking smoke.

At some hours, traffic contributed less than 10% of local emissions in both cities.

Beijing’s cooking emissions were about 75% higher in winter than summer, partly perhaps because the wind changed direction.

Winter winds mostly brought air from restaurants and commercial buildings to the northwest, while summer winds brought more air from a highway to the east.

Europe has tightened exhaust standards in stages since 1992.

London also introduced a congestion charge and an ultra-low emission zone, and its restrictions on polluting solid fuels helped keep coal and wood smoke barely detectable.

Study co-author Eiko Nemitz, also at the UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology, pointed to the success of traffic controls.

“Air quality policies targeting vehicle exhaust emissions have been highly effective,” Nemitz said. “As a result, cooking emissions are now a surprisingly important and poorly regulated part of the urban pollution mix.”

Earlier research in New York found a similar shift, with cooking, wildfire smoke and chemical products becoming increasingly important sources of summertime particle pollution.

Old Delhi told a different story: cooking accounted for 8% of direct particle emissions over an average day, while traffic contributed 56%.

Yet its cooking emissions per unit of area exceeded central London’s in winter and were only about 35% lower in summer.

Cooking’s small share reflects the much larger contribution from traffic and other burning.

The Delhi site stood near busy roads, a railway station and a large bus terminal, and the area released roughly two to four times as much soot as central London.

Both Beijing and Delhi restrict diesel vehicles to nighttime hours. The researchers suggested that looser emission controls and more older vehicles help explain Delhi’s higher soot levels.

Kitchen exhaust filters may have less impact in Delhi, where many homes have poor ventilation and more cooking happens outdoors.

The authors said traffic and other burning should remain the main targets there.

The sources releasing particles locally did not match the mix already in the air.

Cooking accounted for just 5% to 10% of airborne fine particles in London and Beijing, while traffic contributed 5% to 18%.

Much of the pollution formed in the air from gases, including ammonia, nitrogen oxides and sulfur dioxide. Many of these particles formed across the wider region, beyond the city center.

Particles formed this way accounted for 59% to 63% of the total in London’s and Beijing’s air, compared with 30% in Delhi.

Most of Delhi’s particles were organic material, from both local traffic and burning and distant sources such as farm fires.

The towers measured neighborhood emissions, but the air above them also contained pollution formed or carried in from elsewhere.

Cutting local emissions alone may therefore leave London and Beijing above World Health Organization guidelines, the authors wrote.

Most official pollution inventories leave cooking out. The UK counts cooking fuel but not smoke from the food, while European and global inventories omit cooking altogether.

The London Atmospheric Emissions Inventory added cooking recently.

Most of the BT Tower’s measurement area lies in Westminster, where the inventory put cooking at 44% of annual emissions after excluding brake and tire wear, close to the tower’s 45%.

Still, the inventory estimated about 76% more cooking emissions than the tower measured.

The winter measurements took place during the UK’s Plan B rules for the Omicron variant of COVID-19, introduced on December 8, 2021, which may have reduced restaurant and pub visits.

Cooking smoke also raises health concerns, as chefs face a higher risk of respiratory illness than other people.

Earlier research suggests fatty acids in cooking particles may help them stay airborne for days instead of hours.

Langford said better kitchen filtration could offer a relatively quick way to improve air quality.

“Over recent decades we have dramatically reduced emissions from road transport, but we have paid far less attention to emissions from commercial kitchens and food preparation,” Langford said.

“Our results suggest this is an area where further improvements in air quality could be achieved relatively quickly through better filtration of kitchen fumes.”

The study is published in the journal npj Climate and Atmospheric Science.
