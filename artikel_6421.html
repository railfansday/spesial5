Flowers with clear petal ‘windows’ carry fewer microbes, which influences pollinators

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Clear petal windows let infrared heat into two Florida shrubs' blooms, and warmth, not ultraviolet, thins out the flower microbes inside.

Some flowers have tiny, clear windows built right into their petals. While they might just look like a pretty design, researchers at Florida Atlantic University (FAU) have discovered that these see-through spots actually control the temperature inside the flower, changing the nectar and affecting how insects pollinate them.

Ultraviolet is the part of sunlight known for killing microbes, so the windows looked like a way of letting the sun scrub a flower clean.

Instead, the heat-carrying infrared coming through them is what mattered: warmer flowers held fewer microbes, and the ultraviolet didn’t explain the difference.

Jessica Williams, a Ph.D. student at Florida Atlantic University, did the work with four colleagues. They focused on shrubs named Lyonia lucida (also called fetterbush), and Lyonia fruticosa.

They are close relatives in the heath family that also includes blueberries. Both grow in the sandy, sun-baked scrub of south-central Florida and bloom together in late spring.

Each flower is a narrow urn with its petals fused into a chamber, and near the base of that urn a patch of petal has almost no pigment in it.

Other members of the family have the same windows, blueberries among them, and Williams’ team asked what they do to the light and heat inside.

Fetterbush flowers are visited mostly by moths at night, while L. fruticosa gets bees and wasps. Those visitors are probably how most microbes reach a flower.

Over three days in May 2025 at Archbold Biological Station, the team took seven flowers from each of nine plants per species, 126 in all.

For every flower, a thin temperature probe went in through the opening to the base of the chamber while a second probe hung just outside the petals.

Then the flower was clipped off with sterile scissors, dropped into a tube and kept on ice. Within five hours it was rinsed with a few drops of sterile water, pumped in and out five times.

That rinse went onto growth plates for two days at 81°F (27°C), and the team counted the colonies of yeast, mold and bacteria that grew.

Across the 108 flowers with a full set of measurements, the warmer a flower was inside, the fewer microbes grew from its rinse. Yeasts were the most common, then molds, then bacteria, and in both species the warmer flowers had fewer yeasts.

The two shrubs didn’t respond the same way. In L. fruticosa, warmer flowers had fewer bacteria, while in fetterbush the temperature made little difference to the bacteria. Fetterbush also carried more mold.

A fetterbush flower warmed by a bit more than half a degree for every degree the air around it warmed. An L. fruticosa flower barely moved, staying warmer than the air when the air was cool and cooler than the air when it was hot.

So a microbe’s experience of a hot May day depends on which shrub it landed in.

Heat was already known to matter to these microbes. A 2022 study in Frontiers in Microbiology found that heat can change which bacteria live in a flower’s nectar.

The windows let through more light than the pigmented petal, both ultraviolet and infrared. So the team tested the obvious idea, that ultraviolet was sterilizing the flower.

A germicidal ultraviolet lamp did kill. After 30 seconds under it, the share of L. fruticosa rinses with any yeast fell from 90 to 60 percent.

But microbes from flowers whose windows let through more ultraviolet were no better at surviving the lamp than microbes from flowers that let through less. The lamp was probably harsher than the ultraviolet a flower typically sees inside, Williams’ team wrote.

Infrared was a different story. In May 2026 the team chilled cut flowers in a refrigerator, then set them under a 500-watt halogen work light behind a filter.

One filter passed only deep red and infrared, and the other blocked infrared and passed only visible light. A probe inside each flower logged its temperature for 10 minutes.

Fetterbush flowers under infrared alone warmed about 5.8°F (3.21°C) more than flowers with the infrared blocked. After 10 minutes, they were 12.9°F (7.18°C) above where they started.

L. fruticosa flowers warmed about the same amount under either filter. Infrared, in other words, does warm a fetterbush flower.

Williams’ team doesn’t claim the windows evolved to keep microbes out. The chain they describe runs the other way. The windows let infrared in and, in fetterbush at least, the infrared warms the flower.

The warmth then probably makes the flower a harder place for microbes to persist. Microbes were still present in warm flowers, only fewer of them.

The windows may be doing several other jobs at once, the team wrote. Those could run from helping pollen develop to changing how bright the inside of the flower looks to a visiting insect. Fewer microbes would then be a side effect of those jobs.

“One of the exciting things about this work is that a seemingly small floral trait can change the physical environment inside a flower in ways that affect its microbial community,” Williams said.

“Because floral microbes can influence nectar traits and plant-pollinator interactions, these results suggest that flower structure may have ecological consequences that extend beyond the flower itself.”

Nectar and pollinators were not measured here. The field sampling covered 18 plants at one station over three days, and growth plates count only the microbes that will grow in a lab dish.

The team measured heat, not who delivered the microbes. Moths and bees bring different passengers, and which ones arrive first and how they compete inside the flower also shape which microbes settle in.

How those arrivals interact with the heat is, in the team’s words, an open question.

A warmer flower may also keep a different set of microbes rather than simply fewer, a subset that can take the heat. The study didn’t test that.

Finding out would mean identifying which microbes each kind of visitor drops off and which of them survive a warm afternoon inside a fetterbush flower.

The full study was published in the journal New Phytologist.
