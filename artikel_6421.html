A tiny protein ring reveals how cells keep DNA under control

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Scientists filmed a tiny protein complex changing shape and gripping DNA, revealing how it captures and holds different DNA segments.

A textbook draws DNA as a tidy spiral staircase. Inside a living cell, though, it is forever being copied, strained, broken, and patched, and proteins do the handling.

A new study has filmed a protein complex called Smc5/6 at work.

The ring-shaped complex swings open and shut, closes around DNA, and sometimes holds two DNA segments at once.

Kenichi Umeda and Noriyuki Kodera of the Nano Life Science Institute at Kanazawa University in Japan led the work with Yasuto Murayama of the National Institute of Genetics.

They watched purified protein from baker’s yeast, outside any cell, so what they saw is the machine running on a bench rather than inside a living nucleus.

“By observing individual Smc5/6 structures as they interacted with DNA, we could see how changes in their shape are associated with different ways of capturing and holding DNA,” Umeda said.

“These observations bring us closer to understanding how this protein assembly carries out its roles in maintaining chromosomes.”

Smc5/6 belongs to a small family of ring-shaped complexes that organize chromosomes, the bundles that carry a cell’s DNA.

Its job is clearing away DNA structures that would jam cell division, such as stalled copying machinery and strands twisted around each other. Cells need it to divide correctly.

Earlier methods gave either a frozen snapshot of its shape or a record of its movement with no structural detail. The Kanazawa team used a microscope that works by touch.

It drags a needle tip far finer than a hair across the sample and records a new image every 0.5 to 1.5 seconds.

The complex has two long arms connected by a hinge at one end and a pair of motor domains at the other. These motor domains break down ATP, the cell’s energy currency.

Lying flat, the complex measured about 38 nanometers from hinge to motors. A human hair is more than a thousand times wider.

Without fuel, more than 80% of the complexes had their arms close together, about 5 nanometers apart at the middle. The team called this shape the I-form.

Add ATP and some of them swung open into a ring, the O-form, with the arms 19 nanometers apart.

About 60% of complexes with a mutation that allows ATP binding but prevents its breakdown were open.

So binding the fuel opens the ring, and using it up lets the arms fall back together.

The team saw single complexes flip between the two shapes as they watched.

One small subunit, Nse2, is attached partway along one arm like a splint.

Without it, a few complexes bent their arms sharply toward each other, a shape never seen when Nse2 was present.

Other complexes in the same protein family are known to fold at an elbow this way, so the splint may be what keeps Smc5/6 straight.

Loading the complex onto a loop of DNA about 1,900 letters long required both ATP and a pair of helper subunits. Once on, the ring slid freely along the strand.

The team observed two ways the complex gripped DNA.

About half of the normal complexes held the strand at the hinge end, swaying as they did so. The rest gripped it at the motor end and stayed rigid, tilted at about 73 degrees to the DNA.

The mutant that can’t burn ATP almost always gripped at the motor end. Breaking down the fuel therefore appears to help move DNA from one compartment of the ring to the other.

The two grips were also associated with different DNA bends.

DNA held at the hinge curved by a median of 110 degrees at the contact point, while DNA held at the motor end bent less than 50 degrees.

When Smc5/6 was bound to a relaxed circle of DNA, the circle shrank.

The team measured how spread out each DNA loop was.

Loops carrying the protein were about 6% more compact on average, and loops the protein had pulled into a twisted shape were about 30% more compact.

The twisted loops more often carried more than one complex.

In 33 of 38 twisted loops with multiple complexes, two sections of DNA stayed pinned together for about 100 consecutive frames.

A separate test on magnetic beads confirmed it. With ATP present, the protein tethered about 25% of free DNA circles to DNA fixed on the beads, compared with under 3% without ATP.

Cutting either DNA circle once released the other, suggesting that the protein ring encircled both.

The mutant that can’t break down ATP tethered DNA just as well, suggesting that holding two DNA segments requires ATP binding but not its breakdown.

That sets tethering apart from a better-known trick of this protein family, reeling DNA into loops, which runs on a steady supply of ATP.

The experiments used purified yeast protein and bare DNA, without the crowding, other proteins, or ongoing repair activity found inside a real nucleus.

The authors wrote that their case for DNA being enclosed within a closed protein ring rests on several converging lines of evidence rather than a direct chemical proof.

The sharply bent form appeared in only 3% of complexes lacking Nse2.

The team said the microscope’s surface may have held the molecules too flat to show how often it really occurs. Nor can the imaging rule out that some closed-looking complexes were open rings standing on edge.

The next question is whether Nse2 does more than keep the arms straight. Does it also help control how the complex pulls DNA into loops?

The team will need to film the complex while it builds a loop to find out.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
