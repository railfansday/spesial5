One Tiny Protein Change Could Help Explain Severe Childhood Epilepsy

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Scientists have uncovered a previously unknown molecular mechanism that helps position a protein linked to severe childhood epilepsy within nerve cells.

A single change in a protein associated with severe childhood epilepsy can dramatically alter where it resides inside nerve cells, according to new research from the University of Liverpool. The discovery suggests that some neurological disorders may arise not only from impaired protein activity but also from proteins failing to reach the locations where they are needed.

The study focuses on CDKL5, a protein essential for normal brain development and function. Mutations in the gene that produces this protein cause CDKL5 deficiency disorder (CDD), a rare genetic condition characterized by severe seizures beginning in infancy and significant developmental difficulties. The disorder affects approximately 1 in 40,000 to 60,000 live births worldwide.

Despite years of research into CDKL5, scientists have struggled to understand how the protein is organized within nerve cells and why certain mutations have such devastating neurological consequences.

A research team led by Ben Goult, Professor of Mechanistic Cell Biology at the University of Liverpool, identified a previously unknown molecular docking mechanism that anchors CDKL5 to the structural framework of synapses, the specialized junctions through which nerve cells communicate.

Disrupting this anchoring mechanism altered the organization of nerve cells. The researchers also discovered that even a remarkably small modification to the protein could interfere with its positioning.

Dr. Yasumi Otani, first author of the study and former PhD student in the Institute of Systems, Molecular and Integrative Biology, said: “What was particularly striking was that changing just a single amino acid at the very end of CDKL5 was enough to dramatically alter its localization in nerve cells. This shows just how critical the final residues of the protein are for keeping CDKL5 in the right place at synapses.”

The researchers also uncovered an unexpected connection between CDKL5 and the mechanical forces generated within cells. CDKL5 binds directly to talin, a protein capable of changing its shape in response to physical forces.

In experiments using cultured cells, interfering with this molecular interaction or reducing the forces produced by the cells changed the distribution of CDKL5. The findings suggest that cellular mechanics may help regulate where CDKL5 carries out its biological functions.

Many genetic mutations associated with CDD produce shortened versions of the CDKL5 protein that lack the newly identified docking sequence. These mutations may therefore disrupt neurological function partly by preventing CDKL5 from reaching its intended location, rather than solely by impairing its enzymatic activity.

Professor Ben Goult said: “Much of the research into CDKL5 deficiency disorder has focused on the activity of the enzyme itself. Our work highlights another potentially crucial factor: where that activity takes place. By identifying the molecular interactions that position CDKL5, we can begin to understand how the protein is organized within synapses and why disrupting this organization might interfere with communication between nerve cells.”

The research, conducted in collaboration with scientists at the University of Helsinki in Finland, establishes a molecular framework for investigating how defective protein positioning contributes to CDD. It also opens a potential avenue for therapeutic research, although whether restoring CDKL5 localization could improve neurological function remains unknown.

Andreas Borg, founder of CURE5, a parent-led foundation dedicated to finding a cure for CDKL5 deficiency disorder, said: “Seeing how profoundly CDKL5 loss affects so many systems in the body also gives us hope that new opportunities for therapeutic intervention remain to be discovered. The new understanding of the protein’s location and function identified in this work opens up one such possibility. As parents, even the smallest improvements in our children’s lives mean the world.”

Reference: “Cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) binds talin and is anchored at the postsynaptic density by direct PDZ interactions” by Yasumi Otani, Emily Goodall, Vignesh Srinivasan, Neil J. Ball, Samuel F. H. Barnett, Youlim Hwang, Dominic P. Byrne, Tobias Zech, Juha Saarikangas and Benjamin T. Goult, 20 November 2026, Science Advances. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aed3338
