What limits human lifespan? Cloning offers a surprising clue

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

Cloned animals offer clues to aging, suggesting changes in gene activity may help explain limits on human lifespan.

Scientists can take the nucleus of an adult animal’s cell and use it to create a newborn. That clone can live a normal lifespan, despite starting with DNA from an older animal.

Two researchers in Poland think this offers a clue to aging. Cloning resets many controls that switch genes on and off, while leaving mutations in the DNA largely intact.

They suggest that problems with these controls may be the main limit on human lifespan. But fixing them might reveal another problem that keeps the body from living longer.

Michał Bartoszewicz, an independent researcher in Warsaw, wrote the review with Mariusz Niemczyk of the Medical University of Warsaw.

The team drew on earlier research, rather than running a new experiment.

Cloning involves putting an adult cell’s nucleus into an egg after removing the egg’s own nucleus.

Enzymes inside the egg help the transferred nucleus begin directing embryonic development. This is the method that produced Dolly the sheep.

Most cells in the body share the same DNA, but skin cells and nerve cells use different parts of it.

Chemical tags on DNA and its packaging help control this activity, a system called epigenetics.

Cloning resets these tags particularly thoroughly, but other changes vary.

Some clones have normal or longer telomeres, the protective caps on chromosomes, while others retain their donor cells’ short caps.

The clone gets most of its mitochondria, which supply energy, from the egg. It generally inherits mutations already present in the donor’s nuclear DNA.

The authors see permanent DNA damage as the hardest limit to undo, but doubt it explains most ordinary aging.

They cite 25 rounds of cloning from one genetic line that showed no sign of DNA damage shortening life.

Yet damage can eventually catch up. A separate 20-year experiment repeatedly cloned mice from clones, starting with one donor.

Success rates began falling after generation 27, and generation 58 was the last. Large structural errors and lethal mutations had accumulated in the DNA.

“Age-related dysfunction is unlikely to arise from a single universal mechanism,” the authors wrote.

Their framework groups aging’s limits into three layers: gene control, permanent DNA damage, and worn-out proteins and cell parts that escape the body’s cleanup systems. In humans, they place gene control first.

These controls affect cell identity, nutrient use, inflammation, hormone responses, and tissue repair, connecting with telomeres, mitochondria, and stem cells that renew tissue.

Senescent cells also play a part: they stop dividing without dying and release inflammatory signals that can harm nearby tissue.

The idea also draws on biologist Mikhail Blagosklonny’s work. He proposed that programs that help the body grow can cause harm when they keep running later in life.

A treatment may help only if it tackles the problem currently limiting a tissue’s survival. Once that problem improves, another may take its place.

Rapamycin illustrates this possibility. The drug can extend lifespan in normally aging mice when given late in life, partly by reducing a growth signal called mTOR.

In mice with a faulty DNA repair gene that age unusually fast, rapamycin reduced the signal but didn’t extend life. Cutting calories did.

Blagosklonny suggested that DNA damage may have killed these mice before excess growth signaling had time to matter. That explanation remains a proposed reason for the difference.

Scientists can also turn on three genes, OCT4, SOX2, and KLF4, to push cells toward a younger state. They call this partial reprogramming.

In one experiment, researchers delivered these genes through an engineered virus to male mice aged 124 weeks. Their median remaining lifespan more than doubled.

That means the time they had left, not their entire lifespan. Safety also remains a concern: a related set of four genes caused tumors in several organs in other mouse experiments.

Chemical tags called methyl groups form patterns on DNA that change with age. Epigenetic clocks read these patterns in blood or saliva to estimate biological age or the pace of aging.

The authors argue that current clocks mix together two different kinds of change. Some may drive aging, which they call Type 1, while others help the body respond to damage, called Type 2.

Undoing harmful changes could lower a clock reading and help the body. Suppressing helpful responses might lower the reading too, while making the body less able to cope.

Estimated age can rise after surgery, pregnancy, or infection, then fall during recovery.

Readings also follow a daily cycle, appearing youngest around midnight and oldest around noon.

Newer clocks try to separate harmful changes from helpful responses.

One reanalysis applied two such clocks to data from 34 overweight adults who took omega-3 supplements for six weeks.

The age estimate from the clock designed to track damage fell by 6.1 years.

The one tracking helpful responses rose by 6.2 years, and neither has been shown to predict health.

That makes a lower number hard to interpret on its own. It doesn’t necessarily mean someone has become healthier or will live longer.

The review covers more than a dozen human studies, from six women in an eight-week lifestyle program to 3,567 adults wearing activity trackers.

The two-year CALERIE trial assigned 220 healthy adults without obesity to cut calories by 25% or eat as usual.

Calorie cutting slowed DunedinPACE, one measure of aging speed, but didn’t clearly change several other clocks beyond chance.

In the three-year DO-HEALTH analysis of 777 older adults, omega-3 supplements slowed aging on three of four clocks.

Adding vitamin D and home exercise brought an extra benefit on one clock, but neither trial showed that participants lived longer.

One smaller study reported an average eight-year drop in estimated age among 42 people taking a supplement based on alpha-ketoglutarate.

But the test checked only nine DNA sites, and no independent group has verified it.

Nobody has tested the full framework or established how much each proposed limit contributes to ordinary human aging. It also doesn’t explain what starts aging or whether one process lies behind everything else.

Combining treatments, such as resetting gene control and clearing senescent cells, could be worth exploring. Both benefits and safety need direct testing.

Rather than chasing extreme lifespans first, “safety and healthspan should be the preliminary concerns,” the authors wrote. Healthspan means the years someone lives in good health.

They propose controlled trials of well-studied drugs and a free public database to help identify useful treatment combinations.

Long-term studies across diverse populations must also test whether lower clock readings predict longer, healthier lives.

The review is published in the journal Aging.
