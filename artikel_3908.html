Microsoft's SideWinder Strategic Commander was a failed attempt to reimagine RTS controls. 23 years later, why are some players still using it?

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

How an unconventional peripheral became an equally unexpected survivor

PC players have been conditioned for decades to accept the keyboard-and-mouse setup for RTS games. One hand has to handle movement and commands through a keyboard, while the other points, drags, and selects with a mouse. Peripheral makers have, usually with the addition of more buttons, made many minor variations to this time-honored arrangement, but there's only ever been one real attempt to rethink it entirely for the PC: the Microsoft SideWinder Strategic Commander.

Unveiled on March 1st 2000 at Microsoft's Gamestock event, the Strategic Commander was one of three additions to their SideWinder line, with a scheduled release that October at a pricey $64.95. At E3 later that May, Microsoft demonstrated the Strategic Commander for the first time with SimCity 3000, while promoting its potential for other such games as Diablo II, Warcraft III, and Baldur's Gate II. This was all taking place during the so-called 'Golden Age of Real-Time Strategy Games,' which spanned roughly from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s. It was when the RTS genre was at the peak of its mainstream popularity, and we fans were devoted to mastering its intricacies in any way we could to have a competitive edge.

Microsoft, all the while, were taking notes. "The team conceived the Strategic Commander in the late 1990s," Carl Ledbetter - then Industrial Design Manager for Microsoft's PC Hardware Group, now Head of Design for Xbox Devices - recalls to me. "As RTS games grew in popularity, we saw an interesting opportunity to design a new physical interface around a new interaction model. The idea was for it to be used left-handed in concert with right-hand PC desktop mouse control. We wanted to enable players to perform faster with more fluidity, comfort, optimised control placement for multiple hand sizes, simplicity of set-up, and ease of programming."

For Ledbetter and co. to realise this ambition, the Strategic Commander was designed as a unique combination of mouse, joystick, and gamepad. To manage the increasing complexities of the RTS games of the day, it came equipped with six programmable buttons, three shift buttons, and a three-position profile switch. Back then, these atypical features provided an unprecedented 72 possible key combinations across various configurations, all at the tip of our stubby little fingers. Better yet, it allowed us to toggle between three pre-configured profiles at a time, so that we could switch up our configurations on the fly depending on which stage of an RTS match we were in.

On release, contemporary reviewers acknowledged its originality, with critics like ActiveWin's Julien Jay describing it as "probably the most innovative (with the GameVoice) and revolutionary" peripheral designed specifically for strategy players. Others, like Neoseeker's Arnel Lim, suggested that it might’ve gone overboard with its customisation options, saying "you won't use anywhere close to all of them," in reference to the controller's programmable key combinations. Either way, the Strategic Commander failed to supplant conventional peripherals in the hands of most RTS players, and it was discontinued in 2003. As was the rest of the original SideWinder line, owing to declining sales and the industry-wide shift toward standard M+K setups for all PC games and genres.

However, unlike so many of its Microsoft-made siblings, this was not the end for the Strategic Commander. Niche as it was, it went on to enjoy a surprisingly long post-shelf life, one that was stretched out by a dedicated cult following - and that continues to this day. But why? Why, after all these years, is this forgotten peripheral from a discontinued line maintaining such a enthusiastic fanbase, years after the industry around it had written it off as a failure?

The answer lies in software and OS compatibility, as much as the hardware's own appeal. Andreas Weis was the developer of one of the first unofficial patches for the Strategic Commander, allowing players to keep on using it even after the original software stopped being compatible with newer versions the Windows. By that point, the device was a decade old, and well into 'official' obsolescence, yet Weis still answered the call of the Strategic Commander's righteously frustrated - if numerically few - fans. "There was a lot of interest back in the early 2010s in software patches to keep the Strategic Commander functional when the original software for the Strategic Commander stopped working," he explains. "Users were asking around on internet forums about how to get it to work on Windows 7 and Vista machines. Since I was studying computer science at the time, I was quite interested in reverse-engineering, and I hacked together a simple tool that would cover my use cases for the device."

In the years that followed, other patches were released to keep the Strategic Commander operational, the most notable of which was Philip Merwarth’s Strategic Engine software in 2013. Unfortunately, they weren’t open-source. Worse was that the protocol for programming the Strategic Commander wasn’t documented anywhere online in a way that was accessible for users. This is why Weis realized that he could make a unique contribution.

"I polished my old prototype code for the Strategic Commander software patch and released it as a library, so that other people could use it to write their own applications for programming the tool," he says. "As for me, I released one application that made use of the library publicly, the vJoy feeder application, which allows the device to be used with games that support joystick inputs." Curiously, Weis, by this time and like many others, had found other applications for a device with such a specific selling point, and even continues to use the Strategic Commander sparingly in 2026.

"The production quality of Microsoft hardware at the time was just top-notch, and I love the design of the old SideWinder devices, which is why I did not just throw the thing out when the software stopped working," he tells me. "It was worth keeping around to find new uses for it over the years, and I eventually realized that it makes quite a nice addition to a HOTAS (Hands-On Throttle And Stick) setup, where I could put actions that aren’t time-critical and don't fit well on the throttle on the Strategic Commander instead. For example, when I’m playing Elite Dangerous in VR, I’d put actions like deploying the landing gear on the Strategic Commander to free up buttons on the throttle for actions that were more relevant for combat."

When looking back at the history of Microsoft’s SideWinder line, it’s easy to call the Strategic Commander a failed experiment, given the fact that most of us are still using the same standard mouse-and-keyboard setup to play RTS games. However, the central premise behind its design philosophy was that the interface between a player and a game could be redesigned to cater to specific genre quirks, and decades following its release, it’s still maintained a coalition of loyal users: some RTS players who believed the Strategic Commander really pulled it off, and those found other uses for the device in other flavours of PC game. And with the issue of modern compatibility, a roadblock for so much retro hardware, bypassed by homemade patches, both camps can continue twisting and clicking their Strategic Commanders unimpeded.

All the same, I wonder if it might have basked in more conventional success, had Microsoft made more forward-thinking decisions. The Strategic Commander has proven over the years that its unprecedented customisation had applications far beyond what Ledbetter’s team had envisioned, and once the popularity of RTS games settled down, other, similar click-heavy subgenres like MOBAs and Real-Time Tactics got big as well. Would a more officially maintained, potentially refined Strategic Commander model have served those crowds? I think it might. The narrow focus on RTS games may have also hurt the device's since-demonstrated broader appeal; if Microsoft had fully capitalised on this customisation selling point by communicating its benefits to everyone, not just those playing the single most popular PC genre at the time, it could have provided the blueprint for a proper, long-lived alternative to controllers, mice, and keyboards.

Alas, gaming history is littered with revolutionary peripherals that failed to actually revolutionise the industry surrounding them. The Strategic Commander is no different, but for the fact that it’s had this surprisingly long post-shelf life - and, as a result, the opportunity for insight into what could have been done to salvage the its failures. Still, that unexpected longevity can provide come comfort to the Strategic Commander's creators, as well as its lingering users.

"I think the design made sense for its time, especially with the rising popularity of RTS games," Ledbetter reflects. "Looking back, I would have liked more time to test and refine the Strategic Commander. I’m proud of what we brought to market, and I’m glad to know that it remains beloved by many players to this day."
