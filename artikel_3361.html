10 Classic Rock Songs That Are Perfect for Thanksgiving, Ranked

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

As far as music goes, Thanksgiving gets the short end of the stick. Christmas has its carols and Mariah Carey, Easter has countless hymns, St. Patrick's Day has pub-drinking songs. Thanksgiving has, well, what exactly? There's only so many rhymes for "turkey," after all. But the theme of the holiday is being with family and friends, giving thanks for the many wonderful things – and even the not so wonderful – that we've been blessed with over the years. With that in mind, the pool of music suddenly becomes much larger, like the turkey pants you have at the ready. Here are 10 of the best.

Arlo Guthrie's "Alice's Restaurant Massacree" is one of only two songs that are legitimately about Thanksgiving. Only Guthrie's 18-plus minute tune is about much, much more. The story behind it begins on Thanksgiving Day in 1965, when 18-year-old Guthrie and a friend were staying in the home, formerly a Trinity Church, of Ray and Alice Brock in Stockbridge, Massachusetts. A number of guests were coming for dinner, so the pair offered to haul a big pile of garbage accumulated over time in a Volkswagen microbus. That rather innocuous task sparked a turn of events that would be recounted in Guthrie's comically absurd, largely true opus, recounted in a deadpan blues song. There's also an element of protest against the Vietnam War draft and bureaucracy for good measure.

Kiss is no stranger to songs about perseverance, with tracks like "Crazy Crazy Nights" and "I," the latter from the much-maligned Music From "The Elder." Then there's "God Gave Rock and Roll to You II," the band's contribution to the Bill & Ted's Bogus Journey soundtrack. It is, in a nutshell, a song about being thankful for music, a gift that is there to help one persevere over life's hardships, a gift for everyone. Paul Stanley's vocals are engagingly upbeat, as always with songs like these, and sell the hope behind it. As you might suspect, if there's a II there must be a I, and so there is, from Argent in 1973. Only Kiss modified the lyrics to the point that "II" seemed more appropriate. Fun fact: there's also a 1.5 (I.V?), with Christian rock band Petra covering it in 1977.

ZZ Top's cover of Sam & Dave's "I Thank You" is as simple a Thanksgiving track as you're going to get, a straightforward expression of thankfulness, with lyrics like “You didn't have to love me like you did / But you did, but you did / And I thank you.” Being ZZ Top, the sentiment is told in that contained driving, Texas blues-rock sound that is distinctly their own. Being ZZ Top, the lyrics definitely skew towards innuendo, not quite as family-friendly as they are upon first listen. You've been warned.

A standout track from the 1997 album Nine Lives, "Full Circle" is an impossibly catchy song from Aerosmith about making peace with the past, being grateful for the journey, and valuing the time and people you're with right now. It has a pub sing-a-long vibe to it, one that calls for a toast to those very things: "Raise your drinking glass / Here's to yesterday." Here's to "Full Circle."

Arguably the least Led Zeppelin-sounding track on Led Zeppelin II, "Thank You" is a quiet, mellow acoustic number that is a complete 180 from the heavier tracks on the record. Written by Robert Plant as a touching homage to his then-wife Maureen, "Thank You" is filled with gratitude for love, trials and tribulations; honest reflections on the past and present. There's an optimism to it that, unfortunately, wouldn't last, given the couple would divorce 14 years later. Thankfully, they remained friends.

Full disclosure: there's nothing about The Beatles' "Come Together" that expresses any feelings of thanks and gratitude, short of its plea to "come together." Hey, whatever gets the Beatles on a top 10 list of any sort works. "Come Together" has a slow, blues-rock vibe, which pulls together a litany of odd, surreal lyrics (seriously – "He wear no shoeshine, he got toe-jam football / He got monkey finger, he shoot Coca-Cola"). But at Thanksgiving, family and friends do "come together," and presumably give thanks for the Beatles.

"Drove my Chevy to the levee, but the levee was dry."

Thanksgiving is a time to show people you love and care for them, to be thankful for togetherness, to focus on both "thanks" and "giving." Supertramp's "Give a Little Bit" is about taking the time to show people you love and care for them, to be thankful for togetherness, to focus on both "thanks" and "giving." Doesn't get simpler than that. And it doesn't come across as a directive, but rather as a shared, communal effort between band and listener to harvest connections, in a warm, acoustic feel.

Sly and the Family Stone's "Thank You (Fallettinme Be Mice Elf Agin)" ("thank you for letting me be myself again") is a funky cry of gratitude for finding one's authenticity, one's "self," with the love, acceptance and support that comes from loved ones. It's an earned gratitude, one of defiance that says, even amid the societal tensions of the late 1960s, I am still who I am, and grateful that hasn't been lost. The song is also the first time Larry Graham utilizes his famed slap-bass technique ("thumpin' and pluckin'"), and if there's anything one should be thankful for, that's it.

We've gone through 8 songs to this point without mentioning the one thing that Thanksgiving is really about: food, and lots of it. And while tradition dictates that pumpkin pie is the dessert of choice, might we suggest "Cherry Pie" instead? Warrant's infamous "Cherry Pie" is about the dessert in the same way that Aerosmith's "Big Ten-Inch Record" is about a vinyl record. Still, it's one of the greatest hair-metal anthems of all time, and Grandma doesn't need to know of its rather saucy intent, right?

We'll wrap up this list with a "true" Thanksgiving song, and we'll end it on one: Adam Sandler's "The Thanksgiving Song." Sandler introduced the song during the Weekend Update segment of Saturday Night Live's 18th season in November 1992. It is, more or less, a love letter to turkey, in Sandler's semi-falsetto manner, with lyrics expressing how he "loves to eat turkey" and "I wish turkey only cost a nickel." And in between, well, it's Adam Sandler. Nothing in the song makes any real sense whatsoever: "Wrap the turkey up in aluminum foil / My brother likes to masturbate with baby oil"; "Love to eat the turkey at the table / I once saw a movie with Betty Grable"; and so on, referencing Daryl Strawberry, Elvis, Sammy Davis Jr., and his big brown shoe. Inane, yes, but then Thanksgiving should be filled with laughter (which I only thought of after).
