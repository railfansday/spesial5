7 US work permit changes in 2026 and the immigrant groups they hit

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Work permits now expire sooner and no longer stay valid during renewals. See how 3 rule changes affect 4 immigrant groups in 2026.

For many immigrants in the U.S., the right to hold a job depends on a work permit card that has to be renewed, and for years renewal was a routine chore rather than a threat to their income. That changed at the end of 2025, when the federal government put two separate rules into effect within weeks of each other. USAGov reports that since Oct. 30, 2025, a pending renewal no longer keeps a worker's expired card in force. Before that date, a worker could stay on the job while waiting for a replacement card. A second rule, which took effect before the year was out, made the card itself expire sooner. As a result, 2026 is the first year in which workers renew under both rules at once, and every renewal becomes a point where a paycheck can stop.

Many workers haven't felt the change yet, because neither rule reaches back to people already in the system. Renewals filed before Oct. 30, 2025, are not affected, according to E-Verify. El Cronista reports that a worker who already holds a five-year card can keep using it through the date printed on it. Workers will therefore lose the old protections at different times through 2026 and beyond, as each person's card comes due. When a worker filed matters as much as what kind of immigration status they hold. Two people doing the same job with the same status can face very different risks this year, depending on where each one stands in the renewal cycle.

The American Immigration Council identified which 2026 work permit changes hit which immigrant groups, from people waiting on green cards to H-4 spouses and holders of Temporary Protected Status. The same change can mean little to one group and cost another its income. People who already live on the shortest and least secure terms have the least room to absorb a delay, so a missed renewal does them the most damage.

Workers renewing a work permit no longer have a safety margin while they wait for the government to act. Until late 2025, workers in certain categories could keep working for up to 540 days past the expiration date on their card while a renewal was pending, according to the American Immigration Council. That period was long enough to cover most government delays. The Department of Homeland Security ended the grace period on Oct. 30, 2025, and for a covered renewal, the expiration date printed on the card is now the last day of legal work unless the new card is approved first.

Workers and employers had no time to prepare. DHS announced the rule on Oct. 29, 2025, E-Verify reports, and it took effect the next day. The rule came out as an interim one, with a final rule expected to follow. Workers should not plan on the change going away.

The burden falls on employers as well as workers, because the employer has to verify that each employee can legally work. A receipt showing that a renewal is pending does not prove a worker's authorization, E-Verify reports, and a business cannot keep someone on the job just because the application is in. When a decision comes late, the employer loses an experienced worker for as long as the government takes to decide, through no fault of either side.

For anyone renewing under this rule, the question is no longer whether they filed on time but whether the government decides on time.

A work permit used to be a document that some immigrants could file away and largely forget for years at a time. For several categories of workers, the card now lasts no more than 18 months, down from a maximum of five years before the change, according to the American Immigration Council. Workers in these categories now go well under two years between renewals instead of several, and they have to plan around the card far more often. Renewing the permit is no longer an occasional errand but a regular obligation on a worker's calendar.

The new limits are not the same for every worker. Some categories now receive cards valid for up to 18 months, while others receive cards valid for only one year. A worker in the one-year group must renew every single year, and the work on the next application begins soon after the last one is finished. Each worker's schedule depends on which of the two limits applies to that worker's category.

The shorter card is most dangerous in combination with the end of automatic extensions. By itself, a shorter card would mostly mean more paperwork. Apart from a few limited exceptions, workers can no longer keep their jobs on an expired card while a renewal is pending, so for most of them every renewal carries the chance of a stretch without legal permission to work. More frequent renewals give workers more of those chances to fall into a gap. Employers who rely on these workers now have to keep up with the same shorter schedule, and they face the possibility of losing staff at each renewal.

Workers in these categories should treat the expiration date on their card as a standing deadline to watch, not a distant date to remember later.

The American Immigration Council reports that U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) has issued a new edition of Form I-765 and added stricter screening, which makes renewing a work permit less of a formality. No card gets shorter and no worker loses authorization under this change alone. With tighter background checks and wider discretionary review, approval depends more on an officer's judgment and less on filing the right paperwork. That uncertainty carries more weight now that a pending renewal no longer keeps a worker on the job.

Under proposals still under discussion, people whose status is already the least secure would face the most added scrutiny. Tougher requirements for people on parole or deferred action are under consideration, though officials have only suggested or argued over them so far, and none of the proposals is final.

Applicants should plan around the rules in force today and keep watching the proposals.

People waiting on a green card are partway to permanent residence, and their work permit is what keeps them on a payroll while the case is pending. With the 540-day buffer gone, an applicant whose renewal is still pending when the card expires has no legal right to work, even though nothing about the green card case itself has changed, according to the American Immigration Council. These applicants also face that risk on a shorter cycle, because their cards are now capped at 18 months.

The federal government's general guidance does not tell these applicants plainly whether the new rules apply to them. "Check for updates from the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) to find out if this new rule affects your case," USAGov advised. The page was revised on March 17, 2026, but what it says about how long a card lasts may not reflect the shorter limits that took effect in December 2025, so an applicant who relies on it could expect a card to last longer than it will.

The safer course is to assume the stricter rules apply and plan each renewal as a possible gap in pay.

People whose place in the U.S. rests on a claim of protection now have to renew their right to work again and again, whatever stage their case has reached. The group is wider than the word "asylum" suggests. Alongside asylum applicants, refugees and asylees, the changes reach people seeking withholding of removal, cancellation of removal or relief under the Nicaraguan Adjustment and Central American Relief Act (NACARA), according to El Cronista. Work permits for everyone in this group now last no more than 18 months, according to the American Immigration Council, including refugees and asylees whom the government has already recognized.

The real danger for this group is that a card now expires on its printed date whether or not the replacement has arrived. Without the old extensions, a person whose renewal is still pending when the card runs out loses legal permission to work. That gap comes from processing time alone, not from any finding against the person or the case. Someone can file on time and do everything right, then still have to stop working until the government decides. Employers who rely on workers in this group should track their renewal dates rather than assume the card covers them.

H-4 spouses who renew their work permits can now lose the legal right to work on the day the old card expires if the renewal is still undecided. Under the old rule, a spouse could keep working for up to 540 days past the expiration date printed on the card, according to E-Verify, and could file a renewal without watching the date on the old one. Without the extension, the same wait now means time off the job and time without pay.

For H-4 spouses this year, the question is not whether they remain eligible to work but whether they receive the new card before the old one expires.

Holders of Temporary Protected Status and humanitarian parolees face the strictest form of each new work permit limit, because their right to stay in the U.S. already has an end date. Their work permits last one year at most, TPS designations for certain countries are being terminated, and they have limited exceptions to the end of automatic extensions, according to the American Immigration Council. These workers face all three pressures at the same time: a short card, little cushion when it expires, and a status the government can end. At each yearly renewal, the government decides again whether they can keep working legally.

A one-year card is the longest these workers can get, and some may get a shorter one. The permit lasts one year or until the person's status or the country's designation ends, whichever comes first. A worker whose country's designation ends within the year therefore holds a card that ends with it. With TPS being terminated for certain countries, some workers from those countries hold cards backed by protection that is running out. For this group, the work permit cannot outlast the status behind it. The less secure a person's status, the more their income depends on deadlines they do not control.

Anyone in these groups should track two dates: when the card expires and when the protection behind it ends.

New asylum seekers could also wait longer for their first permit under a proposal that has not taken effect. It would allow a waiting period of up to 365 days before an asylum seeker can receive a work permit. If officials adopt it, asylum seekers would wait longer for a first card and then hold it for a shorter time. The rules already in force tighten the end of the process, and the proposal would tighten the start as well.

People in this group and their employers should watch the proposal while planning now around the 18-month limit that already applies.
