More closings can mean less profit; the velocity illusion for builders

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The velocity illusion is when volume and revenue growth mask declining marginal returns. MoviePass, Katerra, and Zillow Offers show how scaling negative unit economics can accelerate losses.  AI Summary

One number goes missing from most corporate growth stories. Not revenue. Not market share. Not units sold. The missing metric? What does the next unit actually earn?

Homebuilding executives love closings. Wall Street loves growth. Boards celebrate scale. But a closing is not a profit, revenue is not a return, and becoming the fifth-largest builder doesn’t necessarily make you the fifth-best business.

A company can sell more homes, acquire more land, and consume more capital while earning less on every additional closing. Management calls it momentum. Shareholders may eventually discover it was deterioration.

That’s the velocity illusion: movement mistaken for progress.

Scale multiplies whatever you already are. If the underlying business creates economic value, growth can amplify it. If the underlying business destroys value, growth can accelerate the destruction.

Three companies learned that lesson the expensive way: MoviePass, Katerra, and Zillow Offers. Two operated directly in housing. Together, they turned billions of dollars of investor capital into volume that never earned its keep. All three offer a warning for homebuilders.

The more customers used the product, the more money the company lost. Over the first nine months of 2018, Helios and Matheson reported $205.0 million in revenue and $424.4 million in cost of revenue – about $2.07 spent for every $1 collected, before any overhead. The gross loss was $219.4 million.

To fund the gap, the company kept issuing stock. Its share count rose more than 3,000% in a year. In July 2018, it executed a 1-for-250 reverse split to stay listed on Nasdaq. By the following year, the stock had lost more than 99.99% of its value from its late-2017 peak.

The company expected to report a 2018 net loss attributable to shareholders of approximately $266.8 million. Revenue fell from $234 million in 2018 to $24 million in 2019. In January 2020, MoviePass and its parent filed for Chapter 7 liquidation.

MoviePass had demonstrated demand, but not profitable demand. Customers weren’t necessarily expressing loyalty to a differentiated experience. They were accepting a subsidy.

MoviePass wasn’t scaling a viable subscription model. It was scaling a subsidy.

Katerra set out to industrialize construction by integrating design, engineering, procurement, manufacturing, logistics and installation. The theory was seductive: control the whole process, remove fragmentation, and win through scale.

It raised close to $3 billion in equity, more than $2 billion of it from SoftBank, and was valued at more than $4 billion.

In December 2019, CEO Michael Marks said annual revenue had doubled to roughly $1.7 billion and headcount had grown from 6,000 to 8,000. That figure was management’s public description, not an audited revenue figure. The same week, Katerra confirmed it was closing a 250,000-square-foot factory in Phoenix and laying off 200 workers.

Bankruptcy filings later showed $2.78 billion in losses across 2018, 2019, and 2020. Measured against the equity raised, Katerra lost roughly 93 cents of every dollar investors put in. Its own chief transformation officer described projects with “significant cost overruns, resulting in massive, ongoing losses.”

In December 2020, SoftBank provided another $200 million rescue – $175 million in cash and $25 million in loan forgiveness. It wasn’t enough. Katerra filed for Chapter 11 in June 2021 with about 6,400 employees, estimating liabilities of $1 billion to $10 billion against assets of $500 million to $1 billion.

Projects won at guaranteed prices and steep discounts became economically destructive. Sophisticated manufacturing infrastructure could not compensate for weak project economics.

A factory doesn’t produce economies of scale merely by existing. Average unit costs must actually decline enough to support the facilities, equipment, fixed payroll, logistics, and capital it requires.

Traditional field construction has inefficiencies, but subcontractor capacity can adjust when a local market slows. An expensive factory is less flexible. When its volume disappears, the overhead stays.

Katerra built the apparatus of national scale before proving that the next project would reliably earn money.

Three billion dollars in equity did not change that equation.

Zillow possessed extraordinary housing data: transaction histories, consumer traffic, pricing algorithms and national market intelligence. Zillow Offers attempted to translate those advantages into a large-scale homebuying and resale business.

In Q3 2021, it purchased 9,680 homes but sold 3,032. It ended the quarter holding 9,790 homes, with another 8,172 under contract. Inventory accumulated faster than the business could renovate and sell it.

Zillow recorded a $304.4 million inventory write-down that quarter. Zillow Offers posted a negative gross profit of about $245 million – an average loss of $80,771 per home sold, nearly 21% of revenue. The figures reflect segment accounting, including write-downs on homes not yet sold, not a uniform cash loss on every completed sale.

That distinction is the point. Excluding those write-downs, Zillow’s average return on the homes it actually sold that quarter was still positive, about $9,183 before interest. The completed sales looked fine. The damage was sitting in the homes it had just bought.

For the full year, the Homes segment generated $6.0 billion in revenue and lost $881.5 million before taxes, on 15,436 homes sold. That’s roughly $57,000 lost for every home Zillow sold in 2021. Inventory write-downs totaled $407.9 million, and wind-down charges recognized in 2021 reached $491.2 million.

Zillow shut the operation down and cut about 25% of its workforce, roughly 2,000 jobs. Its shares fell 25% the day after the announcement. By then, the company’s market value had dropped $30.5 billion from its February 2021 peak.

The failure involved pricing errors, renovation capacity, inventory exposure and changing market conditions. But the central lesson is familiar to anyone who has underwritten land.

A national average cannot tell you everything about a particular house. The block, drainage, school line, street noise, lot orientation or odd floor plan can change what a buyer will pay.

Data can inform local judgment. It cannot replace it.

Zillow could buy houses very quickly. That did not mean it could buy, improve, and resell each one at a profit.

Each company could point to a growing number. None could show that the next unit made money.

Consider a hypothetical builder selling homes for $400,000 each. It expands into new markets, layers on management, standardizes products and chases closings. Volume rises, but incentives and complexity gradually consume the margins.

The company increases closings fivefold. Revenue rises from $400 million to $2 billion. Yet annual operating profit moves from $48 million to a $40 million loss.

That calculation excludes the additional land, development, construction inventory, and financing required to support the growth. The company is bigger. Its economics are worse. The point is not that growth always reduces margins. Closings alone cannot reveal whether growth creates value.

An important distinction exists between high fixed overhead and negative contribution margins.

If an additional sale contributes profit before fixed overhead, increased volume may legitimately improve total earnings. But suppose the directly attributable costs are $405,000 for a house that sells for $400,000. Each incremental closing loses $5,000 before any excluded overhead.

One thousand closings destroy $5 million. Five thousand destroy $25 million. Ten thousand destroy $50 million.

That isn’t a theoretical exercise. MoviePass spent about $2.07 to deliver each dollar of revenue. Zillow’s Homes segment lost roughly $57,000 for every home it sold in 2021 – more than 10 times the $5,000 example.

In homebuilding, timing amplifies the consequences. Capital is committed to land, entitlements, infrastructure, construction, interest and property taxes long before the closing proceeds arrive. A bad land assumption can be repeated across an entire community.

A $5,000 mistake repeated across 10,000 homes is $50 million. Scale multiplies mistakes just as efficiently as savings.

And averages can conceal the marginal problem. A builder can report respectable historical margins while the last homes sold—those discounted to meet a quarterly target—generate almost nothing. Zillow’s completed sales showed a positive average return in the same quarter its newest inventory was written down by $304 million.

Imagine a national builder acquires a successful local operator. Corporate purchasing eliminates product variations and saves $2,000 per home. But the standardized offering is less attractive to local buyers, so sales teams add $10,000 in incentives to maintain velocity.

Purchasing reports success. Sales reports more closings. Corporate reports higher revenue. The underlying economics have deteriorated by $8,000 per home, before other effects. Across 5,000 homes, that’s $40 million.

This is illustrative, but the management trap is real. Departments optimize the numbers they own while no one is accountable for the total return on the capital invested.

The purchasing department may celebrate a saving while the field absorbs delays, rework and incentive costs. Those expenses don’t vanish; they are recorded somewhere else. Management celebrates what it can count while weakening what creates economic value.

Closings are an activity measure. The scoreboard should follow the economics. What did the marginal home earn after incentives, rate buydowns, construction costs, expected warranty obligations and rework? What return did the latest community produce on its invested capital? Did the division generate cash after funding its land and inventory needs? Did average cost per comparable home actually fall as volume increased?

And how much demand would exist without the incentive? Discounting finished inventory can be a rational way to release trapped capital. But a temporary inventory decision is not the same as a business model that permanently depends on subsidies to sustain volume.

One is capital management. The other is a warning.

The velocity illusion is most dangerous when growth comes through acquisition. A homebuilder that buys local operators is paying for distinct judgment, brands, and customer relationships. If it then places one acquired company’s systems over the rest, it should be able to prove that those systems improve the businesses beneath them. A larger platform isn’t proof. Neither are awards, rankings, or a higher closing count.

The evidence is in the numbers most companies don’t publish: division-level profitability, return on invested capital, community-level land economics, and how much purchasing efficiency survives after incentives, carrying costs, lost product differentiation, and operating disruption.

And owners should ask what happened to the local talent and judgment they paid to acquire.

Difficult markets, interest rates, incentives, product mix, and geography all influence results. They can explain a bad year. They cannot, by themselves, prove an integration created value.

If an operating philosophy is making strong local businesses less distinctive or less profitable, the owner should change the operating structure – not sacrifice the assets that justified the acquisitions. Patience is a virtue. Patience without financial accountability can become an extraordinarily expensive mistake.

Before celebrating another thousand homes, prove what the next one earns. Before entering another market, prove the existing markets generate acceptable returns. Before giving one acquired company authority over the rest, prove that its methods improve their economics rather than erase their advantages.

The opportunity in American homebuilding was never merely to become larger. It is to combine product excellence with entrepreneurship, local land judgment and trusted brands. That opportunity is still available. But it requires evidence – not optimism—that the operating model works.

Scale is not a strategy. It’s an amplifier. If the unit creates value, scale can multiply the success. If the unit destroys value, scale industrializes the loss.
