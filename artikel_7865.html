You know your dog better than anyone else, and if you think something is wrong, it probably is. Any dog with a uterus can develop a uterine infection, and, although rare, even dogs that have had a laparoscopic or âkeyholeâ spay, where only the ovaries are removed, can be at risk of pyometra. So, what is pyometra? Read on to find out.

An infected uterus or womb, sometimes referred to in practice as a âpyoâ, is a relatively common condition affecting entire female dogs. It occurs most commonly in middle-aged to older dogs, but cases have been reported in dogs as young as 9 months.

In dogs, the uterus splits into two branches or âhornsâ that allow multiple fetuses to be carried on each side. Female dogs come into season or estrus around every six months once they reach sexual maturity. This is when she becomes fertile and receptive to mating. If she becomes pregnant, all being well, she will deliver a litter of puppies approximately 63 days later. Sometimes there can be a âphantomâ or âfalseâ pregnancy, also known as pseudopregnancy, where the fluctuating hormones surrounding estrus and mating prepare the dog for pregnancy, even when fertilization hasnât occurred.

Every time a dog goes through estrus, her cervix relaxes and opens, and the lining of the uterus thickens in preparation for mating and pregnancy. Ordinarily, the cervix helps prevent dirt, debris, and harmful bacteria from entering the uterus, so when the cervix is open, the risk of contamination and infection increases. If fluid and bacteria accumulate, infection can set in over the following 2-3 weeks, resulting in pyometra.

There are two types of pyometra: open and closed.

The more common and less dangerous of the two, an open pyometra occurs when there is infection in the uterus, but the cervix remains open. Dogs with an open pyometra are often diagnosed more easily because owners notice a foul-smelling discharge coming from the vulva, or that their dog becomes obsessed with licking her nether regions.

Because the pus can drain out of the uterus, an open pyometra tends to be less of a threat than one that is closed. However, an open pyometra can become closed, and vice versa.

A closed pyometra occurs when the cervix has shut, locking infection inside the body. It is a far more dangerous situation because the problem is not always immediately obvious, and, as the infection continues, pus fills and stretches the uterus. This can bring a risk of septicemia (infection of the bloodstream) and uterine rupture, both of which carry a high risk of mortality.

The signs of pyometra usually appear around 2-3 weeks after estrus. As mentioned above, dogs with an open pyometra may have a yellow, greenish, or brown discharge from the vulva which, if you get close enough to discover, smells pretty awful. With each season a dog experiences, her risk of uterine infection increases. At each estrus, the cervix opens, and the repeated thickening of the uterine wall reduces its ability to contract and expel fluid and contaminants. Dogs over 7 years old are typically at higher risk, but pyometra can affect even young dogs.

The most common bacteria involved in pyometra is Escherichia coli (E. coli), responsible for around 90% of cases, usually from fecal contamination.1 Obesity, recessed vulvas, chronic diarrhea, and thick coats that regularly become soiled increase the incidence of fecal contamination of the vagina and uterus, putting dogs at a much greater risk for pyometra.

Studies have also shown that diet can affect the growth of pathogenic E. coli and therefore influence pyometra risk.2

The use of synthetic hormones to prevent embryo implantation or induce abortion in the case of accidental mating, or to suppress or delay estrus, has also been found to increase the risk of pyometra.3

Your vet will diagnose pyometra based on a combination of the history, clinical signs, and examination. They will usually perform a brief abdominal ultrasound. Pyometra appears as fluid inside the uterine horns, visible on ultrasound as discrete black circles or tubes.

They will also perform some bloodwork to check for signs of septicemia and assess kidney function, hydration status, and rule out any other underlying issues.

The fastest and most definitive treatment of pyometra is to perform an ovariohysterectomy â spay the dog.

The first step in treating pyometra is to start antibiotic therapy and intravenous fluids. Depending on the severity of the situation, your vet may recommend that your dog stay overnight on these treatments before proceeding with surgery, or they may want to go ahead right away.

Occasionally, mild cases of pyometra can be successfully managed using a combination of hormonal treatments and antibiotics. However, dogs that have had one episode of pyometra have a much higher chance of having another, which is why surgery is usually recommended. Once the ovaries and uterus have been removed, a dog can never get pyometra again.

The surgical removal of the ovaries and uterus will completely eliminate the risk of pyometra. Dogs that are spayed earlier in life also have a significantly lower risk of mammary cancer.

In the last decade, advice regarding the timing of spaying has changed, and most vets recommend waiting until after the first season to ensure she has reached sexual maturity. For small breeds, the first season might occur at around 6 months, while for large or giant breeds, the first season may not happen until they are at least 12-18 months old.

In a keyhole or laparoscopic spay, only the ovaries are removed (ovariectomy); the uterus is left behind. This has become a popular option due to the perceived reduction in pain, discomfort, and recovery time. However, when performed on young, healthy dogs, the most challenging part of a routine spay is trying to convince the patient to stay quiet and calm after surgery!

Theoretically, by removing the hormonal factor, ovariectomy should prevent pyometra. However, in the case of ovarian remnant syndrome, where fragments of ovarian tissue are left behind, uterine infection can occur.

Breeding from a dog that has (or has had) pyometra is not recommended. The circumstances that allow pyometra to form â open cervix, thickened uterine walls, contamination â can also make pregnancy riskier for the dog.

Additionally, pyometra can damage the uterine lining, reducing fertility and increasing pregnancy risks.

Before surgical spaying became common practice, allowing dogs to mate and have litters was likely seen as the best way to avoid pyometra. This is probably where the old wivesâ tale that âyou should let your dog have puppiesâ originated. These days, with millions of unwanted puppies in need of homes, and surgical sterilization being a relatively safe routine procedure, there is no longer any real justification behind the idea that dogs should have a litter.

A dog is never too old to get a pyometra, so even if your dog is considered elderly, unless they have health conditions that make surgery or anesthesia too much of a risk, itâs far better to have them spayed when theyâre healthy rather than risk them getting a pyometra in old age.
