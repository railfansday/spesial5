Joining a Union – Weigh the Dues Against What the Contract Adds

Before you join a union or vote for one, compare a year of dues with what the contract would add in pay, health coverage, and job protection over nonunion jobs near you. Then check whether you’d have to pay at all, since no one can require dues in a right-to-work state or a government job.

At a private company, once at least 30% of a group of workers back a petition, the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) holds an election, and a union that wins a majority of the votes cast represents them all. The union then negotiates a contract over pay, benefits, and discipline, and members’ dues fund that work.

What a union adds depends on the job. Full-time union members in transportation and material moving jobs earned a median $1,174 a week in 2025, against $866 for nonunion workers in the same jobs, the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports. In retail, union members earned slightly less than nonunion workers, $908 a week against $913. BLS cautions that age, employer size, and region also shape these gaps.

Each union sets its own dues, often 1% to 2% of pay or a few hours’ wages a month, and some add a one-time initiation fee. Compare that with what the contract adds over nonunion jobs near you: pay rates and raises, your health premiums, a retirement plan, and a grievance process that lets the union challenge a firing.

Say you work 40 hours a week in a warehouse and dues run $50 a month, or $600 a year. A contract paying $2 an hour more than nonunion warehouses nearby adds $4,160 a year before taxes, several times the dues even after taxes. Employer-paid family health coverage would also save you the $7,471 that workers paid toward it on average in 2026, research group KFF found. If the contract matched nonunion pay and benefits, the $600 would buy mainly job protection.

In right-to-work states, you choose whether to join and pay dues, and the contract covers you either way, the NLRB says. Public-sector unions can’t charge workers who don’t agree to pay, under the Supreme Court’s 2018 Janus ruling. Ask, too, whether the union has a strike fund and what it pays, since a strike stops your paycheck.

If your workplace is still organizing, ask the union for its contracts at similar employers nearby and run the same math on those.
