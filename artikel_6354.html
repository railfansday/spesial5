What keeps people recycling for decades? One town offers clues

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

A Japanese study found that stronger neighborhood ties and wider social connections were linked to higher recycling participation.

Most recycling programs demand the same daily effort for years to come. They need rules and bins to get off the ground, but the factors that keep people sorting after decades have received less attention.

Osaki, a town in southern Japan, offers a test case. Residents have sorted their trash for more than 20 years, and the town has one of the country’s highest recycling rates.

A new survey found that residents who reported closer ties with their neighbors also reported more waste sorting and more help for others at collection points.

They also reported more positive views of recycling as well as feeling that family and neighbors would expect them to recycle.

Naoko Kaida, a professor at the University of Tsukuba, led the study with colleagues.

The small town of Osaki was running low on landfill space by the late 1990s.

Businesses, residents, and the local government worked together to keep as much waste as possible out of the landfill.

Residents sort their waste into 28 categories including cans, plastic bottles, plastic packaging, cardboard, and even diapers. They separate other bottles by color.

Residents take their sorted waste to shared community recycling stations managed by local hygiene associations. In many communities, residents oversee the sorting themselves.

The effort has worked, sharply reducing the town’s reliance on landfills. Since 2006, over 80% of the waste generated in Osaki has gone to recycling.

Previous studies tried to clarify the relation between recycling and social ties.

Few studies, however, have examined these relationships in a place where waste sorting has become routine. Osaki began with 16 sorting categories in 2000 and later expanded to 28.

Kaida works at the Institute of Systems and Information Engineering, University of Tsukuba, and her team mailed the anonymous questionnaire to all 5,773 households in Osaki in late 2024. Any adult living in one of the households could fill out the questionnaire.

Of the 805 questionnaires returned over four weeks, 277 were complete enough to analyze.

A number of the questions focused on the residents’ perception of their neighbors in terms of the degree of trust they inspired, how well they knew them, and how sure the residents were that their neighbors would help in the case of a disaster.

The questions on social relationships other than those with close neighbors covered people with whom residents shared hobbies, friends with whom they kept in touch although they lived far away, and trusted contacts at the workplace or at school.

Social scientists call both kinds of relationship social capital.

On a six-point scale, residents rated their waste handling on average at 5.36 for following the rules and 4.11 for helping others at the waste sorting stations.

The first score covered all 28 sorting categories and labeling garbage bags, while the second covered unloading garbage and helping people unsure how to sort it.

The team refers to a model of behavior that psychologists frequently use.

According to this model, a person’s intention to behave in a certain way depends on their attitudes to the behavior, other people’s expectations, and their capacity to behave in the intended way.

First, residents who reported stronger neighborhood ties felt more positive about the sorting of waste, and felt more strongly that their family and their neighbors expected them to follow the rules and recycle.

These views in turn were associated with an intention to continue sorting waste, which Kaida and her team found to be the best predictor of the recycling behavior residents reported.

The relationship between neighborhood ties and sorting behavior was indirect, through attitudes and expectations, with no direct link between the two in the team’s model.

Of the three factors in the model, attitude had the strongest tie to intention, which the authors suggested could reflect decades of town policy promoting waste sorting and its benefits.

Social connections outside the neighborhood, such as friendships and hobby groups, were directly linked to recycling and helping others at the station.

This link was about as strong as the indirect link between neighborhood relationships and these behaviors.

The authors suggested, however, that outside contacts may be sources of information or even of resources which can help residents learn and keep up with a very complicated system such as that of waste sorting in Osaki.

In the model, these wider ties had no clear link to residents’ attitudes, their sense of others’ expectations or how capable they felt.

Both kinds of relationship were associated with higher levels of recycling behavior but only to a small degree.

Neither type of social connection was clearly linked to how confident residents felt about their ability to recycle.

The researchers suggested that this confidence may depend more on clear rules, available facilities, and years of recycling experience.

Osaki is an aging town with about 11,700 inhabitants and an average age of 53 in 2020, part of the wider pattern of shrinking, aging populations in rural Japan.

Older residents reported stronger neighborly ties and felt more expectation from others, but the relationship between social ties and recycling behavior remained after controlling for age.

This one-time survey can only report on correlation, meaning that it can show a link between close neighbors and recycling but cannot tell us whether one causes the other.

One point for future studies is to measure social ties and recycling attitudes over time to see how they change together.

The 277 respondents were an older group. About half of them were in their 60s or 70s, and only five respondents were in their 20s.

As mentioned earlier, the same residents rated their recycling behavior, and the researchers did not watch them sort.

Osaki’s established sorting system and neighborhood involvement may limit how well the findings apply elsewhere.

The authors suggested that the same type of relationships may exist in other localities where residents of the neighborhoods help manage waste collection.

In contrast to the rural settings of many Japanese towns, however, community ties in the cities are generally looser, and the link may be weaker.

Very close ties to neighbors, however, carry risks too, the authors wrote, as residents can perceive pressure to conform or find themselves excluded when neighbors monitor one another.

The researchers suggested that towns should do more than share information and enforce recycling rules to keep recycling rates high.

They should also organize regular activities that bring neighbors together and encourage younger generations to recycle.

How much these ties matter likely depends on how a town organizes its waste collection, which future studies comparing towns with different waste sorting systems could clarify.

The full study was published in the Journal of Material Cycles and Waste Management.
