Sitting behind the wheel may do something unusual to your heart rate

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Driving keeps the heart above its resting rate for longer, even when the body remains seated and inactive.

Sitting behind the wheel is usually treated as no different from sitting anywhere else. Heart-rate readings that were collected for a week from 57 young adults in Texas now suggest otherwise.

During the time these people spent driving, their heart rates remained above resting level almost throughout. A return to resting level was recorded about half as often as during other inactive hours.

Their hearts were not found to beat faster in the car than during those other hours. The resting pace was simply reached less often while a person was driving.

The study was designed and supervised by Ioannis Pavlidis who is a computer science professor at the University of Houston.

Colleagues from the same university and from the Texas A&M Transportation Institute worked with him.

Pavlidis said that a driver has to keep watching the road and deciding what to do next.

“The body may be sitting, but the mind is working,” he said. According to him, it is in the heart rate that this mental work can be seen.

What the study isn’t able to show is that driving itself kept anyone’s heart rate up. The comparison that was made was between time spent driving and other inactive time in one week.

There are more differences between these two kinds of time than the car alone.

Posture and caffeine are on the list of them that the authors gave and so are passengers. How urgently a driver needed to arrive is on that list as well.

The group consisted of 30 men and 27 women with an average age of about 26. Most of them were office workers.

A personal iPhone and Apple Watch were used by each participant from Monday through Sunday.

Every car was fitted with a small plug-in device by which each period of driving could be recorded. The watch was the source of the heart rate readings to which weather and traffic conditions were matched.

The rest of each day was labeled with the help of activity data from the phone and the watch. Any recorded minute in which a person was neither driving nor physically active was taken into the comparison.

Such minutes may have been spent at work or at home or somewhere else entirely.

The batteries of the iPhone and Apple Watch had usually run out by early evening. It is for this reason that the recordings cover daytime hours alone.

It was Pavlidis’ group at the Affective and Data Computing Laboratory that averaged the readings minute by minute. The recordings that resulted came to 177,318 minutes which is close to 3,000 hours.

No heart-rate reading existed for 13.6% of the driving minutes or for 3.5% of other inactive minutes. Neither speed nor traffic was found to be linked to the readings that were missing.

Driving took up about a tenth of that time. There were 973 trips in total and half of them were over within 15 minutes. A crash or a traffic ticket was generally not part of these drives.

Before any single minute could be judged, the team had to know each person’s resting heart rate.

An estimate of it is produced by the Apple Watch once a day. These daily estimates were averaged by the team across the whole week.

While a person was driving, their heart rate was above their own resting level 93.7% of the time on average. The figure for other inactive time was 81.3%.

Yet a higher heart rate in the car was not what the Houston team found. During driving, it averaged 11.2 beats per minute above resting level. The average for other inactive time was 11.5 beats above resting level.

A gap as small as that could have come up by chance.

Where driving did differ was in how little the heart rate varied. Its typical spread was 7.98 beats per minute during driving and 12.40 beats during other inactive time.

Heart rates dropped to resting level 2.46 times per hour during other inactive time and 1.31 times per hour while driving.

On weekdays, the two shares of time above resting level were 93.8% and 82.1%.

Weekends showed the same pattern, although people then spend their hours outside the car differently.

According to Pavlidis, a workout also raises heart rate, but the body becomes fitter as a result of it. From the time that is spent behind the wheel, no benefit of that kind is gained.

Yasmine Bouzid and the other authors also examined what a higher or lower heart rate was tied to.

During driving, there were two things that stood out from the rest. Time of day was the first since heart rates were higher in drives after noon than in the morning.

The second was the amount by which the driver’s wrist had turned in the minute before. The gyroscope of the Apple Watch supplied this measure which was treated as a sign of steering.

Away from the car, the list that was found was a broader one. On it were age and sex along with time of day and the Big Five personality trait of openness.

For this, the authors offered one reason which they regarded as possible. Driving is a single task whose demands remain fairly steady from one minute to the next.

Many different situations are covered by the inactive time that is spent away from the car.

Because a drive is repeated day after day, an example using anxiety was worked through by the team.

The volunteers had filled out the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, which scores a tendency to feel anxious from 20 to 80. The lowest score that was found in this group was 20 while the highest was 61.

Over every recorded minute of driving, the team’s heart-rate model was then run twice. A score of 20 was given to every driver in the first of these runs. In the second run, it was a score of 61 that every driver was given.

The heart rate that the model predicted with the score of 61 was higher by 0.99 beats per minute.

What the Houston team then did was to count such beats over a whole year. For a person who drives eight hours a day, the model projected about 173,000 extra heartbeats.

It came to roughly 11,000 extra heartbeats when the same was done for a 30-minute daily drive.

It was only as illustrations that these totals were described by Pavlidis and his coauthors. What they show is how a small difference accumulates, and not what anyone’s risk of heart disease is.

There were only young adults from Texas among the 57 volunteers, and none was followed for more than a week. Older drivers, professional drivers, or people with heart disease may not show the results that were found here.

It will take years of following drivers before the health side of this can be judged. Only then can it be learned whether more time above resting heart rate predicts heart problems.

Pavlidis said that driving is something many people can’t do without. What his lab in Houston wants to find out is how the same trips could be made less demanding.

The Texas A&M Transportation Institute, where study co-author Michael Manser works, is supporting the lab through 2027.

The people who are to be studied next include long-distance commuters and those who drive for a living.

Rideshare drivers are an example of the latter. The groups are meant to be bigger, more varied, and followed for longer.

There is one more question that self-driving cars from Waymo and Tesla have raised for the lab. What does a rider’s heart rate do when it is the vehicle that handles the driving?

The full study was published in the journal npj Digital Public Health.
