Heterochromatin typically represses genes1,2, yet some genes paradoxically require it for activation3,4,5,6,7. How can the same chromatin environment both silence and promote transcription? Here we address this question by interrogating the clustered protocadherin (Pcdh) locus, where subsets of genes are stochastically expressed while most remain silent8. In neurons, stochastic and combinatorial expression of Pcdh genes generates unique cell-surface barcodes required for self–non-self discrimination and neural circuit assembly8. By combining RNA labelling of Pcdh genes and neurodevelopmental cell types with single-molecule chromatin tracing and stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy, we find that a compact heterochromatin domain containing Pcdh genes forms as these genes become activated during development. Pcdh transcription occurs within the compact domain and reads through downstream genes, and distal enhancers must access the domain to engage target promoters. Genetic perturbations in mice suggest that opposing genome folding forces cooperate to establish Pcdh locus architecture and promote stochastic Pcdh expression. Specifically, while DNA loop extrusion allows enhancers to breach heterochromatin and find target promoters, heterochromatin-driven compartmentalization supports an expanded enhancer–promoter search space. These findings shed light on a process foundational to the development of vertebrate nervous systems and suggest a mechanism for how transcription can occur within seemingly silent chromatin environments.

Histone H3 lysine 9 (H3 K9) trimethylation, a canonical post-translational modification of constitutive heterochromatin, is known to spread over large genomic territories and repress gene expression9. These defining properties of heterochromatin were recognized in classic position–effect variegation studies in Drosophila during the 1930s, in which euchromatic genes placed near domains with trimethylated H3 K9 (H3K9me3) became transcriptionally silenced1,2. However, subsequent discovery of genes whose expression depends on their residence within heterochromatin revealed that this conflict is not absolute. For example, expression of the light (lt) and rolled (rl) genes in flies requires their position within pericentromeric heterochromatin3,4,5. In mice and humans, proper expression of olfactory receptor (OR) and KRAB domain-containing zinc-finger protein (KZFP) genes also depends on H3K9me3 heterochromatin6,7. These ‘escapee’ genes suggest the existence of specialized regulatory strategies that allow transcription to occur within, or despite, heterochromatin, raising fundamental questions about when heterochromatin facilitates versus restrains gene expression.

The vertebrate clustered protocadherin (Pcdh) locus provides a striking example of a large gene family whose expression has been proposed to require escape from a heterochromatic environment10,11,12,13. Pcdh genes are expressed predominantly in neurons, where they govern neuronal self–non-self discrimination and circuit formation8. In individual neurons, only a small subset of Pcdh genes are expressed, whereas most remain silent8. Because selection of Pcdh genes is random and combinatorial14,15,16,17, this process generates extraordinary cell surface protein isoform diversity that marks each neuron with a unique identity, or ‘barcode’. Neurites presenting the same Pcdh barcode recognize and avoid one another, thereby preventing self-adhesion, whereas neurites presenting distinct Pcdh barcodes do not self-recognize and can interact18,19,20. Consistent with their critical role in neural circuit assembly, genetic variants in Pcdh genes are associated with neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder21,22 and schizophrenia23.

In mice, there are 58 Pcdh genes distributed across three tandemly arranged clusters—α (Pcdha), β (Pcdhb) and γ (Pcdhg)—spanning roughly 1 megabase of DNA on chromosome 18 (ref. 24) (Fig. 1a). Pcdha genes are regulated by the HS5-1 enhancer located downstream of the Pcdha cluster, whereas Pcdhb and Pcdhg genes are regulated by the HS18-22 and HS16-17 enhancers, respectively, located downstream of the Pcdhg cluster25 (Fig. 1a). The Pcdha and Pcdhg genes are further subdivided into ‘alternate’ and ‘c-type’ isoforms24 (Fig. 1a). Pcdh genes and their promoters are marked by H3 K9 trimethylation and CpG DNA methylation (5mC)10,13,26. Stochastic Pcdh gene choice requires promoter DNA demethylation driven by cohesin-mediated enhancer–promoter interactions, with promoter demethylation leading to CTCF binding and sustained transcription of the respective Pcdh gene10,13,15,27,28. Although these findings have identified how Pcdh promoters escape DNA methylation, they do not address how euchromatic Pcdh enhancers navigate H3K9me3 heterochromatin to activate silent Pcdh promoters. Several questions remain unanswered. Specifically, do stochastic enhancer–promoter interactions occur within heterochromatin? Is Pcdh transcription carried out within a heterochromatic environment otherwise programmed to suppress gene activity, or must Pcdh loci escape this environment to transcribe? And, does heterochromatin contribute to the stochasticity of Pcdh promoter choice?

To answer these questions, we sought a single-molecule view of Pcdh locus architecture during development that included the positioning of active and inactive promoters. Until now, most insights into this relationship have come from bulk genomic analyses. At a locus where only a few genes are active while most remain silent, bulk measurements mainly reflect the non-chosen genes, effectively averaging out the features of the chosen ones. To circumvent this limitation, we used optical reconstruction of chromatin architecture (ORCA), a single-molecule chromatin tracing assay29, in olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) of the mouse olfactory epithelium (OE). We selected OSNs for three reasons. First, they undergo multiple stages of development and constantly regenerate, such that each stage is present within the OE simultaneously. Second, Pcdh gene expression is developmentally regulated during OSN maturation15,20, with approximately one gene per cluster expressed from each allele in mature OSNs (mOSNs) (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). Third, stochastic expression of Pcdh genes has a critical role in OSN axon wiring and odour map formation20,30,31.

We designed ORCA probes that tile the Pcdh locus in 90 10-kilobase bins, omitting repetitive and GC-poor regions (Fig. 1a). ORCA probes were validated in OE sections from mice in which mOSNs were genetically labelled (Extended Data Fig. 2a). We visualized ORCA data as contact frequency matrices, which depict the frequency that each pair of locus positions (bins along the Pcdh locus) falls within a specified three-dimensional (3D) distance across all imaged traces. We set this threshold near the average distance between adjacent locus positions in an experiment. The contact frequency map obtained from ORCA correlated strongly with chromosome conformation capture Hi-C data of the same genomic bins from sorted mOSNs (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c, Pearson’s r = 0.87), and the correlation remained high across a sweep of ORCA contact thresholds (Extended Data Fig. 2d). ORCA data from whole OE tissue also remained consistent across separate experiments (Extended Data Fig. 2e,f, Pearson’s r = 0.98) and showed good detection efficiency across locus positions (Extended Data Fig. 2g). These results demonstrate that ORCA faithfully captures Pcdh locus architecture.

To study how Pcdh locus architecture changes during Pcdh gene activation and neuronal maturation, we combined ORCA imaging of Pcdh DNA with RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) labelling of the five major OSN developmental cell stages: horizontal basal cells (HBCs), globose basal cells (GBCs), immediate neuronal precursors (INPs), immature OSNs (iOSNs) and mOSNs (Fig. 1c). This lineage spawns from the mitotic HBC population located near the basal surface of the OE. As OSNs progress along their developmental axis, these cells migrate towards the apical surface of the OE (Fig. 1c). RNA labelling of marker genes for each stage reflected this basal-to-apical migration (Fig. 1d). We also stained samples with fluorescently tagged wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), a lectin that binds cell membranes, to obtain 3D segmentations of cells in the OE (Extended Data Fig. 3a). We mapped both RNA and Pcdh DNA puncta to these segmentations, then classified ORCA traces (that is, individual DNA paths) into different developmental stages on the basis of the RNA signal of the cells in which they resided (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c).

The contact frequency maps for each stage of OSN development illustrated two trends. First, stripes denoting contacts between Pcdh enhancers and their target promoters became more prominent as cells matured (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 1d–f). Second, contacts between locus positions containing Pcdhb genes and the alternate genes from the Pcdha and Pcdhg clusters, as well as the 60-kilobase stretch upstream of the Pcdha genes, increased (Fig. 1e,f). Subtracting the contact frequency map of each developmental stage from the mOSN map emphasized that these inter-cluster contacts constituted the most pronounced changes in locus architecture over development (Fig. 1e).

The formation of these inter-cluster contact domains was reminiscent of the checkerboard pattern created by compartmentalization of active and repressed chromatin domains seen on Hi-C maps32. Given that this compartmentalization is mainly driven by homotypic interactions between heterochromatic regions of the genome, we hypothesized that the locus regions engaging in these contacts would have histone marks characteristic of heterochromatin. To investigate this possibility, we performed quantitative native chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (nChIP–seq) for H3K9me3 in FACS-isolated HBCs and mOSNs. In HBCs, the Pcdh locus was lightly coated in H3K9me3, with H3K9me3 levels increasing roughly threefold in mOSNs (Fig. 1g,h). Furthermore, in mOSNs, two major stretches of enriched H3K9me3 emerged separated by regions devoid of H3K9me3 between and beyond them (Fig. 1g). Specifically, the DNA regions of the Pcdh locus that engaged in inter-cluster contacts over OSN development became heavily coated with H3K9me3, whereas regions of the locus containing Pcdh c-type genes and enhancers remained free of H3K9me3 (Fig. 1e,g). We hereafter refer to the H3K9me3-marked chromatin regions engaging in inter-cluster contacts as the ‘heterochromatin domain’ of the Pcdh locus. This definition indicates the spatial arrangement of the regions within the Pcdh locus that are marked by H3K9me3 in OSNs. We refer to the regions of the locus not marked by H3K9me3 containing Pcdh c-type genes and enhancers as euchromatin.

To understand the positioning of the heterochromatin domain within the architecture of the Pcdh locus, we calculated the distance of each locus position to the geometric centre of its respective trace and examined the median over all traces across each stage of development. All locus positions moved closer to the geometric centre as cells matured, indicating a general compaction of the locus (Fig. 1i). Layered on top of this trend, locus positions belonging to the heterochromatin domain moved closest to the centre (Fig. 1i, boxed regions), whereas euchromatic regions remained further in the periphery. To better quantify the degree of compaction of the heterochromatin domain, we measured its radius of gyration (Rg), a metric commonly used to describe the volume of a polymer, and found that Rg decreases as neurons mature (Fig. 1j). Surprisingly, as compaction increases, so does the number of genes expressed per cell from within the domain (Fig. 1j). We further visualized these changes in locus architecture by plotting individual traces coloured by regions that become heterochromatic or euchromatic over development and by examining single-molecule distance maps (Fig. 1k and Extended Data Fig. 4). Despite heterogeneity across traces, the formation of the domain was evident: in HBCs, both regions tended to occupy large and intermixed volumes, whereas in mOSNs, the regions segregated, with heterochromatin forming a dense, more centrally located domain and euchromatin occupying more open volumes in the periphery (Fig. 1k and Extended Data Fig. 4).

Overall, these results indicate that in neural stem cells, the Pcdh locus is lightly coated with H3K9me3 heterochromatin, yet open and intermixed, with a compact heterochromatin domain forming in its centre as cells mature into post-mitotic neurons (Fig. 1l). Counterintuitively, the formation of this compact structure coincides with activation of Pcdh genes (Fig. 1j).

These results could be explained by the presence of two distinct subpopulations—actively transcribing loci that transiently lose heterochromatin and silent loci that do not. Alternatively, actively transcribing loci could retain heterochromatin and their compacted architecture. Because our bulk measurements of H3K9me3 by nChIP–seq could not distinguish between these two possibilities, we turned to stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) to measure H3K9me3 in single cells. We combined STORM for H3K9me3 with RNA FISH labelling of Pcdh isoforms and OSN cell types, as well as ORCA imaging of DNA, to test for colocalization of heterochromatin with transcribing Pcdh alleles (Fig. 2a). We used RNA probes against 11 Pcdh isoforms and confirmed their specificity for their target isoforms in vitro (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b).

Overlaying Pcdh RNA staining on Pcdh DNA loci labelled by ORCA revealed DNA traces that colocalized with RNA puncta (Extended Data Fig. 6a). By measuring distances between Pcdh loci and Pcdh RNA puncta, we identified a data-driven distance threshold below which RNA transcripts could be classified as nascent (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). RNA puncta within this threshold were brighter than those beyond it, consistent with nascent transcriptional bursting33 (Extended Data Fig. 6d).

We then examined the spatial relationship between transcribing Pcdh DNA loci and H3K9me3 STORM signal in mOSNs. Remarkably, we observed many examples of nascent transcription occurring at Pcdh loci that colocalized with H3K9me3 STORM (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Plotting the ORCA traces of these loci alongside H3K9me3 STORM localizations revealed transcribing promoters spatially residing within heterochromatin (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 7a).

To assess whether STORM captured a nuclear enrichment of H3K9me3 over the Pcdh locus, we quantified the number of H3K9me3 localizations surrounding Pcdh loci relative to randomized positions within the same nuclei. The median number of H3K9me3 localizations detected around Pcdh DNA loci exceeded that of randomized positions in 999 of 1,000 simulations (empirical P = 0.002) (Fig. 2c). We performed the same analyses on Tex15 loci, which are not marked by H3K9me3 as assayed by bulk nChIP–seq (Extended Data Fig. 7b–d). We used Tex15 nascent RNA transcripts as proxies for Tex15 DNA positions (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d). In contrast to Pcdh loci, Tex15 loci did not show an enrichment for H3K9me3 (empirical P = 0.292) (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Furthermore, we observed more Tex15 loci lacking any H3K9me3 signal compared with Pcdh loci (Extended Data Fig. 7e).

We next asked whether Pcdh transcription was associated with reduced local H3K9me3 signal and domain compaction by first dividing loci into those with or without detectable nascent transcription, the latter of which we hereafter refer to as non-transcribing. We then measured the number of H3K9me3 STORM localizations around these loci and the Rg of their heterochromatin domains by ORCA. Surprisingly, transcribing Pcdh loci had slightly higher H3K9me3 STORM signal and slightly more compact heterochromatin domains relative to non-transcribing loci (Fig. 2d,e). Furthermore, transcription of individual Pcdh genes was associated neither with a change in H3K9me3 STORM signal around transcribing promoters, at the resolution of our assay, nor with a change in heterochromatin domain compaction (Fig. 2f,g).

Overall, these analyses indicate that transcription of Pcdh loci can occur in H3K9me3-rich nuclear territories and does not disrupt the heterochromatin domain of the locus.

These data prompted us to further evaluate features of a transcribing Pcdh locus. To do that, we expanded the design of our RNA probes to a total of 31 Pcdh genes that showed appreciable staining and specificity (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b). We noted that nascently transcribing RNA puncta often colocalized with other Pcdh RNAs of the same cluster (Fig. 3a), and more nascent puncta were detected for progressively downstream isoforms at the Pcdha and Pcdhg, but not Pcdhb, clusters (Fig. 3b). Meanwhile, mature transcript levels measured by bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) remained relatively flat across all clusters (Fig. 3b). We reasoned that these results could reflect nascent transcription of Pcdha and Pcdhg genes that reads through downstream isoforms (Fig. 3c). Yet, the number of nascent Pcdh RNAs detected at a locus was not associated with changes in heterochromatin domain Rg (Fig. 3d), indicating that this putative transcriptional readthrough occurs without domain decompaction.

To identify features of locus architecture associated with transcription, we subtracted the contact frequency maps of non-transcribing loci from the maps of transcribing loci in mOSNs. This revealed an enrichment of enhancer–promoter contacts in transcribing loci (Fig. 3e–g), which was further supported by an increase in transcription probability as a function of enhancer–promoter proximity (Extended Data Fig. 8). For Pcdha and Pcdhg genes, these enhancer–promoter contact enrichments included promoters upstream of the transcribing isoform, and the magnitude of enrichment experienced by each promoter diminished when filtering on transcription of progressively downstream isoforms (Fig. 3e,g). By contrast, Pcdhb promoters had more local enhancer-contact enrichments upon transcription (Fig. 3f).

Together, these data indicate that at the Pcdha and Pcdhg clusters, transcription of chosen promoters reads through downstream genes. This pattern is consistent with splicing of Pcdha and Pcdhg variable exons to three small constant exons located 3′ of these clusters34, and was not observed at the Pcdhb cluster, which lacks constant exons. Furthermore, these data indicate that Pcdh gene choice is driven by highly specific enhancer–promoter interactions across this multi-gene, multi-enhancer locus.

We next asked where Pcdh transcription and enhancer–promoter interactions occur within the spatial architecture of the locus (Fig. 1l) and, in particular, whether transcribing promoters remain within or exit the heterochromatin domain. To assess the positioning of transcribing promoters, we measured the distances between promoters and heterochromatic locus positions in the presence and absence of transcription in mOSNs (Fig. 4a). These distances did not change during transcription (Fig. 4a). On the other hand, distances between promoters and euchromatic locus positions decreased during transcription (Fig. 4a). This effect seemed driven by decreased distances between promoters and enhancers upon transcription (Fig. 4b). These results indicate that enhancer–promoter contacts associated with transcription bring euchromatic locus positions closer to the heterochromatin domain, but neither these contacts nor transcription itself cause transcribing promoters to exit the domain. Plotting transcription probability as a function of a promoter’s distance to the heterochromatin centre supported this conclusion, as promoters were most likely to be transcribing at distances within the median Rg of the heterochromatin domain (Fig. 4c).

To further dissect the positioning of enhancers and promoters relative to the heterochromatin and euchromatin compartments of the locus during contact, we calculated the distances of each locus position to the centre of the heterochromatin domain (dHC) and the centre of the euchromatic region housing the cluster-specific enhancer(s) of that position (dEC) (Extended Data Fig. 9a). We examined Pcdhb16 as a case study. In non-transcribing traces and transcribing traces without enhancer–promoter contacts, Pcdhb16 was closer to the heterochromatin centre than the euchromatin centre, whereas its enhancer HS18-22 showed the opposite relationship (Extended Data Fig. 9a). In Pcdhb16-transcribing traces with enhancer–promoter contacts, Pcdhb16 and its enhancer HS18-22 were more equidistant to the two centres (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Further evaluation of the equidistant state showed that the dHC of both promoter and enhancer decreased during contact, but that the enhancer dHC decreased by a greater magnitude (Extended Data Fig. 9b), consistent with promoters remaining within the heterochromatin domain during contact and the enhancers approaching the domain to engage promoters (Fig. 4a,b). Extending these analyses to all other Pcdh genes stained for showed similar positionings of transcribing Pcdh promoters and cognate enhancers relative to their compartment centres: when in contact, promoters and enhancers became more equidistant to the two centres; when not in contact, promoters were closer to the heterochromatin centre than the euchromatin centre, whereas enhancers were closer to the euchromatin centre (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). Quantifying the percentage of transcribing traces with and without enhancer–promoter contacts revealed the latter state to be more prevalent (Extended Data Fig. 9e), indicating that promoters transcribe at positions closer to the heterochromatin centre, and that Pcdh transcription can be sustained in the absence of enhancer engagement.

Individual traces reflected the above behaviours. Transcribing promoters remained within the heterochromatin domain, and enhancers approached the domain to engage promoters (Fig. 4d).

Lastly, we investigated the genome folding mechanisms that establish Pcdh locus architecture and how this architecture supports stochastic Pcdh gene choice. The wild-type (WT) locus architecture is defined by stripes corresponding to enhancer–promoter contacts and by inter-cluster contacts within the heterochromatin domain (Fig. 1e). To abolish enhancer–promoter contacts, we conditionally removed Rad21, the kleisin subunit of the cohesin protein complex, using an Omp-ires-cre;Rad21fl/fl;tdTomatofl/fl (Rad21 cKO) mouse (Extended Data Fig. 10a). To perturb heterochromatin compaction, we conditionally deleted Setdb1, the SET domain bifurcated histone methyltransferase 1, which catalyses di- and trimethylation of H3K9, using an Omp-ires-cre;Setdb1fl/fl;tdTomatofl/fl (Setdb1 cKO) mouse (Extended Data Fig. 10a). We confirmed knockout of Rad21 and Setdb1 in mOSNs by immunofluorescence (Extended Data Fig. 10b), then performed ORCA in both mouse models.

Rad21 deletion abolished enhancer–promoter contacts as evidenced by a loss of stripes (Fig. 5a), consistent with previous reports10,13,15,27. By contrast, Setdb1 deletion resulted in a loss of inter-cluster contacts (Fig. 5a), which was accompanied by an approximately 40% reduction of H3K9me3 over the heterochromatin domain (Fig. 5b,c and Extended Data Fig. 10c). Furthermore, separation between heterochromatic and euchromatic regions of the locus increased in Rad21 cKO but decreased in Setdb1 cKO (Fig. 5d), whereas the relative size of the heterochromatin domain increased in Setdb1 cKO but decreased in Rad21 cKO (Fig. 5e).

Individual traces reflected these structural changes. Single molecules showed distancing of Pcdh enhancers from the heterochromatin domain in Rad21 cKO OSNs relative to WT (Fig. 5f), which was further supported by changes in the distance of each locus position relative to the heterochromatin domain centre (Extended Data Fig. 10d). Meanwhile, the heterochromatin domain seemed less compact and more intermixed with euchromatin in Setdb1 cKO OSNs relative to WT (Fig. 5f), which was further supported by an increase in the entropy of each locus position in Setdb1 cKO (Extended Data Fig. 10e). These data suggest that Pcdh enhancers require cohesin to approach the heterochromatin domain and contact target promoters, whereas heterochromatin keeps the promoters in a compact and ordered environment.

To evaluate how these changes in Pcdh locus architecture affect stochastic Pcdh gene expression, we performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq). Ablation of Rad21 resulted in widespread downregulation of Pcdh expression in favour of Pcdhac2, the Pcdha gene most proximal to the HS5-1 enhancer, which also resides in euchromatin (Fig. 5g,h and Extended Data Fig. 10f,g). This gain in expression of enhancer-proximal Pcdh genes was not observed at the Pcdhg cluster, suggesting distinct enhancer–promoter specificities at the two clusters (Fig. 5g and Extended Data Fig. 10f,g). By contrast, ablation of Setdb1 resulted in upregulation of Pcdha10–12 and Pcdhgb6–b7, the genes closest to the heterochromatin–euchromatin boundaries at the locus (Fig. 5g,i and Extended Data Fig. 10f,h). Enhancer–promoter contact frequencies reflected these expression changes in both genotypes (Extended Data Fig. 10i,j).

These results indicate that DNA loop extrusion and heterochromatin compaction cooperate to establish Pcdh locus architecture and promote an unbiased enhancer–promoter search space at the locus. While cohesin is required for Pcdh enhancers to gain access to the heterochromatin domain housing Pcdh promoters, the compaction of the domain expands the ability of the enhancers to find target promoters. Thus, although generally viewed as antagonistic processes35, both cohesin and heterochromatin-driven chromatin compartmentalization are indispensable for proper Pcdh expression.

Here we show that heterochromatin spatially partitions the Pcdh locus into a compacted domain housing Pcdh promoters and multiple open regions housing Pcdh enhancers. Transcription from chosen promoters occurs within the heterochromatin domain, and enhancers must access the domain to engage target promoters (Extended Data Fig. 10k). Counterintuitively, Pcdh transcription can read through hundreds of kilobases without dissolving the compacted state of the locus (Extended Data Fig. 10k). We further demonstrate that Pcdh locus architecture and proper Pcdh expression are achieved through cooperation between heterochromatin-mediated compaction and cohesin-mediated DNA loop extrusion.

Our findings challenge the prevailing view that heterochromatin is a physically inaccessible chromatin structure. Heterochromatin compaction was initially proposed to sterically hinder the transcriptional machinery and thereby repress gene expression36,37. However, rather than acting as an impenetrable barrier, our data indicate that heterochromatin can support active transcription while retaining a compact 3D organization. These findings add to a growing body of evidence suggesting that heterochromatin is not intrinsically inaccessible and can support transcription. For example, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)-dense centromeric and pericentromeric heterochromatin are actively transcribed and accumulate abundant RNA despite their highly condensed appearance38. Furthermore, transcription can stabilize H3K9me3-modifying enzymes39,40,41, and dense heterochromatin nonetheless allows passage of transcription factors42 and large macromolecules43, including RNA polymerase II (ref. 44).

These observations have motivated an alternative model of heterochromatin organization, wherein domain-scale compaction emerges from frequent intra-domain interactions that serve to reinforce the epigenetic state of the domain rather than directly silence the underlying DNA through steric exclusion. By this model, distal genomic loci are brought into proximity by the activity of heterochromatin reader and writer proteins, as readers bound to heterochromatin marks on one chromatin fibre might promote deposition of the same modification by writers on another fibre. This 3D reader–writer activity establishes a positive feedback loop that reinforces the heterochromatic state of the domain. Heterochromatin compaction thus functions to concentrate this feedback, ensuring it acts primarily back on the existing heterochromatin to stabilize its epigenetic state, rather than spreading or diluting to neighbouring euchromatin45,46.

Although heterochromatin has been proposed to be important for epigenetic memory at the Pcdh locus11,12, we found that chosen Pcdh promoters do not segregate from flanking promoters retaining the silent state, contrasting with the behaviour of chosen promoters in H3K27me3 heterochromatin29,47. This unexpected organization argues against domain-scale spatial segregation as a primary mechanism for heterochromatin-mediated memory of Pcdh gene choice. Instead, these data suggest that a more local compartmentalization of active and inactive genes might develop, with biophysically distinct microcompartments, or ‘pockets’, of active chromatin containing expressed genes forming within the heterochromatin domain. This model is consistent with the presence of both highly dynamic and more stable HP1 populations within the same heterochromatin environment48 and structural heterogeneity of heterochromatin compartments, as revealed by recent single-molecule footprinting49 and chromatin tracing46 studies.

Lastly, beyond classical examples of heterochromatic genes, such as the lt and rl genes in Drosophila3,4,5, the intermixed spatial organization of active and inactive genes within H3K9me3-enriched chromatin domains discussed here may reflect a more general property of gene regulation within heterochromatic territories—with particular relevance to genes arranged in clusters, such as OR6 and KZFP genes7. We propose that domain-scale spatial segregation of active and inactive genes might not be required at these clusters to accommodate and maintain expression of subsets of genes within a more broadly repressive chromatin domain. Furthermore, as we have shown here for Pcdh genes, these loci might also require the coordinated integration of genome folding mechanisms across scales—from higher-order heterochromatin compartmentalization to enhancer–promoter interactions mediated by various looping factors50—to achieve genomic architectures required for precise and selective gene activation.

In summary, our findings illustrate how seemingly silent chromatin domains can coordinate gene expression and invite a reconceptualization of heterochromatin from a singularly repressive chromatin environment to one that can, when properly configured, permit active transcription.

In Extended Data Fig. 10e, entropy was calculated for the distance distributions between pairs of locus positions as described previously58. For each barcode pair, we pooled 3D distances across all traces within each genotype separately, then normalized each genotype’s distances by that genotype’s own median distance for the pair to account for length-scale differences between experiments. We then pooled the normalized distances from both genotypes together and set common histogram bin edges (50 bins) using the 1st and 99th percentiles of this pooled, normalized distribution, so that entropy values were computed on the same scale for both genotypes. Using these shared bin edges, we separately estimated each genotype’s distribution, normalized bin counts to probabilities pk and computed Shannon entropy as \(S=-{\sum }_{k}{p}_{k}{\log }_{2}({p}_{k})\), such that higher S reflects broader, more variable distances and lower S indicates more narrowly peaked, reproducible distances. For each locus position, we calculated its entropy with respect to all other positions, averaged these pairwise entropies per position and compared the resulting mean entropies between Setdb1 cKO and WT.

To quantify spatial segregation between heterochromatic and euchromatic regions of the Pcdh locus, we calculated a separation score as follows. For each polymer, we designated heterochromatin locus positions (barcodes 2–14 and 29–67) as region A and euchromatic locus positions (barcodes 15–28 and 68–90) as region B, computed all pairwise 3D distances and took the median distance between A and B (heterochromatin–euchromatin) and the median distance within A (heterochromatin–heterochromatin). scRNA-seq experiments were performed as previously described15. In brief, cells were dissociated from OE tissue from 4- to 5-week-old mice as described above. About 70,000 mOSNs were FACS-isolated. Complementary DNA and library preparation were performed using the 10x Genomics Single Cell 5′ Gene Expression workflow, and libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeq SP100 PE50 and a NextSeq 2000 P2 200 through the UCSF Gladstone Genomic Core and the UCSF Center for Advanced Technology. Sequencing data were processed using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger 6.0.1. Data analysis was performed using Seurat60,61 and custom scripts. Cells were filtered to retain cells with 1,000–10,000 detected genes and less than 5% mitochondrial reads. Counts were then normalized using Seurat’s NormalizeData with log normalization and a scale factor of 10,000. See Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 for further details on scRNA-seq analysis and statistics.

For analyses in Fig. 5 and Extended Data Fig. 10, Pcdh isoform choice was analysed in mOSNs defined by detected Omp expression and no detected Gap43 expression using raw unique molecular identifier counts. A Pcdh isoform was considered detected in a given mOSN if one or more unique molecular identifier-corrected reads were assigned to that gene. For each condition, the percentage of mOSNs expressing each Pcdh isoform was calculated as the fraction of mOSNs with detected expression of that isoform. Changes in Pcdh choice across genotypes were calculated as percentage-point differences relative to WT. Data for single-cell experiments represent one biological replicate per condition.

nChIP–seq was performed as previously described62. Briefly, live cells were sorted from papain-dissociated mouse OE; in experiments for which spike-ins were included to enable quantification of ChIP signal across conditions, HEK293T cells at around 70% confluency were trypsinized, washed with PBS and added at a ratio of 0.75% to the sorted mouse cells. (HEK293T cells were grown in RPMI or DMEM media supplemented with 10% FBS and 1 × penicillin-streptomycin, incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2, and tested for mycoplasma (ATCC Universal Mycoplasma Detection Kit) before use in experiments.) Live sorted cells were pelleted in a swinging bucket centrifuge (800g, 15 min, 4 °C) and then resuspended in Buffer 1 (0.3 M sucrose, 60 mM KCl, 15 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 15 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors). Cells were lysed on ice for 10 min by adding an equal volume of Buffer 2 (0.4% Igepal CA-630, 0.3 M sucrose, 60 mM KCl, 15 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EGTA, 15 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors). Nuclei were pelleted (1,000g, 10 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in MNase buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 4 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.1 mM PMSF, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors). Resuspended nuclei were treated with MNase (Sigma) at 37 °C, and the reaction was quenched with the addition of EDTA to a final concentration of 20 mM. Two fractions of solubilized chromatin were collected; nuclei were pelleted (10,000g, 10 min, 4 °C) and fraction S1 was collected and stored at 4 °C overnight. Nuclei were resuspended in Dialysis Buffer (1 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM PMSF, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors) and rotated overnight at 4 °C. Insoluble material was pelleted (10,000g, 10 min, 4 °C) and fraction S2 was retained. MNase treatment was optimized to yield predominantly mono-nucleosomes in the S1 fraction and predominantly di-nucleosomes, tri-nucleosomes and some larger chromatin fragments in the S2 fraction. S1 and S2 DNA concentrations were measured by HS DNA Qubit after RNase A treatment. Equal volumes of S1 and S2 fractions were pooled, and total yields were estimated. In spike-in experiments, chromatin input was not always equalized across immunoprecipitations, as spike-in normalization accounts for differences in input.

MNase-digested chromatin was diluted in Wash Buffer 1 (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 10 mM EDTA, 125 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors), and 5% inputs were reserved. Histone H3 lysine 9 tri-methyl antibody (H3K9me3, Abcam ab8898) was added, and the binding reaction was rotated overnight at 4 °C. For each immunoprecipitation, 20 μl of Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen) were blocked overnight with 2 mg ml−1 yeast transfer RNA and 2 mg ml−1 BSA in Wash Buffer 1. The next day, blocked beads were added to antibody-bound chromatin and rotated for 1–3 h at 4 °C. Chromatin-bound beads were washed at 4 °C a total of four times with Wash Buffer I, three times with Wash Buffer II (5 mM Tris pH 7.5, 17.5 mM NaCl, 0.01% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 1 × Roche cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitors), and one time with Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA). Two 15-min elutions were conducted at 37 °C in Elution Buffer (1% SDS, 250 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT). ChIP DNA and input DNA were column purified using Zymo ChIP DNA columns (Zymo Research, D5205 or D5201).

Libraries were prepared using the Ovation Ultralow V2 DNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit (Tecan Life Sciences). The quality of the libraries was assessed by Bioanalyzer and quantified using a combination of Bioanalyzer and Qubit. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 (Gladstone Institutes), NextSeq 500/550 (UCSF Center for Advanced Technology) or NovaSeq X (Chan Zuckerberg Initiative Biohub San Francisco).

Data processing and analysis was conducted in accordance with previous literature63,64. For spike-in experiments, adapters were trimmed with cutadapt with --minimum-length 18 and checked for sequencing quality control with FastQC. Reads were aligned with Bowtie2 to a concatenated mouse and human genome (mm10 + hg38) in which the spike-in chromosomes were pre-labelled with a ‘_hg38’ suffix. Alignments were split by chromosome using bamUtil’s splitChromosome function, and samtools’ merge command was used to collect mouse and human alignments separately. Then, duplicates were marked and removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, and the spike-in chromosome suffix was stripped. Alignments with mapping quality < 30 were removed using samtools, and read counts aligning to the mouse and human genomes were used to calculate spike-in scaling factors.

As in the original ChIP-Rx method describing quantitative spike-in normalized ChIP followed by sequencing, our spike-in corrections normalized for the number of spike-in reads in the immunoprecipitation63. We sequenced every input as well, allowing us to adjust for the variability in spike-in read fraction in each input64,65. The spike-in adjusted coverage signal, \({z}_{i}\), was calculated from counts per million (CPM)-normalized coverage values, \({x}_{i}\), with the equation \({z}_{i}={x}_{i}\cdot \frac{{S}_{\mathrm{in}}}{{T}_{\mathrm{in}}}\frac{{T}_{\mathrm{ip}}}{{S}_{\mathrm{ip}}}\), where \({S}_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \({T}_{\mathrm{in}}\) are the number of spike-in (human) and target (mouse) reads in the inputs, respectively, and \({S}_{\mathrm{ip}}\) and \({T}_{\mathrm{ip}}\) are the number of spike-in (human) and target (mouse) reads in the immunoprecipitations, respectively. Bigwig files were generated from Deeptools bamCoverage using --scaleFactor of \(\frac{{S}_{\mathrm{in}}}{{T}_{\mathrm{in}}}\cdot \frac{{T}_{\mathrm{ip}}}{{S}_{\mathrm{ip}}}\), --binSize 10, --minMappingQuality 30 and --normalizeUsing CPM. In Fig. 5c, to compare spike-in-normalized nChIP–seq values from different experiments, we batch-corrected using the average of the scale factors from the WT samples within each experimental batch. Significance was assessed and confirmed using both a Welch’s unpaired t-test and a two-way ANOVA (main effects only).

For experiments without spike-ins, adapters were trimmed and the first 4 nucleotides of each read removed with cutadapt (v1.9.1; -u 4, --minimum-length 18). Reads were aligned to mm10 with Bowtie2. BigWig files were generated with deepTools bamCoverage in 10-base-pair bins from unextended reads, normalized to 1 × genome coverage (reads per genomic content; effective genome size 2,407,883,318; chr. X, chr. Y and chr. M excluded from normalization).

Immunohistochemistry for RAD21 was performed as described previously15. Sections were removed from −20 °C, washed in 1 × PBS for 5 min at room temperature, then washed three times in 0.1% PBS-Triton X for 5 min. Sections were incubated in blocking solution (3% BSA, 0.2% Tween in 4 × SSC) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation proceeded overnight (12–16 h) at 4 °C in a humidified chamber.
