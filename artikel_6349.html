Air pollution may weaken one of the brain’s natural defenses against dementia

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

A mouse study suggests that fine particles in polluted air can weaken the brain’s protective barrier, offering a clue to the link between air pollution and dementia.

Air pollution has been linked to dementia for years. It is difficult, however, to work out what happens inside the brain between the time a person breathes in polluted air and the onset of memory problems.

An experiment in Ohio has provided some clues. After months of breathing polluted air, laboratory mice kept their weight, groomed themselves and went about their usual activities without obvious signs of distress.

Put those same mice in a maze they had already learned to navigate, though, and something was wrong.

Changes had taken place inside their brains, even in animals that had no genetic predisposition to Alzheimer’s-like disease.

Before working with mice, the team examined health records from 475,325 adults enrolled in the UK Biobank between 2006 and 2010.

The median age at enrollment was 58. Over a median follow-up period of nearly 14 years, 12,758 participants developed Alzheimer’s disease or a related dementia or died of an Alzheimer’s-related cause.

The researchers wanted to know how much of the risk was associated with where people lived and how much was associated with their genes.

Higher levels of fine particle pollution were linked to dementia, even after accounting for factors such as smoking and diabetes.

People with greater inherited susceptibility also faced higher risk, although the combined association was smaller than expected if the two individual effects multiplied.

For the animal experiments, the team used air collected outdoors in Cleveland, Ohio.

The particles of interest were PM2.5, a group of airborne particles measuring about one ten-thousandth of an inch (2.5 micrometers) or less.

The samples contained a mixture of substances associated with dust and combustion, including iron, nickel and copper.

The team concentrated the particles until their levels reached roughly 10 times those measured outdoors in Cleveland. The resulting concentration was comparable to the average reported for New Delhi in 2023.

At just four weeks old, mice began breathing either this polluted air or filtered air for eight hours a day, five days a week. Among them were animals carrying five genetic mutations associated with inherited Alzheimer’s disease.

Sixteen weeks into the experiment, the team began testing the animals’ memory.

One test involved a circular platform with an escape hole. Over three days, the mice had repeated opportunities to learn where the hole was, followed by a two-day break before their final test.

Ordinary mice breathing polluted air had about as much difficulty finding the hole as Alzheimer’s-prone mice breathing filtered air. The genetically vulnerable mice exposed to pollution struggled the most.

Other tests revealed problems recognizing unfamiliar objects, weaker muscles in the front and back legs, and behavior consistent with increased anxiety.

Blood vessels in the brain have a protective barrier that limits what can pass from the blood into surrounding brain tissue. Without a properly functioning barrier, potentially harmful substances have easier access to that tissue.

To check for leaks, the team used three chemical markers, including a blue dye.

Much more of these substances escaped from blood vessels in pollution-exposed mice than in ordinary mice breathing filtered air.

Some of the leakage measurements, however, came from groups of as few as two animals.

The brains of exposed mice also contained higher levels of several chemicals involved in inflammation.

Whether the inflammation damaged blood vessels or whether other processes contributed to both problems remains unclear.

Within the hippocampus, a region involved in forming memories, lies a smaller area called the dentate gyrus. New brain cells continue to form there after birth in mice.

Interestingly, pollution did not significantly affect how many new cells ordinary mice produced. The problem was keeping those cells alive, as fewer survived over a period of 30 days.

In mice prone to Alzheimer’s disease, both the production and survival of new cells suffered.

Pollution also disturbed the balance between two forms of a molecule called NAD, which cells depend on for energy production and processes involved in repairing damage.

Much of the investigation centered on microglia, the immune cells that live inside the brain. Sanjay Rajagopalan, a professor of medicine at Case Western Reserve University, co-led the work.

“By recreating in mice what happens when people breath polluted air, we found that pollution causes major changes in which genes are active in the brain’s immune cells,” said Sanjay Rajagopalan.

When the researchers compared polluted-air and filtered-air groups, they identified changes in the activity of 766 genes in ordinary mice. They also found that genetic background influenced how 2,876 genes responded to pollution.

“These cells normally keep the brain healthy by clearing away debris and fighting infection. Seeing how pollution disrupts them helps explain how a stress that affects much of the world’s population harms the brain, and it points us toward a way to treat it,” Rajagopalan said.

Alzheimer’s disease is associated with the accumulation of a protein called amyloid in the brain. As the protein collects, it forms deposits known as plaques.

Despite examining brain tissue with two different staining methods, the team could not find these plaques in ordinary mice that had breathed polluted air. Their memory problems had developed without detectable amyloid buildup.

Andrew Pieper, a professor of translational psychiatry at Case Western Reserve University, was also a senior author.

“We’ve identified a key way that air pollution harms the healthy brain and speeds up Alzheimer’s disease,” said Andrew Pieper.

How much pollution each person actually breathed remains unknown. The team estimated exposure using home addresses.

Only white participants and male mice were studied. Gene analysis involved just three mice per group.

“This helps us understand how factors in our environment can accelerate and intensify brain diseases of aging. It also opens the door to studying other environmental stresses on the brain, and to finding ways to keep the brain resilient even when we can’t avoid those stressors,” Pieper said.

Air pollution’s role in human dementia needs further investigation. But unlike inherited risk, pollution can be reduced.

The full study was published in the Journal of Neuroinflammation.
