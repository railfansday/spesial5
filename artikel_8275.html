How Useful Is An Alpine Climbing Award, Really?

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

The Ultar Sar team, of Sebastian Pelletti, Ethan Berman, and Maarten Van Haeren, accept their Piolet d'Or in Saint-Christophe-En-Oisans, France, in October 2026. (Photo: Piotr Drożdż)

When I learned I’d be attending a gala for the “Oscars of Alpinism,” I made a note to pack formal clothes. The Piolets d’Or, or Golden Ice Axes, is a big deal; a rare career-building opportunity in a professional alpinist’s life. Earning this award can transform a strong amateur into a full-fledged member of global alpinism’s elite. This year, I joined the recipients who’d been coaxed out of their humble mountain hideouts to partake in alpinism’s biggest award ceremony.

Established in 1992, the Piolets have a respectable mission: to promote difficult, alpine-style new routes on the world’s great mountains. But the award has weathered plenty of criticism over the years. After rejecting his Piolet nomination in 2006, U.K. legend Ian Parnell later wrote in Alpinist: “Is it possible to choose a winner in an inherently unquantifiable pursuit [like alpinism]?” Others decry the Piolets d’Or for awarding climbers who recently died in the mountains, as well as for holding a subjective line of what counts as good climbing style.

I’ve been critical of the Piolets at times myself, especially after hearing one well-known alpinist talk longingly of wanting to climb something worthy of the award. I winced thinking about anyone tackling runout mixed terrain at high altitude with a golden ice axe taunting them overhead.

I thought of these critics, and my own biases, during my transatlantic flight a few weeks ago to this year’s ceremony in Saint-Christophe-en-Oisans, France. The award clearly has a dark side. But it has also become increasingly important in the year 2026. Lately, I’ve watched the professional climbing landscape bend to the pressures of sponsorship and social-media engagement—physical performance is but one metric to make a modern-day alpinist’s career.

In this new landscape, we see some climbers eager to make a quick buck. Others orient ascents around appeasing future sponsors or gaining attention online. In some instances, this materializes as full-scale fraud. In others, imaginative white lies of what constitutes a “summit.”

Through this lens, the Piolets d’Or represents an institution that is slowly becoming necessary. Nearly every year for the last 34 years, a varied, rotating cast of legendary alpinists sifts through the reams of new climbing achievements. Tossing aside marketing fluff and half-baked route attempts, they present to the world the year’s genuine highpoints of alpinism.

I wanted to know: Does the good of the Piolets d’Or outweigh the bad?

The singular Piolet d’Or first launched with the aim of recognizing the greatest alpine ascent of the previous year. In true Oscars’ fashion, at the early Piolet, all of the potential candidates were invited to an in-person ceremony, where the sole winner was read out, for the first time, in front of a couple thousand people.

“It was a mess,” British alpinist Paul Ramsden, one of this year’s jury members, told me of the Piolet’s early days. He and countryman Mick Fowler had been invited to the ceremony in Paris in 2003, when their ascent of Siguniang (6,250m) in China was in the running. “We all got up onto the stage, and they read my and Mick’s names out, and a French team beside us burst into tears,” Ramsden said.

American Dean Potter was on stage too, in the running for his solo ascents of the Patagonian spires Cerro Torre and Fitz Roy. When he got snubbed, he was livid, Ramsden remembered. Potter screamed at the organizers, asking why they’d told him to fly all the way to Europe to stand on stage for an award they knew he’d never receive.

Later that evening, in a sign of the early Piolet d’Or attitude, a special party took place offsite to celebrate Ramsden and Fowler’s win. With his golden ice axe in hand, Ramsden remembered ambling over to the afterparty, only to be stopped by a bouncer. The club had a dress code, and Ramsden was wearing sneakers. “So we weren’t allowed into our own party,” Ramsden laughed.

The Piolet d’Or ran uninterrupted from 1992 to 2007, when mounting criticism came to a head. Most notable was from the Slovenian alpinist Marko Prezelj, who was invited to that year’s event after his ascent of Chomo Lhari (7,326m), on the border of Tibet. He walked slowly onto the stage, took a long pause, then refused to accept his award, explaining why he thought the Piolet was antithetical to his view of alpinism. “Comparing different climbs is not possible without some kind of personal involvement and even then it is difficult,” Prezelj later wrote. “Last year I climbed in Alaska, Patagonia, and Tibet. I cannot decide which expedition was the most… the ‘most what’ in fact?”

Prezelj and others had become increasingly uneasy with how the Piolet d’Or pitted alpinists against each other. With only one winner, everyone else, in their eyes, became a loser. Such was not the spirit of alpinism.

The organizers cancelled the ceremony in 2008 and took a beat to reimagine it. A year later, after personnel changes and plenty of disagreements, a new version of the Piolet emerged, complete with an updated charter. The now-plural Piolets rewarded a climb’s lightweight style, self-sufficiency, technical difficulty, and management of objective hazards. Most importantly, though, the Piolets adopted a more community-based attitude. Everyone who was nominated for the award was given a Piolet d’Or.

This year’s ceremony was devoid of the drama that tainted the Piolet’s past. No one walked onto the stage just to reject the award. There was no French team in tears, and—as far as I could tell—no leery feeling of one-upsmanship among the award recipients. It was a bunch of climbers, wearing sneakers and duct-taped down jackets, drinking a bit too much red wine.

Paul Ramsden, who is 57, has been to a number of these week-long parties over the years, five times as a recipient—the most of any climber—and multiple times again as a jury member. Despite the criticism it’s received over the years, and his own jarring experience in 2003, he is now a proud supporter of the Piolets d’Or.

“I see awful style from climbers in some parts of the world,” he told me, citing large teams with bolt kits and hundreds of meters of fixed ropes, all but assured to succeed with enough dogged, industrial determination. “By rewarding alpine-style climbs, we’re not really rewarding the recipients. We’re signalling to the rest of the climbing community what ‘good’ style is.” In practice, that means that if a climber doesn’t have the skill to scale an unclimbed face with the same hardware they might take to a crag, they should leave the objective for a future generation of climbers who can.

One alpinist, who wished to remain anonymous, but has been nominated for a Piolet before, emphasized how flimsy and subjective any notion of “good” climbing style is. In a phone call with me, they noted how the Piolets jury considers climbing in insulated boots and ultralight crampons with Google-Earth reconnaissance as “good” climbing style, but considered carrying a few extra ropes to fix sections of the route poor form. The jury considers climbing in a lightweight team of two an enviable expression of alpine self sufficiency, yet a team of five or six is antiquated and ungainly.

Even understated, minimalist climbing expeditions to the Himalaya these days require a cottage-industry of resources: international flights, bus rides, porters, cook tents, tens of thousands of dollars of equipment—the list goes on. Deciding what the “correct” amount of resources an expedition should use is an inexact science, and one which the Piolets jury is still figuring out.

“I think it’s cool that we have a pretty objective governing body deciding what is actually good for alpine climbing,” Australian-Argentine climber Sebastian Pelletti, a recipient of a Piolet this year, told me. Like me, Pelletti has noticed professional alpinists erroneously claiming a “new” route to their sponsors and the world.

Last year, I wrote about one of those so-called new routes on Jirishanca (6,094m), in Peru’s Cordillera Huayhuash. Three very famous European alpinists climbed about a thousand meters of new terrain to the mountain’s summit ridge. Above, a spine of unconsolidated snow—the crux of the mountain—proved too difficult for the team. They bailed. A few days later, the climbers and their sponsors plastered a topo for this new route far and wide, drawing a bright red line that stopped on Jirishanca’s “east summit.” The east summit does not exist, nor has it ever been considered a final destination on the mountain. It is a brief bump in an otherwise vertical landscape, at least six pitches below Jirishanca’s actual summit.

When I wrote that article, some people called it a hit piece; I thought I was going light on the climbers. So when the Piolets d’Or announced their “long list” of notable ascents of 2025 earlier this year, I combed through it, seeing which routes made the cut. (Not all long-listed ascents are short-listed for the Piolets d’Or. The long list aims to recognize all new alpine climbs accomplished in good style.)

The Jirishanca “east summit” team was absent in the Peru section; the jury had not even considered their effort a route. But also within Peru’s long list, in the very same Huayhuash range, was a route on a mountain I had not heard much about, by climbers far less famous than their peers down valley. It was about the same length as the Jirishanca route, and just as difficult, but it went to the summit of Yerupajá Grande (6,635 m) and down the opposite side of the mountain. The climbers called it The Essence of Commitment, and they were being awarded the highest prize in alpinism.
