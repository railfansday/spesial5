With no new pennies being made for circulation, a store short on them may round your cash total to the nearest nickel, up or down, while card payments stay exact. Every penny you have is still worth a cent, so spend them or deposit them at your bank instead of paying a coin kiosk’s cash-out fee.

Each penny cost the U.S. Mint 3.69 cents to make and distribute in fiscal 2024, according to its 2024 annual report, so on Nov. 12, 2025, the Mint struck its last one for circulation.

As pennies run short, a store can round a cash total instead of making exact change. The method recommended most often, cited in the Treasury Department’s guidance, rounds a total ending in 1, 2, 6, or 7 cents down to the nearest nickel and one ending in 3, 4, 8, or 9 up. A $10.42 total becomes $10.40, and $10.43 becomes $10.45. The Common Cents Act, which Congress passed in September 2026 and sent to the President for signature on Nov. 12, 2026, would allow the same rounding nationwide when a store can’t make exact change.

The guidance and the act both apply rounding to cash only, so card, check, and other electronic payments keep their exact total. Rounding costs shoppers little. The Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond found that more cash totals end in 3, 4, 8, or 9, but it puts the cost at about $6 million a year across 258 million U.S. adults, or roughly 2 cents each.

Spend the pennies you already have on cash purchases. They keep their full value, and the Treasury Department encourages spending them.

Look through a big jar first. A few rare pennies sell to collectors for far more than a cent. Pennies dated 1943 were made of steel, and a copper one struck by mistake sold for over $1 million in 2017, according to the American Numismatic Association.

Then deposit the rest at your bank or credit union, which may ask you to roll a large batch first. Coinstar’s kiosks keep up to 15.9% of the total plus up to 99 cents when they pay out cash, as much as $4.17 on $20 in coins.
