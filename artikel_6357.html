When rain returns after a drought, groundwater reserves don’t always get caught up before the next drought

Tanggal: 2026-11-10

Most groundwater refills faster than it drains in a drought, but in about a quarter of places, the next drought hits before recovery ends.

When a drought breaks, the rain returns first, then the soil moisture, the plants and the rivers. Groundwater usually comes last, and in some places the next drought arrives before it has caught up.

A new global analysis suggests that, in most places, groundwater refills faster than it drains during a drought. But across about a quarter of the places studied, water levels tended to slip back into drought before they had fully recovered.

That matters because groundwater supplies roughly 35 percent of all the fresh water people withdraw, and it’s the reserve many communities rely on when rivers run low.

Hydrologist Sandra Hauswirth led the work with three colleagues at Utrecht University in the Netherlands. Their results come from a computer model rather than direct measurements.

It estimates how deep you’d have to dig to reach water, a level called the water table, month by month from 1960 to 2019.

This model divides the world’s land into squares about 0.6 mile (1 kilometer) across. It was developed at Utrecht’s Department of Physical Geography, and it tracks both the rain that soaks into soil and the water people pump out of it.

Checked against real wells, the model gave useful estimates at about 80 percent of wells shallower than about 200 feet (60 meters). For deeper wells, the figure was about 70 percent.

For every square and every calendar month, the team worked out how deep the water table usually was and how much that depth normally varied.

A drought started when the groundwater dropped so far below its usual level for that time of year that only about 16 percent of months would qualify. It ended when the water rose back above that line.

Droughts lasting two months or less were left out, because monthly data can’t capture them reliably. For the rest, the team measured how fast the water fell, how fast it rose again, and what it did in the six months afterward.

Climate did more than anything else to set the pace of recovery. Places where a lot of rain soaks into the ground, such as the Amazon and Southeast Asia, recovered quickly. Their droughts also tended to be shorter and more frequent.

Dry regions recovered slowly. The slow places included Australia, the Middle East and Central Asia, southern Africa, southern South America and the U.S. Midwest.

During drought recovery, the groundwater at a typical location rose about 0.7 inches (1.8 centimeters) a month. In the fastest places it climbed as much as about 3 feet (1 meter) a month.

Those raw speeds partly reflect how much a water table swings in an ordinary year. So the team also compared each place with its own normal range, and the split between wet and dry regions became sharper.

Average speed told only part of the story. Two places could recover at the same average rate and still behave very differently during a drought.

Hauswirth hadn’t expected recovery to vary so much within a single region. In an interview with Earth.com, she said, “I was surprised to see that the groundwater recovery pattern was not necessarily homogeneous within a region and could vary quite a bit locally.”

To capture those differences, the team sorted every location by how its droughts unfolded. About 57 percent were resilient: on average, the water came back faster than it had dropped. Another 26 percent were unstable, slipping back into drought before they had fully recovered.

That makes the unstable group the second largest. The researchers described its behavior as even more critical than that of the vulnerable group, where the water fell quickly and climbed back slowly.

The unstable group covered about 15 percent of places, and only around 2 percent were stable, with the fall and the rise at about the same speed.

Unstable places weren’t clustered in one part of the world, and they weren’t limited to heavily pumped areas. Their droughts were shorter, milder and more frequent than in any other group, often with little time to refill in between.

Terrain set the vulnerable places apart. In every region the team examined up close, they turned up at higher elevations, where the ground stores less water and the water table swings more widely.

In Europe, some may be linked to limestone country, where water drains through cracks and caves.

The close-ups included California, Spain, India and the North China Plain, all places known for heavy groundwater use. In Spain, the vulnerable and unstable spots shared similar pumping patterns. In California, pumping appeared to affect more of the categories.

Sorting places this way, Hauswirth said, made it clear that local features “are an important part of the drivers and should not be underestimated.”

For water managers in vulnerable regions, her advice starts with knowing where the water comes from. Where possible, she said, they should map how much of local demand and supply is covered by groundwater.

That “could help us better understand when groundwater resources are overexploited,” she told Earth.com, and when a wet spell could help restore levels, possibly by cutting back on pumping.

The authors name their own blind spots. They filtered out places where water tables have fallen steadily for decades, often from heavy pumping, because a long decline makes single droughts hard to pick out. Those places may be underrepresented in the results.

Every number here is also a model’s estimate. This model performed well against real wells, but global groundwater models have both overestimated and underestimated water levels in the past.

And because the team averaged 60 years together, the results can’t show whether recovery is speeding up or slowing down.

“I want to dive deeper into how these recovery patterns have changed over time, as the current work has mainly focused on the spatial pattern,” Hauswirth said.

Next, the team wants to pair the four categories with projections of a warmer climate. That could show which places are at risk of shifting from resilient toward vulnerable or unstable.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Research: Water.
