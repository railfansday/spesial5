A mother bat’s heart actually beats faster the second she hears her own baby calling in a crowded colony

Tanggal: 2026-11-13

Mother bats' heart rates rose only for their own pups' calls, and the strongest reaction came from calls the pups made after learning to fly.

A newborn bat’s distress call does the job of a human baby’s cry. The researchers who study these bats expected it to be the sound that a mother reacts to most. It isn’t.

When mother Japanese house bats heard recordings of their own pups, their hearts sped up, while recordings of other mothers’ pups barely changed their pulse.

And the biggest rise came from a sound that isn’t a cry at all. It was the echolocation calls their pups made at a month old, once the young bats could fly.

For a mother raising pups in a dark roost shared by many other families, that heartbeat is a sign of recognition that can’t be detected by watching her.

Japanese house bats raise their young in colonies of 50 to 200 animals, with many mothers and pups roosting together.

Each mother looks after her own, and a companion study from the same laboratory found that mothers retrieve their own pups and not the pups of other mothers.

Kazuki Yoshino-Hashizawa, the study’s lead author, works at Doshisha University in Kyoto and the Institute of Science Tokyo. He said the idea came from watching the laboratory colony.

“When a pup was separated from their roosting area, only the biological mother immediately rushed over to bring it back,” he said.

In an interview with Earth.com, he compared a mother bat’s heart rate reading to a lie detector, which catches reactions a person never shows.

“Just as with humans, overt behavior doesn’t always tell the whole story – we all experience internal emotional states that aren’t immediately visible outside,” he said.

In May 2022 the team caught four pregnant females from a colony roosting in bridge girders near the Doshisha campus in Kyotanabe. Between June 20 and 22, each gave birth to three pups. Two died soon after birth, which left ten.

A newborn weighed about 0.04 ounces (1.1 to 1.3 grams), roughly the weight of a paper clip. Every day for a month, the researchers separated each pup from its mother for three minutes and recorded its calls.

Until they were about 10 to 12 days old, the bat pups gave isolation calls, the long, low-pitched sounds a pup makes when it is away from its mother.

At around eight days, the first echolocation calls appeared. Those are the short, high-pitched pulses that bats use to find their way in the dark. By 30 days the pups’ calls matched an adult’s, and the young bats could fly.

The heart recordings were taken 38 to 45 days after the births. Younger pups can’t hold their body temperature for more than an hour, the authors wrote, so earlier testing would have put them at risk.

The team recorded each mother’s heartbeat through three small electrodes placed under her skin. In a soundproof booth, a speaker 6 inches (15 centimeters) away played her three-second clips of calls. A resting bat’s heart runs at 200 to 300 beats a minute.

Each mother heard five kinds of clip. Four were pup calls: her own pups’ isolation calls from day two, and echolocation calls from day 30. She also heard the same two call types from other mothers’ pups.

The fifth was the echolocation calls of an adult male she had never met.

Only male pups’ calls were used, because some litters had no females. One mother died before the second day of testing.

Her own pups’ isolation calls raised a mother bat’s heart rate by about 70 beats a minute. Other pups’ calls produced small increases that could have been due to chance. All four mothers reacted more to their own pups than to the others.

The researchers expected the newborn distress calls to matter most, since those calls exist to bring a mother to a lost pup.

Instead, the echolocation calls of her own month-old pups produced the largest rise, about 130 beats a minute at its peak. A mother’s response didn’t fade as her pups grew up and their voices changed.

“We were intrigued to find that physiological responsiveness is not just a simple, fixed reflex to infant distress, but something that adapts as calls develop,” Yoshino-Hashizawa told Earth.com.

He said it suggests mothers stay highly sensitive to their own pups’ voices as the pups move toward independence.

Because all the families shared one cage, the mothers had heard the other pups daily, so familiarity alone doesn’t explain why their own pups stood out. The team’s best guess for the month-old calls is memory.

Mother goats and sea lions appear to update their memory of their young’s calls as those calls change. By the time of the test, the day-two cries were more than a month old. The researchers can’t settle this yet, because the two call types also came from pups of different ages.

The team hadn’t planned for one result: the unfamiliar adult male’s calls also sent heart rates up. The mothers, about 40 days past giving birth and not in mating season, may have reacted defensively to a strange male’s voice, the authors suggest.

A faster heart rate shows a mother bat is aroused, not whether she is drawn to the sound or alarmed by it.

Asked how he would tell the two apart, Yoshino-Hashizawa pointed to heart rate variability, the small changes in timing from one beat to the next. It helps separate the two in people, but nobody knows what a normal reading looks like in a bat.

“Their heart rates fluctuate dramatically depending on their physiological state – ranging from deep torpor to intense flight,” he said. A version that fits bats is his group’s next goal.

All of this rests on four mothers and 135 usable heart recordings. Pregnant bats are hard to breed in captivity, the authors wrote, and larger groups are needed to generalize the pattern.

Until then, four mothers’ heartbeats say a bat knows her own pups’ voices long after they stop crying for her. Whether what she feels is care or alarm is still an open question.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Ethology.
