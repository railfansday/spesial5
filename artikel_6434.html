Common pregnancy supplement linked to less fussiness in babies

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

A new study suggests that vitamin C supplements during pregnancy may be linked to less fussiness and fewer negative emotions in babies at six months.

Women who took more vitamin C in supplements during pregnancy tended to have less fussy babies at six months, a study in Oregon found.

The finding offers a clue to a question researchers have been trying to answer: how might a mother’s distress during pregnancy relate to her baby’s early emotions?

The team followed 302 pregnant women around Portland. Those who reported more depression, anxiety, and stress tended to have babies who showed more negative emotions, and they also took less vitamin C in supplements.

Differences in supplement use accounted for a small part of that relationship.

Vitamin C from food showed no similar pattern, and the study cannot establish whether supplements actually influenced the babies’ behavior.

Elizabeth Wood, an assistant professor of psychiatry at Oregon Health and Science University, led the work.

She and senior author Elinor Sullivan, a professor of psychiatry and behavioral neuroscience, work with the university’s Center for Mental Health Innovation.

Our bodies can’t make vitamin C. During pregnancy, a baby therefore relies entirely on its mother’s supply.

The fetus usually gets priority, so a newborn can have more vitamin C in its umbilical cord blood than its mother has in her blood. But research in guinea pigs found that this advantage disappeared when the mother herself ran short.

In those animals, a shortage during pregnancy also left the young with a smaller hippocampus, the part of the brain involved in memory. The brain normally holds high concentrations of vitamin C.

Pregnancy raises the body’s need for the vitamin. The recommended daily amount for adult women rises from 75 milligrams before pregnancy to 85 milligrams during it.

The women gave birth between 2019 and 2021, and most of the information came from weeks 37 to 39 of pregnancy.

Each woman filled out five questionnaires about depression, anxiety, and stress, which the team combined into one measure of distress.

To find out what the women ate, dietitians asked them to recall all the food and drink they’d had in the previous 24 hours. They repeated the exercise on three unscheduled days over two weeks.

The women also listed their supplements, including the name, brand, dose, and how often they took each one. Photographs of the bottle labels helped the researchers check those details.

About six months after birth, caregivers filled out a questionnaire about their babies. One question asked how often a baby cried when frustrated.

The researchers wanted to measure “negative affect,” a term for how readily someone feels and shows emotions such as sadness and irritability.

High levels in infancy can be an early sign of greater risk for mental health problems later, though a fussy baby won’t necessarily develop one.

The pattern involving supplements was clear enough to show up in the analysis: more vitamin C from supplements went along with less negative affect in babies.

Women with more distress took less of it, and their babies tended to show more negative emotions.

Vitamin C from meals had no reliable connection to the babies’ emotions. Nor did the amount women got from food relate to the amount they took in supplements.

Together, the findings suggested that supplement intake accounted for a small part of the link between distress during pregnancy and a baby’s emotions.

The team allowed for differences in the mothers’ ages, diet quality, and whether they’d given birth before. They also took into account the mothers’ distress six months after delivery.

Because the body absorbs a smaller proportion of vitamin C at higher doses, the researchers capped the amount counted from supplements at 500 milligrams per day.

The results held up when they used another calculation to account for poor absorption.

Total vitamin C from food and supplements still went along with fewer negative emotions in babies. However, that combined amount no longer explained the connection with the mothers’ distress.

The study didn’t establish why more distressed women took less vitamin C in supplements.

The authors suggested that women with less distress may find it easier to keep up a daily habit, perhaps because they’re in better overall health or find health information easier to understand.

Household income wasn’t linked to supplement intake in this group. Knowledge, motivation, and a doctor’s advice might have more to do with the habit, the authors wrote.

“The most encouraging takeaway here is that nutrition is relatively modifiable, especially when people have adequate resources and support,” Sullivan said.

“Vitamin C supplements are inexpensive, easy to access and something patients can start incorporating immediately and consistently into their daily lives.”

The team didn’t test the women’s blood for vitamin C, which might have given a better idea of what reached their babies. Supplement amounts came from labels, and US supplements face limited regulation.

Nearly all the women took prenatal vitamins, which contain several nutrients at once. Even after the researchers adjusted for this, other nutrients or generally healthier habits could still explain some of the findings.

About 73% of the women were White and non-Hispanic, so the results may not apply equally to other groups. The data are openly available through the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive.

About 25% of pregnant women in the US get less vitamin C from food than the estimated average requirement of 70 milligrams a day, the authors noted.

A 2019 analysis in JAMA Network Open found that counting supplements brought that share down to 11.5%, a drop of more than half.

Only 48 women in the Oregon study fell below the 70-milligram mark. Within that small group, supplements didn’t measurably weaken the connection between distress and babies’ emotions.

The authors thought there were probably too few women in this group to draw a firm conclusion.

Taking a supplement may be easier than changing a whole diet or easing distress during pregnancy, the researchers wrote.

They proposed randomized trials, which assign some women to take vitamin C and others to a comparison group, to find out whether it helps.

The authors pointed to vitamin C’s low toxicity as a reason to explore the idea.

The daily upper intake limit for pregnant adults is 2,000 milligrams from all sources, a safety limit rather than a recommended supplement dose.

The team also plans to follow the children as they grow and look for later clinical diagnoses. “Nutrition is a crucial component for a healthy pregnancy, and as we’re learning more and more, also for a healthy early childhood,” Wood said.

For now, the findings raise an intriguing possibility: a mother’s vitamin C intake during pregnancy may be connected to her baby’s early emotional development, but whether supplements make a difference remains unknown.

The study is published in the journal Developmental Psychology.
