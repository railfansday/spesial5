Did viruses borrow their shells from bacteria? New evidence raises questions

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Tiny viruses found in gut DNA carry a virus protein shell much like the boxes bacteria use for chemistry, and the design crossed both ways.

Many viruses have a remarkably simple design: a protein shell containing their genetic material.

Bacteria build almost the same shell and use it as a sealed container for chemical reactions, with enzymes inside instead of genes. For years the leading idea was that bacteria had borrowed the design from viruses.

Now a search through billions of DNA fragments has turned up tiny viruses whose shell proteins look more like the bacterial version.

The family tree they belong to runs in both directions. The researchers found evidence that the shell may have passed between viruses and cells at least three times, in both directions.

Abelardo Aguilar, a Ph.D. student in the Department of Biology at the University of Miami, led the search with Antoni Luque, an associate professor of biology there.

The team never grew a single virus. Everything came from DNA sequences in public databases, read from soil, seawater, wastewater, and the human gut.

“The majority of viruses cannot be grown in the lab,” Luque said.

“But now through sequencing, we have access to information on viruses for which we don’t know the host, that we can’t culture in the lab, but that are everywhere.”

The protein at the center of the story folds into a shape biologists call the HK97 fold, and copies of it assemble into hollow shells.

In a large family of viruses, that shell is the capsid, the case around the genome.

In bacteria and in archaea, a separate branch of single-celled life, the same fold builds a container called an encapsulin, which cells use to store iron and run other chemistry.

The two versions differ in size. Most encapsulins are built from 60 copies of the protein.

Many viral capsids are larger and have additional assembly machinery, including scaffolds, DNA-packaging portals, and sometimes tails.

The size gap is the problem with the old story. If encapsulins came from viruses, the simpler structure evolved from the more complex one, and that idea never said how.

Aguilar’s team reasoned that if the two shells were related, there might be viruses nobody had found yet, small enough to sit between them.

The smallest known tailed virus of this family has a genome 11,600 base pairs long, the paired letters that DNA is written in.

The team sifted more than 14.4 billion DNA fragments, including those in IMG/M, run by the Department of Energy’s Joint Genome Institute.

They kept only fragments that looked like complete viral genomes, carried this virus family’s hallmark genes, and fell below that line. That left 4,730 candidate genomes.

Each genome’s shell protein was then checked against the sequence signatures of encapsulins, with AlphaFold and other AI tools predicting the proteins’ shapes.

Among the genomes between 5,000 and 11,600 letters long, 93 of 491 shell proteins, nearly 19%, carried encapsulin signatures. In a control set of slightly larger viral genomes, 2.5% did.

The biggest cluster of these encapsulin-like viruses, 76 of the 93, came from human gut samples. Their genomes averaged about 8,750 letters.

None carried a portal, a tail, or the motor that known viruses of this family use to pack DNA into the shell.

Genes they did carry included ones for splicing themselves into a host bacterium’s chromosome and lying dormant there.

So how do these viruses copy themselves with no portal? The authors suggested they may depend on other viruses for the missing parts, or may carry single-stranded DNA and pack it through pores in the shell.

Several of their genes fit the second idea, though it remains untested, the team wrote.

The team built family trees of the HK97 fold three ways, from the proteins’ sequences, from their predicted shapes, and from which features they share.

All three methods produced groups in which viruses and encapsulins sat side by side.

In one group, a family of encapsulins clustered with viruses that infect soil bacteria.

In another, a virus that infects archaea clustered with an archaeal encapsulin rather than with other viruses.

At least three separate crossings, in the team’s reading, and not all in the same direction.

A capsid is built in stages. The shell proteins first assemble around the scaffold into a smaller, empty form called a procapsid.

As the genome is packaged, the procapsid expands and matures into the finished viral shell.

Aguilar’s team compared a bacterial encapsulin with a procapsid and finished capsid from a Staphylococcus aureus genetic element.

The two proteins share only 14% of their sequence. Yet the encapsulin and the procapsid are almost the same size inside, about 1.3 millionths of an inch (33 nanometers) across.

Inside an encapsulin sits an enzyme. Inside a procapsid sits the scaffold, and the two proteins turned out to share a similar helical structure.

So the authors proposed that whatever sits inside the shell decides what the shell becomes. An enzyme turns it into a reactor. A scaffold lets it expand and take on a genome.

“One implication of this research is the possibility that viral capsids could also facilitate biochemical reactions, which would open up the potential roles that capsids have in environments as a whole,” Aguilar explained.

The study doesn’t settle whether the first HK97 shell held genes or enzymes.

The deep branches of the family tree are too poorly supported to say, and the authors wrote that repeated crossings may have erased the signature of the earliest steps.

The authors’ best guess, which they labeled speculative, is that the earliest shells were built from 12 to 24 proteins, too small to hold any genome.

In their model, chemistry came first and genes later, once the shells grew to 60 proteins.

Whether procapsids in living bacteria run reactions during an infection is what no one has measured.

Infected cells often produce empty procapsids, the authors wrote, so a lab could test that directly.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Communications.
