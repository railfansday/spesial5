Britain’s coastal floods have a surprising connection to distant skies

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and Arctic help explain where extreme storm surges strike Britain’s coast and northwestern Europe.

Storm surges look like local disasters, but which stretch of Britain’s coast floods can often be traced to air pressure patterns over the Atlantic and the Arctic.

Those far-off patterns help decide whether northern or southern coasts take the high water.

A surge is hard to forecast, because wind, low air pressure, and the shape of the coast all play a part at once.

Understanding why surges hit certain places, such as East Anglia in eastern England, could give coastal towns earlier flood warnings.

Lisanne Blok of the Department of Earth Sciences at the University of Cambridge led the work, with colleagues at the National Oceanography Centre and the British Antarctic Survey.

Her team studied more than 70 years of sea levels around northwestern Europe.

A computer model rebuilt those levels day by day from 1950 onward, using past weather data rather than tide gauge readings.

The team first removed the tides and the normal seasonal rise and fall of the sea. Then they used machine learning to sort the coastline into regions that tend to get extreme surges on the same days.

They ended up with six regions across the seas of northwestern Europe, plus eight smaller ones inside the North Sea.

“If we can identify conditions emerging in, for example, the Atlantic that might result in a storm surge in East Anglia, then we can pinpoint parts of the coast that should prepare for flooding,” said Blok.

Big, repeating pressure patterns over the ocean are what scientists call climate modes.

“Computer models used to forecast risk of coastal flooding don’t always account for large-scale climate modes, instead focusing on localized weather variables,” she said. “But what our findings tell us is that climate modes are really key.”

A storm surge builds when strong winds and low air pressure push a bulge of seawater toward land.

Sometimes it rises high enough to spill over flood defenses. If that happens at high tide, with big waves on top, the sea can reach extreme heights and flood the coast.

Over January 31 and February 1, 1953, a deep low-pressure system moved down the North Sea and pushed a huge mass of water along Britain’s east coast.

It arrived with a spring tide, the extra-high tide that comes around a new or full moon, and sea levels rose about 10 feet (3 meters) above predicted levels.

Floods swept through parts of Britain, the Netherlands, and Belgium.

Another surge struck Britain’s east coast on December 5 and 6, 2013, with water levels in some places higher than during the 1953 flood, but modern defenses prevented a disaster on that scale.

The North Sea’s narrow funnel shape, tightest between East Anglia and the Netherlands, pushes water toward land. Its shallow floor, roughly 30 to 490 feet (10 to 150 meters) deep, makes the surge taller.

On the worst 1% of days in the rebuilt records, surges added at least 22 inches (57 centimeters) to the North Sea’s water level, more than in any other region.

“The storm surges are very dynamic and complex in this region,” said Blok. “There is also heightened risk, because lots of people live along the low-lying coastal regions bordering the North Sea.”

Next, the team compared each region’s surges with those big pressure patterns.

“We can link regional storm surge extremes to very distinct climate patterns,” Blok said. “I didn’t expect such clear relationships, especially across the large distances involved.”

The strongest links were to pressure patterns across the Northern Hemisphere, including the North Atlantic Oscillation, a seesaw in air pressure between the regions around Iceland and the Azores.

When Iceland’s pressure is low compared with the Azores, the jet stream, a fast wind high up, moves north, and surges become more likely on northern and central coasts.

“As the storms shift to the north, they also deliver high seas to more northerly coasts,” said Blok.

When pressure over Iceland is unusually high instead, the pressure difference with the Azores shrinks.

The jet stream slows and bends south. Storms then take southern routes, bringing high seas to southern coasts.

Storms behind extreme surges off southwest England and west of Ireland tended to start near Newfoundland, off eastern Canada, thousands of miles away.

Storms behind surges farther north, in the seas toward Norway, more often began off Greenland or in the central North Atlantic.

Those southwestern surges were also more common in autumn, while the other regions saw most of theirs in winter.

Ali Mashayek, an associate professor in Cambridge’s Department of Earth Sciences, was the study’s senior author.

“Conditions in the Arctic have a big impact on UK weather,” he said. “That’s something we’ve known for a while, although we haven’t been able to link it to storm surge distribution before.”

Mashayek said Arctic warming will weaken the pressure difference that powers the jet stream, and “that means it is likely we will see a shift in UK storm patterns, including where storm surges occur, in future.”

But he added that “this is something we are still trying to understand.”

It’s time to view local weather extremes in a global context, Mashayek said. “We now have more evidence that the extreme weather in our backyard may be driven by what happens in very distant parts of the world.”

“This speaks to the broader issue that climate change and the extremes it induces are a truly global problem that need global solutions.”

The surge records left out waves and long-term sea level rise. The team also didn’t check whether the links change from season to season.

Next, the researchers want to run the same analysis on future climate projections, to see whether the surge regions shift as the planet warms.

The full study was published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.
