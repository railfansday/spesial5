Mountains rise above a sea of clouds in the Pacific Northwest

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

A blanket of marine clouds covered the Pacific Northwest, leaving mountain peaks rising above the clouds like islands in the sky.

Today’s Image of the Day from NASA Earth Observatory captures a spectacular blanket of marine clouds stretching across the Pacific Northwest.

From space, the mountains appear to rise from a white sea.

A broad layer of low clouds covered much of western Washington and Oregon on September 19, 2026, stretching from the Pacific coast toward the foothills of the Cascade Range.

Only the higher terrain escaped the cloudy blanket. Peaks in Washington’s Olympic Mountains and parts of Oregon’s Coast Range poked through the clouds like islands.

NASA’s Terra satellite captured the striking scene at about 10:45 a.m. Pacific Time.

The image was captured by the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS), an instrument aboard NASA’s Terra satellite that has observed Earth for more than two decades.

From orbit, MODIS can track clouds, smoke, vegetation, snow, ice, ocean conditions, and other features across large areas of the planet.

On this September morning, its view revealed just how far marine clouds had traveled inland.

Clouds also stretched far offshore over the Pacific Ocean.

Farther north, a different kind of haze appeared over the landscape as smoke from wildland fires collected in valleys of the North Cascades.

The widespread cloud cover over western Washington and Oregon resulted from cool, moist air moving inland from the ocean.

For several nights, marine air had been flowing onshore across the region.

This pattern can produce marine stratus – low, relatively uniform clouds that commonly develop along coastal areas.

The Pacific Ocean provides a huge source of moisture, while differences in temperature between the ocean, land, and atmosphere help determine where clouds form and how far they travel.

During the night and early morning, cool ocean air can move inland beneath warmer air higher in the atmosphere.

That arrangement creates what meteorologists call a temperature inversion.

Normally, air becomes colder with increasing altitude through the lower atmosphere. During an inversion, however, warmer air sits above cooler air near the surface.

The warmer layer acts like a lid, limiting the ability of the cooler air below to rise and mix with the atmosphere above.

When moist air trapped near the surface cools enough, water vapor condenses into tiny droplets. If enough droplets collect, a layer of stratus clouds develops.

The marine push was particularly strong during the early hours of September 19.

Cool, moist air reached well beyond the coastline, carrying clouds eastward until they approached the western foothills of the Cascades.

In some places, the cloud layer reached all the way to the ground.

At that point, what appears from space as a cloud becomes fog for people standing beneath it.

Clouds and fog are essentially the same phenomenon; the distinction largely depends on whether the suspended water droplets are touching the surface.

For residents below, the morning would have looked gray and muted. From hundreds of miles above Earth, however, the same weather pattern created a dramatic landscape.

Mountain summits rose through the smooth cloud deck while lower valleys and coastal terrain disappeared beneath it.

The Olympic Mountains offered one of the most striking examples.

Much of the lower terrain was hidden, leaving the tallest sections exposed above the cloud layer.

Similar features appeared along Oregon’s Coast Range.

Scenes like this also reveal how strongly topography shapes regional weather.

Mountains can redirect airflow, force air upward, influence rainfall, and help determine how far marine air penetrates inland.

The Cascades form an especially important barrier between the moist climate west of the range and the generally drier landscapes farther east.

On September 19, the marine layer had pushed far inland, but it did not last all day.

Several hours after Terra passed overhead, NASA’s Aqua satellite offered another look at the Pacific Northwest.

By about 4:15 p.m. Pacific Time, skies had cleared across much of western Washington and Oregon.

The transformation illustrates the daily rhythm of marine clouds.

As sunlight warms the land during the day, temperatures near the surface rise and atmospheric mixing increases. That process can weaken the inversion and break apart low clouds.

Yet the clearing was not complete. Some marine clouds remained pressed against the Oregon coast, where temperatures stayed cooler than they did farther inland.

The contrast captured during the day demonstrates how quickly coastal weather can change.

A landscape almost completely hidden beneath clouds in the morning can emerge only hours later.

From the ground, that shift might feel like an ordinary transition from a foggy morning to a sunny afternoon.

From orbit, it becomes something larger: an entire region briefly wrapped in a white blanket, with mountains rising above it like islands in the sky.
