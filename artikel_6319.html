One of Denmark’s last hunter-gatherers ate seafood as a boy, but his teeth hint that farming arrived during his lifetime

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

A man buried on Sejerø around 3800 BC had unmixed hunter-gatherer DNA, yet from his teens his diet held more land food, possibly from farms.

In 1956, people on the Danish island of Sejerø were digging for artifacts in a kitchen midden known as Rødhals. They found a skeleton under 8 inches (0.2 meters) of unbroken shell layer. Kalundborg Folkeblad, the local paper, ran a photo of the find on October 22.

Seventy years on, the man in that grave is the most recent securely dated person from Denmark with unmixed hunter-gatherer ancestry and a seafood diet.

That puts him among the last hunter-gatherers of the country. Yet one of his teeth shows his diet taking in more and more land food from his teens.

Anders Fischer of the University of Gothenburg and John Meadows of the Leibniz Centre for Archaeology at Schloss Gottorf in Germany led the work with 15 co-authors from universities and museums in six countries.

The land food may have come from farms, they suggest. The paper admits the isotopes alone cannot show whether it came from farming or from hunting and gathering.

The Danish National Museum sent archaeologist Ole Klindt-Jensen to record and save the burial in a hurry. According to the paper he worked “in the pouring rain.” He never published his field notes, which sit in the museum archive as NM1 1069/56.

On his sketch the man lies on his back, arms down along his body, legs straight, skull to the west. Two large stones face the body with their flat sides.

The bigger is possibly a moraine boulder that was already there, while the smaller apparently stood on edge by design.

Between the right forearm and the lower ribs, Klindt-Jensen noted a bone object about 5.5 inches (140 millimeters) long, a “skinning knife” in his words.

A museum employee later changed the entry to a piece of bone rolled by water. The employee seems to have discarded the object.

Going by graves of the early Funnel Beaker culture, the first farmers in the region, from about 3800 to 3500 BC, Fischer’s team thinks it may have been an archer’s wrist guard.

If so, it was the only grave good recovered. The imperfect conditions of the dig could have missed beads or arrowheads.

He stood about 5 feet 1 inch (1.55 meters) tall and died at 30 to 40 years of age, perhaps closer to 40 judging by the degenerative changes in his bones.

The skeleton is almost complete, with heavy, well-preserved bones. Tooth wear and the lengths of his two upper arm bones say he was right-handed.

Nothing on the skeleton points to violence, and the bones reflect good health and nutrition in early life. Traces of inflammation or vitamin deficiency probably date to later life.

Both Achilles tendons had partly turned to bone at the heel, and his right knee showed moderate wear. Long hours of paddling could explain the knee and heels, the team says. In a paddling position, his right leg would have been bent and the left stretched back.

Bony outgrowths also appeared at the elbows and wrists. Marie Louise Jørkov of the University of Copenhagen photographed them for the Laboratory of Biological Anthropology.

His genome came out in Nature in 2024, in a study by Morten Allentoft and colleagues, under the code NEO645. He carried typical Western Hunter-Gatherer ancestry, unmixed with farmer ancestry. Going by genetics alone he probably had dark skin, with an 85 percent chance of blue eyes.

To get at his diet, the team used up the root of his lower right first premolar and cut thin layers of dentine from it, each formed at a different age.

They measured carbon, nitrogen and sulfur isotopes in every layer, in the crown, and in a sample from his left femur. That covers roughly age seven to adulthood.

Comparison material came from the Rødhals midden (fish, mammal bones and mollusc shells), from modern samples, and from 65 other Danish Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age) people. Data for eight of them appear here for the first time.

Shallow eelgrass beds, where eel, flatfish and shellfish live, gave him most of his protein all his life. Around eight or nine he ate a bit more from land, and from age 11 he ate more deep-water fish such as haddock and mature cod. In adolescence and adulthood the land share climbed sharply.

As an adult, and for years, he ate in a way not recorded for any other Mesolithic person so far. His enamel gave a strontium value of 0.7096, a chemical signal tied to the ground where a person grew up, which fits a childhood over a large part of the hunter-gatherer area.

Five radiocarbon dates exist for the skeleton, and all are too old because of the fish he ate. Meadows ran them through a Bayesian model that corrects for diet. It puts his death between 3930 and 3710 BC, most likely between 3890 and 3770 BC.

Directly dated bones of domestic cattle and sheep place the arrival of herding in Denmark at about 3950 to 3850 BC. That overlaps his lifetime.

The Sejerby Female’s grave lies about 1.6 miles (2.5 kilometers) away and dates 200 to 300 years after him. She is the earliest directly dated person with Anatolian farmer ancestry on the Sejerø archipelago.

Her diet was almost all land food. By the middle of the fourth millennium BC, dolmens on the archipelago suggest farmers were in control.

His own grave has a kind of stone frame and no ochre staining. That sets it apart from typical Late Mesolithic burials in Denmark. In build it is closer to the graves of early farmers nearby.

Bone from his femurs and ribs shows that he ate more land protein, but not whether it came from hunting and gathering or from herding and crops.

He might have spent his adolescence and adult years foraging on a bigger island. He could then have returned to Sejerø near the end.

The authors give two reasons to doubt that he simply foraged elsewhere. He ate no freshwater fish, unlike inland foragers, and for years no other Mesolithic person on record shared his adult diet.

“If Rødhals Man really was exceptional, an obvious explanation is the arrival of farmers during his lifetime,” they wrote.

A small cavity in his lower right third molar is more typical of Neolithic people, but it is one tooth. A 5,500-year-old settlement shows what Neolithic farmers in Scandinavia ate a few centuries after he died.

A man buried at Dragsholm had the same hunter-gatherer genetic profile and a similar date, but a far more land-based diet. “Lola,” a woman known only from DNA in birch tar chewing gum found at Syltholm on Lolland, is of comparable age.

No later skeleton with mainly hunter-gatherer ancestry has turned up in Denmark. The 2024 genome work found that incoming farmers apparently replaced the locals fairly abruptly.

Other ancient DNA has shown how plague later struck Stone Age farmers in Scandinavia.

Fischer and colleagues had already described the fish and shellfish from the Rødhals midden in a 2021 paper in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports. The team used up the premolar root, so it documented the tooth in 3D and put the model on Zenodo.

The authors write that at his death he “stood at the doorstep to a new age.” Some 5,800 years later, a rainy dig on Sejerø brought him back to light.

The full study appears in the journal Antiquity, where anyone can read it for free.
