If you’re a fan of soft, crunchy, chewable ice, a countertop nugget ice maker can bring that sought-after texture right to your kitchen. Also known as pellet or “Sonic” ice, nugget ice is made of compacted flakes that are soft, airy, and easy to munch on and slow to melt. It’s especially good for soaking up the flavor of whatever you’re drinking, making it a favorite for everything from sodas and cocktails to iced coffee and just plain, old water.

Nugget ice makers have become increasingly popular home appliances, but the machines themselves can be relatively advanced. They require more complex internal components than basic ice makers, and thus tend to cost more—often starting around $400 for a high-quality design. Depending on the appliance, you’ll also find features like large ice baskets, water reservoirs, self-cleaning cycles, and high daily production.

To help you find the right machine for your kitchen and budget, we researched, tested, and reviewed the best nugget ice makers from manufacturers such as GE, Euhomy, and Gevi. One of the key benefits to a good nugget ice maker is that it can churn out upward of 30 or even 40 pounds a day, making it ideal for hosting large, thirsty groups. The best models can have the first cubes ready within about 10 minutes, and will have a full bin within an hour or so.

A countertop machine, of course, can’t store three dozen pounds of ice. Most ice bins hold 1 to 3 pounds, which will replenish as depleted. It’s rare to go through a full bin, so unless you expect to host a particularly large number of people, this may not be the most pressing concern.

The vast majority of home nugget ice makers are equipped with a built-in water reservoir. These are great for at-home use as they don’t require installation and are highly portable, making it easy to pack them away for storage.

The larger the tank, the more ice the machine will produce. However, this also means that it will require more counter space. Models with smaller water reservoirs eat up less room, but require you to refill them frequently.

You can plug some models directly into your home plumbing system, which eliminates the need to fill a tank as it will draw water continuously. These, however, may require expert installation depending on your DIY skills, and will usually end up in a cabinet or closet rather than on your countertop.

The bigger the ice maker, the more countertop space it uses, so consider how much room you have in your kitchen. Our top pick, the GE Opal 2.0, is a great ice maker, but it has a pretty big footprint for a kitchen with limited counter real estate. When measuring dimensions, don’t forget about the inch or two of clearance that you’ll need for the fan, which keeps the motor from overheating.

If you have the space for it, Randhawa recommends placing the model in the pantry. This not only keeps your countertops open, but can muffle the operating noise.

“With most nugget ice machines you should expect some sound when operating on standby, but they can get pretty noisy when producing ice,” says Randhawa. “That might not matter in the din and clatter of cocktail hour, but could be more bothersome when things quiet down.”

It’s difficult to determine how loud a particular model will be without actually hearing it as there is no standardized noise rating. Your best bet is to check reviews for what previous buyers or reviewers have to say.

Nugget ice makers use a large metal cylinder with an auger inside that freezes as the machine runs. Water then pours into the cylinder and starts to freeze along the walls, producing a thin sheet of ice that the auger scrapes and pushes through the machine as ice flakes.

The machine then squeezes the flakes to push out excess water and compacts them into small, airy pieces so that what you end up with is a soft, porous nugget of ice that (probably) won’t crack your teeth when you chew it. They’re ideal for beverages because they take on the flavor of the drink, and there’s really nothing quite like that taste and texture.

Unfortunately, nugget ice makers aren’t ready to use right out of the box. Once you’ve gotten your unit, it’s best to let it sit upright on a level surface for at least two hours before plugging it in. This helps the refrigerant settle. The time may vary depending on the model you have, so it’s best to refer to the manual for the specific time.

You should also run the self-cleaning or sanitizing cycle before getting started. A combination of equal parts water and vinegar can get the job done. By doing this, you’re clearing the machine of any lingering residue or grime that could affect the clarity and taste of the ice. After completing a cycle with the solution, you can run another one with just water for a full wash-down.

Nugget ice makers are not a set-it-and-forget-it machine—they require regular cleaning and additional upkeep. One of the main concerns with these appliances is calcium buildup, or limescale, which accumulates if you have hard tap water.

To prevent this, it’s a good rule of thumb to occasionally descale your ice maker. You can make a 1:1 solution of distilled white vinegar and water, put it in the reservoir, and run it through the machine for a few cycles. Another tip is to use filtered water.

Self-cleaning modes should be run at least once a month, but if you’re a frequent party host, every other week couldn’t hurt. If you’re using a nugget ice maker that hooks up to a water line, you would benefit from an inline filter. Vacuuming the fan and blowing out the condenser also prevents clogs from dust and debris that may cause overheating.

Nugget ice makers make anywhere from 24 to 44 pounds of ice in a 24-hour period. They start up relatively quickly (as little as 10 minutes) but may take up to an hour to fully fill the ice bin.

Models with large capacity bins are best for big groups, while smaller ones are good for home bars and entertaining a couple of friends. Some options have ice bins that are insulated to keep ice from melting, while others just have an acrylic bucket in which ice melts faster.

For those that regularly host large gatherings or just don’t want to run out of nugget ice, we suggest running your nugget ice maker the day before and storing nugget ice in the freezer. Alternatively, you could stockpile extra nugget ice just in case you’re burning through more than expected the day of your event.

Most nugget ice machines are countertop units, so special installation isn’t necessary. We’ve specifically recommended models that don’t require you to install them, as the vast majority of owners will want something user-friendly and easy to store when not in use. Typically speaking, ice makers that require installation are heavy-duty, professional-grade units for serving large groups of people.

According to Randhawa, “Most nugget ice machines just use a standard outlet and require space around it to vent per the instructions.” Without proper ventilation space, your unit could overheat and break down.

“[Nugget ice makers] run compressors constantly and consume more power than people think it should for a countertop appliance,” says Zhao. “If energy consumption is a concern to you, look for those with an Energy Star rating, and factor in that it will likely be running a good portion of the day.”

Newer models tend to need about 0.05 to 1 kilowatt-hour per day, depending on your ice use. The more ice you make, the more power you’ll need. That’s roughly the equivalent of an energy-efficient fridge, which increases electric bills by about $10 to $15 a month.

If you always want ice at the ready, then you’ll have to run your nugget ice maker at all times, but it’ll likely shut off once the bin is full. “This is a normal operating procedure,” says Zhao. “Turning it off and on [manually] every day reduces its life and means you are waiting 15 to 20 minutes for your first batch every day.”

However, these machines don’t technically need to run year-round if you’re worried about possibly running up your electric bill. “You can turn them off and put them away when you don’t plan on using them,” says Randhawa. “We put ours away during vacations and over the winter, after the temperature drops, which is generally when we don’t want ice in our drinks.”
