A few years ago, on a damp evening in late March in London, I learned the hard way that countries don’t all change their clocks for daylight saving time simultaneously. After ordering a glass of white wine at a cozy bar in Bermondsey, I mindlessly scrolled on my phone while the bartenders chatted about the clocks jumping forward that coming Sunday. I nearly spit out my Chablis.

Back home in New York City, I had already skipped ahead one hour on the second Sunday in March. As I frantically Googled, I learned that in the United Kingdom, the clocks go forward one hour on the last Sunday in March (and back one hour on the last Sunday in October).

Don’t be like me and lose two hours of sleep in one month. When you travel, remember the following to avoid daylight saving time-related snafus and other time zone mistakes — and if you're in the U.S., most states fell back one hour on Sunday, Nov. 1.

The U.K. isn’t the only country that changes its clocks on a different weekend than we do in the U.S. For example, most European countries follow the same calendar as the U.K., moving clocks forward one hour on the last Sunday in March and back one hour on the last Sunday in October.

Generally, when traveling in the spring and fall, it’s always best to check when the country you’re going to falls forward or backward — and also whether that country observes daylight saving time at all. In fact, according to the Pew Research Center, about 66% of the world doesn’t.

Though daylight saving time is commonly observed in North America and Europe, many non-European Union countries in Europe — including Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Georgia, Iceland, Russia and Turkey — stay on standard time year-round.

In 2023, Egypt became the only country in Africa to change its clocks when it reintroduced daylight saving time for the first time in seven years. Now, each year, its clocks move forward one hour on the last Friday in April and backward one hour on the last Thursday in October.

It’s also worth double-checking these things even if you're traveling within the U.S. In 48 states, daylight saving time runs from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November. Arizona and Hawaii are the outliers that stay on standard time year-round. While it’s less of an issue on an island like Hawaii, Arizona shares enough borders with other states that do observe daylight saving time that it can be problematic.

“Always double-check timing when flying in/out and on road trips through the state,” Becky Blaine, TPG’s newsletter managing editor and former Arizona resident, said. Otherwise, you can risk missing a flight or a reservation.

To keep it simple, remember that Arizona is in the Pacific Daylight Time zone from mid-March to early November, and the rest of the year it’s in the same time zone as other states in Mountain Standard Time.

It’s a similar situation in Canada. Generally, Canada follows the same daylight saving schedule as the U.S., but most of Saskatchewan, the Yukon and some parts of Quebec and British Columbia stay on standard time year-round.

In Australia, daylight saving time starts the first Sunday in October and ends the first Sunday in April in the Australian Capital Territory, New South Wales, South Australia, Tasmania and Victoria; meanwhile, the Northern Territory, Queensland and Western Australia don’t observe it.

Outside of daylight saving time changes, there are some general time zone issues to be aware of while traveling.

In countries as large as the U.S., Canada and Australia, it’s easy to see why there are so many time zones. But Eric Rosen, TPG’s director of travel content, also pointed out that the airport in the Maldives is in a different time zone than some resorts in the tiny island nation.

Technically, the Maldives has only one official time zone: Maldives Time, which is five hours ahead of Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). However, some of the archipelago's private island resorts add an hour to Maldives Time to maximize their guests' evening light.

For example, The St. Regis Maldives Vommuli Resort sets its clock one hour ahead of the official clock to what it calls "Island Time," but also provides guests with butlers who coordinate transfers to and from the airport so nobody accidentally misses a flight.

Before you go to the Maldives, be sure to check if your resort also does this.

Due to its geographic location, the Canadian island of Newfoundland is 90 minutes ahead of Eastern Time instead of just 60 minutes ahead, as in the rest of Atlantic Canada. On a July trip, Nick Ewen, TPG’s editor-in-chief, found this challenging for calendar purposes.

While traveling in Newfoundland, he received an Outlook invitation to a 7:30 p.m. dinner meeting the following week and accepted it without much thought. However, the actual dinner back home in the U.S. started at 6 p.m. — a detail he completely missed until the reminder popped up 15 minutes before. At that point, he realized he would be late.

Other places offset from UTC by 30 minutes include India, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Iran and some states in Australia. Want to be confused even more? Nepal's time zone is offset by 45 minutes due to its geography.

Even when not dealing with half-hour time zones, it can be easy to make similar mistakes when adding events back home to your digital calendar while traveling in a different time zone.

"Last year, I brought my middle son to a birthday party. We were running a few minutes late, but when we arrived, it seemed like the party was almost over," Tarah Chieffi, a senior writer at TPG, said. "While the kids were eating cake, I double-checked my calendar to make sure we had gotten the time right, and it seemed like we had. But then I went to check the invitation, and we had, in fact, arrived one hour late."

Her mistake? When she entered the party into her calendar while traveling, her calendar app defaulted to the time zone she was in and showed her the incorrect time back home.

"Now, I always toggle the time zone to my home time zone if I enter appointments into my calendar while I'm traveling," she said.

"Outlook lets you have up to three time zones showing on the calendar," Madison Blancaflor, a managing editor at TPG, said.

Go to "Settings," then "Calendar," then "Time zones." Then, check the box for "Show second time zone in Day and Week view" underneath your default time zone. If you need a third one, you can also check that box.

It's also important to remember that it's possible to quickly encounter multiple time zones on cruises — especially on transoceanic cruises, where you'll change time zones daily or almost daily. And if you're on a cruise traversing the Pacific Ocean, you could lose an entire day if you cross the international date line, like Clint Henderson, TPG's principal spokesperson, did when he sailed from Seward, Alaska, to Tokyo last year.

Even on shorter cruises, check if you'll have to change time zones. When I was on a five-day Alaska cruise earlier this summer, I changed time zones twice as we moved in and out of Alaska Daylight Time on a round-trip sailing from Vancouver (which was one hour ahead, on Pacific Daylight Time).

Cabin stewards usually leave notes on your bed at turndown to remind you of upcoming time changes. Clint said he had to set his clocks back between one and two hours each night on his cruise. But you can't always rely on your cellphone to display the correct time when it’s in airplane mode and using satellite-based internet. Bring an analog watch or an alarm clock to ground you if you're unsure whether your device will update properly.

However, some cruise ships may ignore time changes at select ports. "I've had that happen on Royal Caribbean ships in the Caribbean, for example, to avoid the ship time changing three days in a row," said Christine Gallipeau, a senior editor on TPG's points and loyalty team. "So if you're doing shore excursions, make sure you know what time the ship is following, as it may not necessarily match the local time."

When traveling, don’t assume every country or state follows the same daylight-saving-time rules and schedules as where you live, and be mindful of time zone anomalies. Losing or gaining an unexpected hour can lead to lost sleep, arriving late to an event and, worst of all, missed flights.
