Sewage pollution may take a hidden ocean route into coastal air

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Tijuana River sewage chemicals survive a 22-mile trip north underwater and then escape into San Diego's air, far from the river mouth.

When raw sewage pours into the ocean, closing the beach can help protect swimmers.

Chemicals from the Tijuana River sewage that reaches the Pacific at Imperial Beach can also enter the air.

The findings suggest that chemicals from the sewage travel north in ocean currents for about 22 miles (35 kilometers) before escaping into the air near La Jolla.

Wind alone could not explain their presence that far away.

Karolina Cysneiros de Carvalho, an atmospheric scientist, led the study at the University of California, Riverside and the National Center for Atmospheric Research in Colorado.

Her co-authors were UC Riverside’s William Porter and Kelley Barsanti and Kimberly Prather of UC San Diego.

The team sampled the air at four coastal sites in August and September 2024, when the river was carrying far more wastewater than it typically does during a dry summer.

“It was already known that compounds from this waste could enter the air, but we didn’t realize until now how far it could travel underwater before emerging,” Cysneiros de Carvalho said.

The Tijuana River drains land on both sides of the border, three quarters of it in Mexico. It carries untreated sewage, industrial waste and street runoff into the sea, a plume visible from orbit.

In most summers, its flow drops to almost nothing. In the summer of 2024, between 42 million and 55 million gallons (160 million to 207 million liters) a day kept coming, with no rain to explain it.

The team tracked one chemical first: dimethyl trisulfide, a sulfur compound given off by rotting organic matter and sewage.

They measured it at a hotspot on the river about 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) inland, at the river’s estuary and at Imperial Beach.

The last site was the Scripps Institution of Oceanography pier in La Jolla, far up the coast.

The hotspot accounted for 83% of the combined signal across the four sites, and La Jolla for 2%. Small as that was, the compound was there, 22 miles from the river.

Dimethyl trisulfide breaks down fast in air.

By the team’s calculations, it falls to about a third of its starting level within 3.6 miles (5.8 kilometers), even when the night air is least reactive.

Carried by wind alone, at most 10% of the compound would survive the trip to La Jolla, and under some conditions almost none would remain.

“We found pollutants far from the source that couldn’t be explained by just the wind,” said William Porter, a UC Riverside atmospheric scientist and co-author.

On September 10, 2024, a pump station began diverting wastewater south, away from the river.

Levels of the compound at the La Jolla pier then fell by 86%.

The mid-morning signal was gone within 24 hours, but an early-morning peak lasted another two days before it dropped below detection.

The two-day tail mattered. The air mixed more after the diversion, which by itself would have diluted the compound about threefold.

It fell about twelvefold instead, so something besides mixing was removing it, and something had been holding a reserve of it long enough to leak out over two more days.

The evidence points to the ocean as that reserve. In water, the compound breaks down slowly.

It also escapes into the air slowly, largely because of resistance to its movement through the water toward the surface.

In the team’s models, 14% to 31% of the compound survives the 22-mile trip north in the top 6.6 feet (2 meters) of the sea. At a depth of 33 feet (10 meters), 61% to 76% survives.

Some compounds also stick loosely to organic particles in the water, which shelters them a little longer before they let go.

The water itself showed the route. Moorings off Del Mar, 5.6 miles (9 kilometers) past La Jolla, recorded a layer of unusually fresh water below the surface in August and September 2024.

It matched water measured off the river mouth to the south.

In earlier summers, when the river’s flow was negligible, the two sites looked distinct.

Ocean models show the coastal current turned northward in August 2024, right when the freshening appeared.

Dimethyl trisulfide wasn’t alone, either. Among hundreds of compounds the team measured, a group rose and fell with the river’s flow and with the tracer.

Their chemistry fell in a middle range: soluble enough to stay in seawater for the trip, volatile enough to leave it afterward.

The study did not measure whether the Tijuana River sewage chemicals reaching La Jolla’s air pose a health risk, and it covered one unusual season rather than a normal one.

The record at the pier runs less than a week past the diversion, so how the pattern changes over months is open.

Winter rains, shifting currents and changing river flows could all alter the pattern.

Porter said the result shows water and air pollution are not separate problems.

“You can’t just shut down the beach and call it good,” he said. “We need to clean up the water not just for swimming but for breathing.”

The next test is measuring the same chemicals in the water and the air at once, at several depths, through a full year.

That would show how often the ocean carries sewage chemicals this far, and how much escapes into the air.

The full study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.
