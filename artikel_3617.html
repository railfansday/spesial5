The Curious Case of Olivia Reingold’s AI Photo at a Democratic Victory Party: Valentino, Chanel, and a Free Press Mystery

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

On Monday night, the eve of the 2026 midterm elections, the fashion crowd celebrated at the 2026 CFDA Fashion Awards, colloquially referred to as the Oscars of American fashion. Tory Burch was honored with the Positive Change Award, which was presented to her by Anna Wintour, the global editorial director of Vogue and chief content officer of Condé Nast. In her remarks, Wintour made the evening’s only direct reference to the midterms, reminiscing about attending a fundraising event for James Talarico, who successfully flipped Texas in the race for the Senate last night, alongside Burch. After applause from the audience, Wintour, a prolific Democratic fundraiser herself, looked up and addressed the room: “Let’s hope all of Texas feels the way you do,” she said.

The American fashion crowd has largely distanced itself from politics since the 2024 election. During their time in the White House as first ladies, Michelle Obama and Jill Biden were staunch supporters of the American fashion industry, often wearing American designers for public appearances. Melania Trump, in contrast, has more often preferred European labels. It’s an MO that trickles down into the political parties they represent.

There were many headlines that came out of last night’s midterms: Talarico flipped Texas, Annie Andrews flipped South Carolina, New Hampshire’s Chris Pappas became the first out gay man to be elected to the Senate, and Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia and Deb Haaland of New Mexico became the first Black and Native American women, respectively, to be elected governor in the history of the US.

And yet it wasn’t just good news (and many great memes) that circulated in my group chats last night; there were also some fashion choices worth noting. First, the most pressing: Where were Steve Kornacki’s khakis? And what was the reasoning behind Haley Stevens’s ludicrously capacious hat? Some questions in life simply go unanswered.

Kornacki, a national political correspondent for NBC News, does not make his political affiliations public. But men in his age range (he is 47) and in the generations that follow—millennials and Gen Z—have been contending with what formalwear means and signals today.

Democratic men have been hard at work on their public image since the 2024 election, largely distancing themselves from the MAGA look and its too-large suits and extra-wide ties. In separating themselves from the manosphere, men like Abdul El-Sayed, who won the Senate race in Michigan, and Jon Ossoff, who was reelected to the Senate in Georgia, have been dressing more like the less buttoned up, progressive men they represent and less like the old establishment politicians on both the right and the left.

On the campaign trail, El-Sayed often sported tees and sweaters rather than button-downs and ties, frequently wearing taut sweaters and henleys under his suit jacket, while Ossoff largely opted for rolled cuffs and no jacket. As the two delivered victory speeches last night, they each wore a tailored suit—a little too tight in the arms; these are men who seem to eat their fair share of protein—and ditched their tie.

It’s a familiar image of a more laid-back look, but one in stark contrast to that of the faces of the right: Donald Trump, JD Vance, et al. Ossoff and El-Sayed, similarly to Kamala Harris during her presidential bid, also leaned into what was happening on the internet. These days, netizens are happy to objectify men and christen a new internet boyfriend every week. Ossoff’s and El-Sayed’s campaigns seemed to understand the power of thirst—that their suits were too tight was most likely not completely intentional, but it did help underscore a simple fact: that they are fit, and that their virility can be both attractive and nonthreatening all at once.

Zohran Mamdani speaking at an event on election night. Mamdani also ditched his tie on Tuesday, in line with some of his fellow Democrats.

It is worth noting, however, that there are some men further to the left, both in politics and around it, who have reembraced the suit: Zohran Mamdani and Hasan Piker. On one hand, this has served a similar purpose as Ossoff’s and El-Sayed’s choices: It has distanced them from looksmaxxers and other conservative-leaning young men. On the other hand, a suit for Mamdani and Piker signifies what suits have always represented: that these are grown men doing important things.

The men are not the full story, however. Bottoms and Haaland came as they are, nodding to the communities they represent. The former wore a bright fuchsia suit and matching blouse, a callback to the lively and monochromatic dressing that is a deeply embedded and celebrated tradition in Black church culture. Haaland’s jewelry—firefly earrings and a beaded necklace—nodded at the style of accessories that is representative of Native style and fashion. If there is something that fashion clearly signifies, it’s community and belonging.

It is a running punch line that the left is more closely connected to fashion—it’s the wine-and-cheese crowd, the elites. (Hillary Clinton, during her bid for the presidency in 2016, was dragged online and by the press for wearing an Armani jacket that reportedly retailed for some $12,000 while delivering a speech that touched on income inequality.) While it’s true that fashion designers are often more enthusiastic about dressing progressives than they are about dressing conservatives, in the era of Trump and the MAGA look, this has become a half-baked story.

Olivia Reingold, a staff writer at The Free Press, made a post on X last night while reporting from an event for the Michigan Democratic Party. “So far at Abdul El-Sayed’s election night event I’ve spotted, Valentino heels, a Chanel bag, a Moncler jacket, Rolex watches, and a YSL bag among other designer items,” Reingold wrote, captioning an image of a woman holding a gold quilted Chanel handbag and a pair of Valentino Rockstud leather pumps.

When one takes a closer look, however, the image has several discrepancies. The style and make of the Chanel Flap bag is inconsistent with others the brand has sold, and the Valentino shoes in question appear askew in proportion; their straps have several punched holes that the authentic Valentino style does not. This could mean that the products in the image are not authentic, but there is another suggestive tell: The bottom strap on the left shoe appears to cut through its connecting strap rather than around, a classic mistake made when artificial intelligence is used to create or alter an image. A run of the image through several AI detectors, including SynthID, a digital watermarking approach created by Google DeepMind that detects media generated by OpenAI, Google, Nvidia, and Kakao, suggests the use of AI. SynthID detected that “this media was made or edited with OpenAI.” (Vanity Fair has reached out to Chanel and Valentino to advise on the authenticity of the products.)

Reingold did not reply to a request for comment, but on Thursday afternoon followed up with a new post on X, admitting to using AI “to brighten and sharpen an image of a woman in Valentino shoes and a Chanel bag.”

It’s become too easy to try to make a point about the left’s fashion choices: that Rama Duwaji, wife of Mamdani, should not wear pricey shoes, even if borrowed, because of her political beliefs; or that Democratic women should not carry Valentino or Chanel and vote for a candidate seeking to tax the wealthy. Therein lies the crux of the argument: Does this mean that luxury shoppers should align themselves with MAGA because it’s a party associated with billionaires like Elon Musk and Jeff Bezos and logo-clad celebs like Nicki Minaj, who wore Chanel to the polls in New Hampshire? The answer is simple: no.

Our fashion choices say much about our values, identity, and aspirations. And while politicians have oftentimes utilized fashion to align themselves with specific groups or appear friendly to voters, they, in broad strokes, do not define our political affiliations. A Chanel bag does not make a Democrat, just as a red tie does not make a Republican.
