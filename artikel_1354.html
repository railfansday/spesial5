Rethinking corporate climate targets for real-world transitions

Corporate greenhouse gas reduction targets are central to climate action, but they often fall short of driving meaningful business transformation. Despite progress in standardization, current target-setting approaches remain weakened by methodological problems, including inaccurate emissions accounting, excluded emissions sources and limited comparability across companies. More importantly, many corporate targets are disconnected from the real-world sectoral transitions required to meet the Paris Agreement’s temperature goal. Drawing on evidence from four editions of the Corporate Climate Responsibility Monitor and recent literature, we argue that target-setting frameworks should place greater emphasis on systemic changes in business models, technologies and value chains. We propose transition targets as a practical complement to traditional greenhouse gas reduction targets. These targets would incentivize, guide and measure progress towards sector-specific milestones, such as electrification, low-carbon material uptake and the phase-out of emissions-intensive technologies. Embedding transition targets in accountability standards, and eventually in regulation, could help close the gap between corporate climate commitments and real-world action. It would also align corporate target-setting with the physical transition benchmarks introduced by the Paris Agreement’s Global Stocktake. The scientific community has a key role in developing robust, actionable frameworks for corporate decarbonization.

Ten years after the Paris Agreement’s adoption in 2015, greenhouse gas (GHG) reduction target setting has become a cornerstone of corporate climate action. This development has been supported by scientific research that develops and reviews methods for corporate climate target setting aligned with the Paris Agreement’s temperature limit (for example, refs. 1,2,3). Accountability initiatives established by researchers as well as international and non-governmental organizations have operationalized GHG target-setting practices and related validation mechanisms4,5. For example, the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) has published GHG target-setting standards for more than 10 sectors and validated GHG targets for more than 10,000 companies as of May 20266. This reflects the growing recognition that companies have a central role in delivering climate mitigation alongside governments and other public actors. Achieving mitigation consistent with the Paris Agreement’s temperature limit depends on transitions in companies’ production processes, product offerings and procurement practices across all sectors of the economy. These transitions may be undertaken voluntarily, but they are ultimately shaped and accelerated by standards and government policies.

The proliferation of corporate GHG targets has prompted growing scrutiny by researchers and the wider public. Empirical evidence, including our annual Corporate Climate Responsibility Monitor7,8,9,10, shows that GHG targets often fail to drive and reflect meaningful transformation of corporate business activities. We argue that this reflects a twofold limitation of existing GHG target-setting frameworks (Fig. 1). First, methodological shortcomings in existing standards and corporate practice make GHG targets ambiguous in terms of transparency and interpretation. Such limitations include uncertainties in scope coverage, base-year selection and the use of market-based instruments. Second, GHG targets remain structurally disconnected from real-world sectoral transitions. By focusing on aggregate emissions outcomes rather than activity-level changes, GHG targets reveal little about how companies plan to transform their production systems, value chains and products in line with Paris Agreement-compatible sectoral transition and technology pathways.

Against this backdrop, we propose transition targets as a complementary approach to GHG target setting. Transition targets are metrics that guide and measure a company’s progress towards key sectoral transitions. Although recent literature discusses several of the shortcomings of GHG target setting11,12,13,14,15, actionable proposals for rethinking and advancing science-informed corporate target-setting frameworks remain limited. By introducing transition targets, we seek to reorient corporate target setting from tracking emissions outcomes towards guiding real-world transformation of business activities. Early applications of transition targets by companies and accountability initiatives provide practical lessons for designing and operationalizing such frameworks.

Experience with corporate GHG targets shows that current corporate target-setting practice and the standards set by accountability initiatives face several methodological shortcomings. These limitations can make many GHG targets difficult to interpret, apply and compare in practice. As a result, they obscure the level of emission reduction that companies are committed to. For example, a systematic assessment of the emission reduction strategies of 55 major global companies found that the intended emission outcomes of 2030 GHG targets across value chains could not be determined for more than half of them10.

We identify four main methodological shortcomings of current GHG target-setting practices (Supplementary Tables 1–5). These include inaccuracies in emission accounting, incomparable base-year selections, exclusions of emission sources, and the undefined use of carbon credits and other market-based instruments outside the value chain to claim reductions in companies’ own emissions. The prevalence of these shortcomings makes corporate GHG reduction targets vulnerable to misrepresentation by consumers, researchers, investors, courts and policymakers. They can expose companies to potential legal and reputational risks16, even when companies fully comply with prevailing accountability standards.

Researchers, international organizations and accountability initiatives have increasingly recognized these shortcomings. These insights have informed the revision of major corporate climate standards and the development of new international standards since 2025. The SBTi discussed several shortcomings in current target setting in discussion papers, which served as input to the revision of its cross-sectoral Corporate Net Zero Standard17,18,19. Similarly, the GHG Protocol reviewed its emission accounting standards, leading to a revision process20. In addition, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), comprising representatives from the national standards organizations of member countries, published Net Zero Guidelines and initiated an official standard-setting process for Net Zero Aligned Organizations21,22. These standard-setting and revision processes also aim to incorporate differentiated approaches that take fairness and geographical context considerations into account.

Further standardization of GHG target setting and advances in emission accounting may improve the transparency of GHG targets. For example, technological advances in data collection and processing, improved emission accounting approaches23, and revised accounting boundaries can enhance the accuracy of GHG inventories in the future. Although improved GHG inventories remain highly valuable for understanding companies’ emission profiles, they are not well suited to serve as the sole basis for corporate target setting across complex value chains24,25. This is owing to inherent challenges in defining and operationalizing inventory boundaries, as well as the continued reliance on incomplete bottom-up data and assumptions to estimate emissions from value-chain activities. Improved inventories may incrementally address these limitations but are unlikely to overcome them owing to the complexity of value chains. As a result, these limitations continue to create ambiguity in the meaning of GHG targets and limit the ability to assess progress against them.

Beyond methodological limitations, GHG targets are structurally disconnected from the real-world sectoral transitions to meet the Paris Agreement’s temperature limit. By structural disconnect, we mean that GHG targets are designed to measure emissions outcomes, but do not specify or require the changes in business activities needed to achieve those outcomes. This is because emission estimates are imperfect proxies for underlying business activities and may not reliably capture the physical transformations required for decarbonization. Reduction targets based on GHG inventories provide only indirect links to specific activity-level transitions, such as sourcing low-carbon materials or offering low-emission products. Consequently, they are in themselves limited in their ability to track, incentivize or guide corporate action towards low-carbon sectoral transitions. GHG targets also do not directly account for companies’ capacity to innovate and contribute to these transitions13. Companies can therefore set GHG targets, for example, for 2030, without clearly showing whether these targets are informed and substantiated by the underlying transformation of their business activities.

Our analyses of 55 major global companies across sectors highlight examples of these issues10. Even for companies with 2030 GHG targets free of obvious methodological shortcomings, such as the fashion companies adidas and H&M Group, it remains unclear what these targets mean in terms of real-world transitions for their business activities. In the fashion sector, the key transitions include electrifying manufacturing, powering processes with renewable electricity and shifting to low-emission fibres through recycling (Fig. 2). H&M Group commits to 100% renewable electricity across its supply chain26, but this is limited in significance without a commitment to electrify production processes. adidas targets a 42% GHG reduction by 2030 below 2022 levels. Aside from sourcing 10% recycled polyester, however, no other transition commitments such as electrifying manufacturing are evident27.

Recent literature highlights related limitations of standalone GHG targets11,12,13,14,15. Robiou Du Pont et al.13 argue that standalone GHG targets favour established companies and overlook companies’ potential for innovation that could drive Paris Agreement-aligned sectoral transitions. The authors propose to assess companies both “contextually and dynamically” by going beyond GHG metrics and considering other metrics that reflect underlying business activities and transition needs. Reisinger et al.12 call for moving away from rigid benchmarks for science-based net-zero targets. They advocate a flexible menu of target-setting options that accounts for company-specific circumstances and capabilities. In response to these more fundamental critiques of GHG reduction targets, Rekker et al.14 acknowledge existing problems while emphasizing refinements to existing frameworks that balance complexity and practicality. Together, these contributions provide valuable conceptual insights but offer limited guidance on how to translate them into actionable improvements to corporate target-setting practices.

Against this backdrop, we propose transition targets as metrics that guide and measure a company’s progress towards key sectoral transitions through changes in business activities. Transition targets aim to address major current and future emission sources in a company’s GHG footprint along the value chain. For many real-economy companies, this includes emissions from the upstream and downstream value chain beyond their own operational emissions, for example, from purchasing emission-intensive materials from suppliers or selling products that emit GHG emissions during the use phase. These major emission sources translate into key transitions that companies need to undertake to decarbonize their business activities and shift away from fossil fuels. Targets addressing these specific transitions—hence the term transition targets—should be operationalized through suitable metrics for action and accountability.

The development of transition targets begins with identifying the major emission sources across a company’s value chain and the corresponding sectoral transitions needed for decarbonization (Fig. 2). For instance, this could include all emission sources above defined thresholds, such as 5% of total value chain emissions18. These major emission sources should be identified on the basis of annual emission inventories and complementary value-chain analysis that considers the company’s future emissions, technology and product portfolio trends. Suitable metrics are then selected for each transition based on criteria such as specificity, practicality, susceptibility to misrepresentation and regulatory alignment28. These metrics need to be paired with benchmarks grounded in sector-specific decarbonization pathways under the Paris Agreement, enabling both target setting and implementation tracking.

Transition targets represent a transparent and implementation-oriented target-setting approach. For example, such metrics can specify the targeted share of electric vehicles in total sales for automakers, renewable electricity consumption across supply chains, or the share of low-carbon material uptake in manufacturing by a specific date. For service providers, these metrics can define non-physical operational benchmarks such as phasing out services to the fossil-fuel industry. Transition targets can also reflect the innovative capacity of solution-providing companies that may experience short-term emissions growth, for example, growing providers of solar panels or wind turbines. Capturing the contribution of solution providers remains inherently difficult through standalone GHG targets based on GHG inventories that do not provide a reliable indication to what degree Paris Agreement-aligned transitions are being implemented13.

The proposed conceptual shift for corporate target setting parallels the shift reflected in the outcome of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) Global Stocktake of 2023 for countries29. Parties to the UNFCCC introduced physical transition benchmarks to guide implementation and collective progress beyond GHG targets. These include the tripling of renewable energy capacity by 2030 compared with 2023 levels, the expedited deployment of zero- and low-emission vehicles, and an accelerated transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems. Some researchers even advocate for making such clean-energy targets the central target-setting metric for governments going forward30. In a similar direction, transition targets for companies can more clearly anchor the phase-out of fossil fuels and other physical transitions needed for a transition towards net zero.

Across sectors, we identify early applications of transition targets by companies, even if these are not yet applied systematically across all key transitions for a given company. These early adopters of transition targets have often been mobilized and supported by accountability initiatives (Table 1). The First Movers Coalition convened by the World Economic Forum, for example, has piloted methodologies for transition targets such as low-carbon steel and aluminium procurement31,32. In practice, more than 22 companies have signed the commitment to procure 10% low-carbon aluminium by 2030 since 202233,34. This includes automobile manufacturers such as Ford, General Motors, Volvo Cars and Volvo Group, for which the procurement of low-carbon aluminium represents one of the key transitions to decarbonize their upstream value chain. In a similar direction, the SBTi introduced a transition target metric for phasing out the sales of internal combustion engines for automakers alongside GHG reduction targets in 202435. SBTi’s draft Automotive Net-Zero Standard of February 2026 introduces further guidance to anchor a similar transition target metric and related benchmarks to guide the sector’s transformation36. These early efforts serve as an important starting point for method development to systematically introduce transition targets across sectors.

Building on early piloting, transition targets can serve two central, interrelated purposes if introduced alongside traditional GHG reduction targets. First, they provide sector-tailored frameworks to guide and measure the transformation of business activities beyond more generic and indirect GHG target-setting framework. This can further support acknowledging ‘fundamental differences between industries’ in target-setting frameworks14. Second, they can substantiate GHG reduction targets and help external users interpret their meaning and the company’s intention to implement a Paris Agreement-aligned transition. For example, an automobile manufacturer’s GHG reduction target will be easier to interpret by external audiences if it is accompanied by targeted transitions for selling zero-emission vehicles and the procurement of low-carbon aluminium and steel. Accountability initiatives and other standard setters can institutionalize an actionable and science-informed process combining both types of target (Fig. 3). Some initiatives, such as the SBTi, have already started to do so in recent draft standards for the automotive and power sectors released for public consultation in 2025 and 202636,37.

Although the development of transition targets comes with its own methodological challenges, we argue that these are more tractable in practice than those for GHG reduction targets. Transition targets reduce reliance on emission accounting and base-year choices, are less vulnerable to opaque accounting practices such as the use of carbon credits and have a lower susceptibility to scope exclusions. Nevertheless, we identify four considerations for the scientific community, accountability initiatives and companies when operationalizing transition targets.

First, transition targets do not aim to enable universal cross-sector comparison through a single metric. Instead, these targets enable meaningful comparison of corporate climate action within sectors, where firms face comparable technological pathways and decarbonization constraints in implementing transitions. At the same time, they enable cross-sector comparisons where sectors share specific physical or non-physical transitions across value chains. For example, the procurement of low-carbon steel is equally relevant for the automobile manufacturing and construction sectors. Transition targets also allow to better conceptualize forms of shared or overlapping responsibility across value chains. Multiple companies may legitimately contribute to the same sectoral transition, for example, through near-zero steel procurement and production. Transition targets can help make such relationships more transparent by focusing on concrete transition activities and procurement arrangements rather than solely on aggregated emissions outcomes. Introduced alongside GHG reduction targets, transition targets therefore expand, rather than reduce, the scope for comparability in practice. The multi-metric, transition-relevant approach provides an informative basis for assessing corporate intent to transform business activities. On the basis of our experience assessing corporate climate strategies, the pursuit of single-metric comparisons, such as single 2030 GHG targets, is often conceptually ill-suited or even misleading in practice given the different pathways through which companies transition their business activities.

Second, the identification and operationalization of transition targets face their own methodological and practical challenges28. Identifying major emissions sources in companies’ business activities faces challenges for certain sectors. This is, among other factors, owing to heterogeneity across companies, poor granularity of GHG emissions data, or a lack of scientific consensus on pathways towards low-emission raw materials, circularity and business-model changes. Similarly, the identification of appropriate transition target metrics might face challenges. These challenges occur if transition targets span across business activities along the entire value chain, if metrics require a high level of standardization for potentially ambiguous terminology, or if various potential metrics with different meanings exist. The challenges might be particularly pronounced for heterogeneous sectors such as the consumer goods industry with various decarbonization and technology routes or conglomerates of different, unrelated business units. Our own research for sectors with homogeneity of activities, a high share of clustered emission sources and a reasonable degree of scientific consensus on transition needs shows the feasibility of developing transition targets for several sectors10,28.

Third, the analytical complexity for the development of Paris Agreement-aligned benchmarks to inform transition target setting differs across sectors. This relates to both the available literature on specific sectoral transitions and wider implications of a potential global overshoot in the decadal average warming above 1.5 °C. On the former, recent literature by the scientific community or international organizations such as the International Energy Agency (IEA) provides analyses on some of the Paris Agreement-aligned sectoral transitions such as in the automobile manufacturer sector (for example, ref. 38), and syntheses by the integrated assessment community are underway39. Such analyses might be largely absent for some transitions in other, non-homogenous sectors, which increases the complexity to develop Paris Agreement-compatible benchmarks. Although some sectors may move slightly faster or slower than others, tight carbon budgets leave limited flexibility, and all sectors ultimately need to reduce emissions to zero. A likely global 1.5 °C overshoot will lead to continuously updated emissions and associated technology pathways to restrict both the magnitude and the duration of any temperature overshoot as emphasized by the Mutirão Decision by Parties at COP3040. Benchmarks for transition targets must adjust to these evolving scientific findings across sectors over time.

Fourth, standards, validation and verification processes for transition targets can support a strengthened governance of the corporate climate accountability system. The current system largely relies on accountability initiatives established by international organizations, non-governmental organizations and researchers that depend on companies’ voluntary participation. This limits consistent monitoring, verification and enforcement of corporate climate action across jurisdictions. For multinational companies and cross-border value chains, transition targets can support coordination by defining common sectoral metrics while leaving room for implementation pathways to differ across policy and geographical contexts. International standard-setting processes, such as those led by ISO, and emerging disclosure and due diligence regulation by governments provide an opportunity to design and anchor more mandatory requirements41. By translating decarbonization pathways into measurable activity-based indicators, transition targets can support standard setters and regulators in defining clear compliance criteria and verification processes, including safeguards against companies selectively choosing favourable target metrics. For example, the SBTi draft Automotive Sector Net-Zero Standard of 2026 introduces mandatory zero-emission vehicle sales targets across geographies as part of the sectoral target-setting framework36. Such transition target metrics can align closely with policy instruments and legally defined milestones. In this way, transition targets can help bridge voluntary corporate commitments and mandatory policy frameworks, strengthening both the credibility and implementation of corporate climate action.

As the climate crisis accelerates, the scientific community has a critical role in shaping the future of corporate target setting. Building on recently proposed research agendas to strengthen Paris Agreement-aligned GHG benchmarking14, we argue that this agenda should expanded to include the systematic development of transition targets alongside emissions-based metrics. Similar to GHG target setting, researchers and accountability initiatives need to provide a robust conceptual and methodological evidence base in a timely and coherent manner. Research priorities include the identification of key sectoral transitions, the development of target metrics, and the definition of science-informed benchmarks for corporate decarbonization in line with the Paris Agreement’s temperature limit. In addition, heterogeneous sectors such as the consumer goods industry with various decarbonization and technology routes will require rigorous and innovative research to inform more customized target-setting approaches. Further research is also needed on whether, and under what conditions, market-based mechanisms can be used with high integrity in corporate target setting.

The collaboration between the scientific community and accountability initiatives can help integrate transition targets alongside GHG targets and anchor corporate target setting in the transformation of business activities. The ongoing revision and standard-setting processes provide a critical opportunity for the future of corporate target setting19,20,22. They should be treated as a starting point rather than an end point to further conceptualize and operationalize corporate target setting anchored in corporate transitions and their implementation. For example, the SBTi will update and further develop various sector-specific standards after completing its cross-sector standard in 2026. These forthcoming processes will benefit from active engagement of researchers to bridge conceptual advances and practical implementation. Such processes can also include guidance on how stakeholders should interpret the interplay between GHG targets and transition targets. This would help distinguish, for example, whether an interim increase in a company’s absolute emissions reflects the implementation of Paris Agreement-compatible sectoral transitions, business expansion in high-emitting activities, or accounting and boundary changes. The engagement of researchers can contribute to a scientifically grounded, transparent and comparable accountability system for the decades to come.

Without a shift towards transition-oriented metrics, corporate accountability risks continuing to focus on target-setting compliance and emission accounting. At the same time, it may remain unable to assess whether companies are transforming the physical systems and value chains that ultimately determine real-world emissions trajectories. Aligning corporate climate targets with sectoral transitions is therefore not merely a technical refinement but a prerequisite for distinguishing credible transformation from accounting compliance in this decisive decade for climate action.
