Scriptable GeoDNS: leaving AWS for Bunny

Tanggal: 2026-11-07

To op­ti­mize the per­for­mance met­rics of this blog, I re­lied on Ama­zon Cloud­Front to serve the as­sets,1 and Route 53 for the geo-aware DNS2 to di­rect read­ers to the clos­est host serv­ing the con­tent. But Route 53 is ex­pen­sive: US$0.50 per zone per month, US$0.70 per mil­lion ge­olo­ca­tion queries, and US$0.75 per healthcheck per month[1], plus US$1 per month for the KMS key[2] needed to sign zones with DNSSEC. When I dis­cov­ered that Bunny DNS is al­most free[3] for up to 500 do­mains and fea­tures script­able an­swers, I made the jump! Plus, it’s a Eu­ro­pean com­pany based in Slovenia.

I had been host­ing me­dia as­sets on Cloud­Front since 2011. ❦

Route 53 had served bernat.ch since De­cem­ber 2021. I didn’t use healthchecks as they were too ex­pen­sive. I didn’t host most of my zones there ei­ther, as queries for haproxy.debian.net alone would have cost me around US$10 per month. ❦

Bunny.net[4] started as BunnyCDN in 2015[5]. They added IPv6 sup­port in 2017[6]. Switch­ing was un­event­ful. I don’t bring a cus­tom do­main: I serve the con­tent from the b-cdn.net do­main. As I de­pend on con­tent ne­go­ti­a­tion to serve AVIF and WebP im­ages[7], I con­fig­ure the CDN to cache each im­age for­mat sep­arately[8].

I dis­able log­ging[9] to avoid col­lect­ing per­sonal in­for­ma­tion. While the bunny.net GDPR page[10] prom­ises they anonymize data, it is un­clear what Cloud­Front keeps in its own logs when log­ging is disabled.

The only down­side of the bunny.net CDN of­fer­ing is the lack of HTTP/3. I asked their sup­port: two min­utes later, they an­swered they are work­ing on it but have no ETA to share.

Bunny.net in­tro­duced their DNS of­fer­ing in 2022[11] and re­cently re­duced its cost to al­most zero[3]. They sup­port ge­o­graph­i­cal rout­ing[12], but a very cool fea­ture is the script­able DNS[13]: it runs a piece of user-sup­plied Java­Script on each query to com­pute the answer.

I run seven small NixOS vir­tual ma­chines to host this blog around the world: Paris, Nurem­berg, Ash­burn, Sin­ga­pore, San Jose, São Paulo, and Syd­ney. Bunny’s script­able DNS lets me re­turn the IP ad­dresses of the two near­est servers. The browser falls back to the sec­ond server if the first one is down.

A server is alive if it an­swers an ICMP ping. The bunny.net doc­u­men­ta­tion[16] gives more de­tails about the checks. ❦

The user queries Bunny DNS through a re­solver. Usu­ally, ei­ther the re­solver is close to the user, or it ex­poses the user’s sub­net through EDNS client subnet[17]. ❦

Don’t use this script as is: RoutingEngine.getClosestServer() only works with IPv4. The fi­nal script[18] works around this lim­i­tation. ❦

The most an­noy­ing draw­back of this method is that a user from Buenos Aires gets both São Paulo (1000 mi, 30 ms RTT) and Ash­burn (5200 mi, 140 ms RTT). Most re­solvers ran­dom­ize the or­der, so the user may pull the web page from Ash­burn. We could re­turn only the near­est server—and swap the scripted record with the built-in ge­o­graphic rout­ing in­stead. But if the server be­comes un­avail­able, the browser has no al­ter­na­tive un­til the DNS cache ex­pires. It leaves a short win­dow of un­avail­abil­ity, even with a short TTL.

The HTTPS DNS record[19], de­fined in RFC 9460[20], solves both the avail­abil­ity and the la­tency is­sues. Mod­ern browsers query this record to get ad­di­tional in­for­ma­tion to reach an HTTP server, in­clud­ing an al­ter­na­tive port and the ap­pli­ca­tion layer protocol—HTTP/2 or HTTP/3. This looks like the ideal so­lu­tion! Un­for­tu­nately, Chrome ig­nores any record whose tar­get is not .[21], hence the last record at pri­or­ity 3. More­over, un­like with a CNAME, Bunny DNS does not re­turn the IP ad­dresses for first.geo and second.geo in the ad­di­tional sec­tion. The re­solver needs to send a first re­quest to get the HTTPS record, and a sec­ond to re­solve first.geo.

HTTPS records are still too new[22] for this use case. Back to the draw­ing board! 📐

As a com­pro­mise, the script re­turns the two near­est servers if they are at a sim­i­lar dis­tance from the user. Oth­er­wise, it re­turns only the near­est one with a lower TTL of 2 minutes.

We can check it with Doggo. From Chicago, we get only the Ash­burn server with a TTL of 2 min­utes, while from Mex­ico, we get both Ash­burn and San Jose.

The com­plete code[18] is a bit more com­plex. It works around RoutingEngine.getClosestServer() sup­port­ing only IPv4, and it han­dles the case where there are not enough alive servers to build an answer.

Bunny.net has a com­pre­hen­sive Ter­raform provider[24]. I lever­age CDK Ter­rain[25] to build the tar­get state in Type­Script and OpenTofu[26] to de­ploy it. Have a look at “Man­ag­ing in­fra­struc­ture with Ter­raform, CDKTF, and NixOS[27]” for more details.6

The ar­ti­cle is a bit out­dated. CDK Ter­rain re­places CDKTF, OpenTofu re­places Ter­raform, and I now use Col­mena[28] in­stead of NixOps. ❦

Route 53 cov­ered one ad­di­tional use case of mine: serv­ing acme.luffy.cx to solve DNS-01 ACME chal­lenges[29]. Un­like Route 53, Bunny DNS can­not re­strict ac­cess to a sin­gle zone with a fine-grained ac­cess pol­icy. As this us­age re­quires nei­ther high avail­abil­ity nor low la­tency, I re­placed Route 53 with Hick­ory[30], a mem­ory-safe DNS server writ­ten in Rust. Un­for­tu­nately, Bunny DNS can­not pub­lish a DS record in a zone.7 I need one to se­cure the acme.luffy.cx zone and cer­tifi­cate de­liv­ery. There­fore, I host the luffy.cx zone on deSEC[37],8 a free DNS host­ing ser­vice run by a non-profit or­ga­ni­za­tion based in Berlin. I con­tacted bunny.net sup­port to ask when they will sup­port pub­lish­ing a DS record and got an an­swer seven min­utes later: they don’t know. 😭

This is a bum­mer, as they sup­port au­to­matic CDS, a DNS record to ad­ver­tise a DS record to the par­ent zone. They han­dle one side of the del­e­ga­tion, not the other.

I also re­ported a bug with the proof of non-ex­is­tence, and they replied a day later with a de­tailed answer. ❦

Why not use deSEC for ACME chal­lenges? You can limit its to­kens to a sin­gle RRset[38]. I could say I pre­fer to self-host, but the truth is I only dis­cov­ered this while writ­ing this article. ❦

Over­all, I am quite happy with the switch to bunny.net. There are still a few rough edges, like the in­abil­ity to pub­lish DS records, the lack of HTTP/3, and the IPv4-only RoutingEngine.getClosestServer() helper. The bill went from US$5 per month to US$1. So far, per­for­mance is on par with AWS[39], but I only fi­nal­ized the cur­rent setup, in­clud­ing the server in Syd­ney, in late Oc­to­ber. I need a bit more time to gather more data, and I’ll post a short update. 📈
