Why Are Medical Students Learning About Physician Unions?

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

— Brian Muthyala, MD, MPH, weighs the promise and pitfalls of collective action

Medical students are taught how poverty, access to care, and other systemic factors affect patients' health, but many struggle with a fundamental question: What can one physician realistically do about problems that extend far beyond the exam room?

At the University of Minnesota Medical School in Minneapolis, Brian Muthyala, MD, MPH, teaches second-year students about building collective power -- including physician unionization -- as one potential approach to addressing systemic challenges in healthcare.

In this exclusive MedPage Today video, Muthyala discusses why he introduced the course, how physicians can advocate for patients beyond individual clinical encounters, and why he believes unionization holds promise but is not without risks.

The following is a transcript of this video:

I don't think unionization is a panacea. I don't think it's a silver bullet, and it has its own risks.

So I started teaching this course and it's built on the idea that collective problems require collective solutions. So I'm the director of our social medicine thread in the medical school at the University of Minnesota. And so what I teach is around all of the nonbiological reasons that one person might be sick.

And in the context of many conversations over the medical students' first 2 years, they get to this point where they recognize that poverty, low socioeconomic status, a history of experiencing structural racism, you name it, all of these are sort of large collective problems. And many students say, "Well, what am I supposed to do about that? I'm just one person and I need to learn how to take care of pneumonia and heart attacks and rheumatologic conditions. I need to learn all these things, and I've got to deal with this too. What am I supposed to do?"

And the session is really two parts. And so the first is a webinar that's sort of a conversation around what are different ways historically that physicians have built collective power. We talk about things like mutual aid. We talk about things like community-based participation. And then one of the things we talk about is the history of unionization by physicians historically. The second part of that series is I brought a panel of mostly physicians, but also labor organizers as well, to talk about the issue of physician unionization -- challenges, pitfalls, all of those things as one approach that's imperfect certainly, but one approach to building the collective voice of physicians to try to solve large societal or collective problems that exist.

Historically, the way I was taught physician advocacy was, well, if you've got a problem with something, write a letter to your senator, go testify at the Capitol. And that feels unreachable to a lot of students. They're like, "I'm really busy right now. And then when I'm a physician, I have 20-minute appointments. I've got all this paperwork. I've got all this stuff to do. That feels really inaccessible."

We don't get to ignore it as physicians. As physicians, we're sort of stewards of a community trust and it's our responsibility to understand these issues to the best of our ability and address them to the best of our ability -- and that doesn't mean quitting our day job and going to the Capitol or whatever. And so I give them this sort of pyramid of approach and the first level is understanding the problem. So reading about it, understanding the history, learning.

The second is how do I start to communicate interpersonally with patients around these issues as they impact? How does access to care affect the patient that I'm sitting in front of? The next is how do I understand my own institution's support around these issues? What does my clinic do to help somebody sign up for insurance if they just lost Medicaid, whatever that may be. Because every system is going to be different in terms of what resources they have. And the last is a policy level. You get to choose which level you want to approach these issues.

We've seen a corporatization of healthcare delivery in the last 25 years. If you want to have a seat at the table, we have to build a system that will allow you to operate against and oftentimes push against large corporate interests, which I and others, and I think many students identify as something that puts patients at risk. And ultimately that's what this comes down to is I think unionization can be -- I don't know if it has always been -- can be one way to elevate the needs of patients to a level in which they can be advocated for. And I think physicians are responding to the corporatization of medical care.

I say this in the class: I am nervous about physician unionization. Historically, physician groups have not been unions, but physician groups have been professional societies, so like the American Medical Association [AMA] as an example. My worry about physician unions are the same worries that I have around historical physician groups. The AMA was against the initial legislation that brought forward Medicare and Medicaid in this country. The AMA, again, historically at its inception did not allow for nonwhite physicians to join its ranks.

And so the history of physicians joining their collective voice is complicated and physicians are not a homogeneous group. In fact, I think we're a very heterogeneous group. Will unions be able to focus a large heterogeneous group's energies, privilege, power towards the betterment of patients is a question that I don't think has been answered. And I think rightfully should be questioned if it's possible.

I'm an optimist by my nature and I think that the more voices that are at a table that are not representative of corporate entities is a good one. And so I think physician unions have the potential and possibility of really advocating for significant change for the betterment of patients, but that remains to be seen at this point.
