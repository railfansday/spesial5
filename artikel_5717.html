FERC must take action to protect ratepayers from higher transmission costs in SPP

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Southwest Power Pool is misusing an exemption meant for urgent reliability needs to avoid competition in favor of incumbent utilities, writes Paul Cicio.

Paul Cicio is chairman of the Electricity Transmission Competition Coalition and president and CEO of the Industrial Energy Consumers of America.

As electricity prices continue to surge, consumers cannot afford to pay more than necessary for the new transmission lines that deliver their power. Requiring utilities to compete with one another gives regulators a direct way to reduce those costs.

Earlier this year, the Southwest Power Pool decided to exempt four projects expected to cost $3.8 billion from competitive bidding by classifying them as short-term reliability projects under its 2025 Integrated Transmission Planning portfolio.

SPP’s tariff specifies that unless a project is required to be in service in three years or less, it must face competition in building new large transmission lines, consistent with Federal Energy Regulatory Commission Order 1000. But the projects SPP has designated as short-term in its 2025 portfolio are not expected to enter service until 2029 or 2030.

SPP violated its FERC-approved tariff by not requiring the transmission projects to face competition. It is misusing an exemption meant for urgent reliability needs to avoid competition in favor of incumbent utilities, contrary to the vital electricity affordability needs of ratepayers. As a result, ratepayers in the 17-state region could pay an estimated $750 million in higher electricity bills for these projects.

We at the Electricity Transmission Competition Coalition and the Industrial Energy Consumers of America have filed a complaint asking FERC to hold SPP accountable for violating its tariff and require the four projects to be competitively bid.

Ratepayers are concerned because SPP has relied on this exemption before and the projects have grown larger and more expensive over time.

In 2024, SPP directly assigned $3.2 billion in projects to incumbent utilities based on short-term reliability need, without competitive bidding. The Industrial Energy Consumers of America opposed those projects in a January 2025 letter to SPP.

This past January, the ETCC urged the SPP Board of Directors to put its 2025 short-term reliability projects, which included new 765-kV lines, through competitive bidding. But SPP has continued to bypass competition over these and other stakeholder objections without providing sufficient justification or transparency.

If FERC allows SPP to continue, the short-term reliability exemption could become a routine way to avoid competition altogether, and the precedent would reach ratepayers across the country.

When utilities are required to compete with one another, they sharpen their pencils and contractually commit to cost containment provisions. Some include provisions that guarantee delivery of the project on time or pay a financial penalty.

None of the $3.2 billion projects SPP directly assigned in 2024 included a guaranteed in-service deadline or cost containment provisions.

Competition works. ETCC reviewed 19 transmission projects that went through competitive bidding and found an average cost reduction of 34%. ETCC’s review of 15 projects that did not go through competitive bidding found an average cost overrun of 89%.

Also, there is no evidence that assigning projects directly to incumbent utilities gets them built faster. For example, SPP competitively bid the Minco and Wolf Creek projects, which entered into service in 2025 and met their needed or expected in-service dates.

Competition can deliver on both reduced costs and on time start-up of the project. Ratepayers do not have to sacrifice reliability for lower costs.

The nearly $3.8 billion price tag is only the beginning of what consumers could ultimately pay. When financing costs, utility profits and other expenses are factored in, the total cost to ratepayers could more than double over the life of these projects.

SPP's decisions today will shape electricity costs for families and businesses across the region for decades to come. And SPP is poised to develop an even costlier transmission plan for 2027 that includes billions of dollars more in projects that would be exempted from competition.

The Federal Power Act is first and foremost a consumer protection act and FERC has a responsibility to ensure that consumers receive just and reasonable rates. FERC approved SPP’s tariff and has a duty to enforce it.

Electricity consumers will ultimately pay for the billions of dollars that grid operators commit to new transmission. FERC should hold SPP to competitive bidding as the standard for that spending, with exemptions limited to the narrow cases for projects that can be completed and placed into service in three years or less.

The SPP complaint is yet another test for FERC. Will the commission stand with consumers or protect monopoly utility profits? There is a troubling pattern of inaction by FERC to enforce Order 1000. Consumers have filed four complaints at FERC dating back to 2022 without them taking action, despite electricity affordability being top of mind nationwide.
