Lightning lights up towering storms above the South China Sea

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Lightning illuminates towering thunderstorms over the South China Sea in an astronaut photo that also captures Singapore and fishing boat lights.

Today’s Image of the Day captures the South China Sea after dark, where city lights and scattered offshore glows surround towering thunderstorms.

Lightning briefly illuminates the clouds from within, revealing rounded storm tops that rise above the surrounding cloud cover.

These domes mark places where strong updrafts have pushed parts of the storms above their broad, flattened tops.

An astronaut aboard the International Space Station captured the scene near Borneo on October 28, 2026.

The photograph brings together several sources of nighttime light, each with a different explanation.

Thunderstorms begin with rising air. When warm, moist air moves upward, it cools, and water vapor condenses into cloud droplets.

With a continuing supply of moisture and upward motion, a cloud can grow into a towering storm.

That growth eventually encounters an atmospheric boundary called the tropopause. It separates the troposphere, the lowest layer where most weather develops, from the stratosphere above.

At this boundary, rising air generally spreads sideways. The result is the broad, flattened cloud canopy known as a thunderstorm’s anvil.

A particularly strong updraft can briefly push beyond that canopy, producing a rounded bulge called an overshooting top.

These protrusions reveal the force of the rising air inside a storm, even when much of its internal activity remains hidden.

Several overshooting tops appear in the astronaut’s photograph. Lightning illuminates two of them, helping expose their shapes against the dark surroundings.

Such domes can sometimes extend into the stratosphere.

Their presence signals vigorous storm development, although the photograph alone does not establish the precise height of these particular clouds.

The light inside a thunderstorm begins with interactions among tiny particles.

High in the cloud, rising air supports a mixture of ice crystals, supercooled water droplets, and soft ice pellets called graupel. Collisions among these particles transfer electrical charge.

The storm’s air currents help separate the charged particles.

Smaller ice crystals can travel upward, while heavier particles remain lower, allowing regions with different electrical charges to develop.

When the electrical field becomes strong enough, a lightning discharge occurs.

That discharge can remain inside a cloud or connect different clouds, rather than reaching the ground.

From below, an observer may see a branching bolt or a sudden brightening overhead. From orbit, clouds can scatter the light into a much broader glow.

The storm briefly reveals its contours, giving astronauts a view of cloud structure that people beneath it cannot see.

The photograph freezes one of those fleeting moments. It records the illuminated cloud tops alongside lights that were already shining across the surrounding land and sea.

Singapore appears on the left side of the image. The Singapore Strait separates the city-state from the Indonesian islands of Batam and Bintan to its south.

Elsewhere, clusters of light interrupt the darkness offshore.

NASA identifies the blue lights west of Singapore as likely fishing vessels using powerful lamps to attract squid or schooling fish, including anchovies and sardines.

Scattered orange points farther offshore to the northwest are likely gas flares.

Those identifications carry some uncertainty.

Color and location help interpret the scene, but a distant point of light does not reveal its source as clearly as a close-up photograph would.

Together, the lights show how much activity continues after sunset.

Urban areas form concentrated patches, while offshore operations produce isolated points and clusters across otherwise dark water.

The setting also helps explain why storms are a prominent part of the view.

Singapore experiences thunderstorms throughout the year. They become more frequent during the transition periods between monsoons, generally in April and May and again in October and November.

During these periods, light and variable winds favor the development of local thunderstorms, particularly during the afternoon and evening.

Warm, humid air supplies the ingredients for clouds to build upward.

This seasonal pattern provides context for the photograph, though it does not establish how each storm in the frame formed.

The image captures the region at 14:20 UTC, well after sunset locally. A member of the station’s Expedition 75 crew took the photo with a Nikon Z9 camera and a 14-millimeter lens.

The published photograph was cropped and enhanced for contrast, with lens artifacts removed.

For a moment, lightning made the atmosphere itself part of the illuminated landscape.

Beneath the station, the lights of a city, people working at sea, and towering tropical storms shared the same frame.
