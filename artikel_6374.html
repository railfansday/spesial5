Could a nuclear blast save Earth from an asteroid? Good aim will mean everything

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

Computer simulations reveal how an asteroid’s shape could affect efforts to push it off course with X-rays from a nearby nuclear blast.

A nuclear blast set off near a dangerous asteroid could, in theory, push it away from Earth. But if the asteroid has a lumpy, uneven shape, how hard it gets pushed depends on which side the blast hits.

In new computer tests, hitting one side of an oddly shaped asteroid instead of another gave it nearly five times as much push.

So a real defense mission could depend on knowing an asteroid’s shape before anyone chooses where to aim.

Nathan Moore, a physicist at Sandia National Laboratories, led the research. “We expected aim to matter,” he said. “What surprised us was how much.”

No asteroid was blasted for this work. The team ran every test in computer simulations, using the shapes of real asteroids.

In this kind of defense, a nuclear device would go off some distance from the asteroid, not on it.

The blast sends out a burst of X-rays that heats one side of the asteroid and turns part of its surface into vapor. That vapor rushes off into space, and the pressure it puts on the surface pushes the asteroid the other way.

The goal is to push the asteroid, not smash it. Breaking it apart could turn one big threat into smaller pieces that could still be deadly.

A nuclear blast is a different approach from crashing a spacecraft into an asteroid, which NASA’s DART mission tested in 2022.

Moore has worked on this question since a 2024 experiment at Sandia’s Z machine, a lab device that produces intense bursts of X-rays.

That experiment showed for the first time that X-rays could push a small piece of asteroid material.

Next, he wanted to know how a full-size asteroid would respond. Most researchers had treated asteroids as perfect spheres because that made the math easier, he said.

Real asteroids have irregular shapes that a perfect sphere cannot capture.

“You can make estimates that way, but asteroids come in all kinds of odd, lumpy shapes,” Moore said.

He found a way forward in a colleague’s work on how objects in space give off gas.

“I think we had an aha moment when we realized we could do this,” he said. “No more spheres. We would be able to model deflection for any asteroid.”

Moore built the new model by joining two computer programs that Sandia already used for very different jobs.

One usually simulates fast, high-energy events, such as experiments on the Z machine. The other usually handles weak forces that change slowly, like the drag the solar wind puts on a satellite.

In the new model, the first program works out how the X-rays heat the surface and turn part of it to gas.

The second follows that gas as it spreads across the asteroid’s real 3D shape, and it calculates the pressure on every part of the surface.

“The key innovation here was figuring out how to combine the two simulation approaches,” Moore said.

He also pointed to speed. “Our new algorithm is over 1,600 times faster, which was critical to this breakthrough,” he said. “With the old models, computers just couldn’t handle it.”

To use the model, the team loads in an asteroid’s shape, which can come from detailed 3D images taken by passing spacecraft.

They then set the size of the blast, how far away it goes off, and which side it hits.

The program maps how much of each part of the surface turns to vapor. From that map, it works out how hard the asteroid gets pushed and how fast it spins afterward.

The team tested the model on three real asteroids with very different shapes. Bennu is the asteroid NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft sampled in 2020. The other two were Itokawa and Golevka.

For each one, the researchers tried blasts at close range and farther away. They also changed which way the asteroid was facing when the X-rays arrived.

In the simulations, orienting Bennu at its most favorable angle, instead of its worst, gave it up to nearly twice as much push.

The difference was larger for the other two, at up to nearly four times for Itokawa and five times for Golevka.

In one simulation, the blast changed an asteroid’s speed by about 0.15 inch (3.9 millimeters) per second from one angle. From another angle, it was only about 0.03 inch (0.8 millimeter) per second.

The asteroid and the blast were the same both times, and only the side facing the X-rays was different.

The aim also affected how fast an asteroid spun after the blast. For one asteroid, the change in its spin rate varied 17-fold depending on which side faced the blast.

“Our study shows how important it is to aim carefully,” Moore said. “Pick the right side and you win. Guess wrong and it could be a bad day.”

To run the model, the team needs a fairly accurate picture of an asteroid’s shape from every side. In a real emergency, there might not be time to get one.

Placing a nuclear device at the right distance, on the right side of an asteroid speeding through space, would also be hard.

Another unknown is what happens to the whole asteroid.

“Right now, our computer model only considers breakup of rocks on the surface,” he said. “We can’t predict breakup of the entire asteroid body accurately yet.”

When the researchers applied the blast pressures to a loose, Itokawa-like pile of boulders, the rock thrown off was lopsided, just like the vapor.

Predicting whether a whole asteroid breaks apart will take more research and better modeling tools. An X-ray blast would set off many effects at once, and those effects could change one another.

That full calculation is what Moore and other scientists are working toward.

The full study was published in the journal Icarus.
