The search for early signs of Alzheimer’s is looking beyond the brain’s memory center

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Tracking shrinkage in two brain regions could help improve early Alzheimer’s detection, though the approach needs further testing.

Doctors searching a brain scan for Alzheimer’s disease usually look at the hippocampus, the brain’s main memory center.

A new study suggests that two neighboring regions, the entorhinal cortex and the amygdala, shrink faster than the hippocampus in older adults with memory problems.

If further research confirms that these changes precede symptoms, tracking the two regions could help identify people at risk before memory loss begins.

Michael Miller is a biomedical engineer at Johns Hopkins University and the study’s senior author.

Miller said shrinkage from the slow loss of brain cells can show up on scans as much as a decade before a person has symptoms.

“Imagine if we could start to treat the neurodegenerative changes at the 2, 3 or 4-year point rather than after 15 years when the changes are manifesting in memory symptoms,” Miller said.

“That’s the great promise here for your loved ones who are at risk.”

The study compared how fast each region shrank in groups of people at different stages of the disease. It didn’t time when the shrinkage starts, and the measurements can’t yet be used to diagnose anyone.

The findings were published in August and presented this week at Neuroscience 2026, the annual meeting of the Society for Neuroscience.

In Alzheimer’s, a protein called tau clumps into tangles inside brain cells. A staging system published in 1991 mapped where those tangles appear as the disease advances.

The tangles show up first in and around the entorhinal cortex, a thin strip of tissue beside the hippocampus. They reach the hippocampus next and then spread across the brain’s outer layer.

The amygdala is a nearby region involved in processing fear and emotional memories.

The 1991 staging system doesn’t focus on the amygdala until the middle stages of the disease. Later tissue studies found tangles there relatively early too.

Tangles don’t show up on an ordinary MRI. An MRI can show a region getting smaller as its cells are lost.

But the entorhinal cortex is hard to outline on a scan. It’s thin, and it lies close to fluid-filled spaces that can confuse automated software.

Miller’s team used MRI scans from 1,149 people in two research projects that scan the same volunteers repeatedly over years. Every person had at least three scans.

Most of the volunteers, 1,019 of them, came from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, a multi-site project launched in 2003.

The other 130 came from BIOCARD, a study that began at the National Institutes of Health in 1995 and moved to Johns Hopkins in 2009.

The volunteers’ average ages at the first scan ranged from about 68 to 74. Some had typical memory for their age, and some had Alzheimer’s dementia.

Others had mild cognitive impairment, a stage of memory and thinking trouble that doesn’t yet stop a person from managing daily life.

Two people outlined the amygdala and entorhinal cortex by hand on 665 scans, with a neuroanatomist guiding the work. A deep-learning program learned from those outlines and then marked the regions across the full set of scans.

From those outlines, the team calculated how much volume each region lost per year in each person.

Among BIOCARD volunteers with mild cognitive impairment or dementia, the hippocampus lost 0.76% of its volume a year.

The amygdala lost 2.38% a year, and the entorhinal cortex, which the team measured together with a thin adjoining strip of tissue, lost 3.69%.

In the larger project, the order was the same. The hippocampus in that project’s Alzheimer’s group lost 3.23% of its volume a year, compared to 7.19% for the amygdala and 8.40% for the entorhinal cortex.

In both projects, and on both sides of the brain, the entorhinal cortex and the amygdala shrank faster than the hippocampus.

Volunteers with typical memory showed that order too, at lower rates. In the larger project, their entorhinal cortex lost 1.84% of its volume a year. The rate was 3.53% in mild cognitive impairment and 8.40% in Alzheimer’s.

So the order alone doesn’t separate healthy people from people with the disease. The pace of the loss was higher at each later stage.

Different hospitals use different scanners, and the team found that raw volume measurements varied between manufacturers. The yearly shrinkage rates were steadier.

Within each diagnostic group, the rates didn’t differ reliably between machines from GE, Philips, and Siemens.

The team also checked the shrinkage against PET scans, which use a radioactive tracer to show where tau has built up.

Among volunteers with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s, people with a stronger tau signal in the entorhinal cortex were also losing volume there faster. The team found the same link in the hippocampus, and in both projects.

The team rebuilt the surface of the entorhinal cortex in more than 400 computer models to measure its thickness. The faster the region lost volume, the faster that sheet of tissue thinned.

Inside the amygdala, the loss was uneven. In one group with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s, three subregions toward the middle of the brain shrank by 6.34% to 8.31% a year.

The subregion on the outer side shrank by 2.52%.

The outer subregion also had the fewest tangles in postmortem Alzheimer’s brain tissue that the group had mapped in an earlier study.

The scans show tau and tissue loss in the same places. They can’t show that the tau caused the loss.

The team also asked a practical question: Can these two regions help distinguish people with typical memory from those with early mild cognitive impairment?

To find out, they tested 346 volunteers with typical memory and 210 with early mild cognitive impairment.

A statistical model was first built using only hippocampus measurements.

It scored 0.635 on a scale where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect. When the amygdala and entorhinal cortex were added, the score rose to 0.665.

The authors described that performance as moderate.

Most of the gain came from fewer healthy people being wrongly flagged. At the same detection rate, about eight fewer healthy people in every 100 were misclassified.

Scientists call any measurable sign of a disease a biomarker. A scan result is one example, and a blood test is another.

“As biomarkers and treatments become increasingly available, knowing who is going to have cognitive decline is going to be really important,” said co-author Meaghan Morris.

The new analysis didn’t measure how long before symptoms the shrinkage starts. The decade Miller described comes from a 2014 study by some of the same researchers.

The 2014 study estimated that shrinkage in this part of the brain speeds up almost 10 years before the symptoms of mild cognitive impairment begin.

The volunteers’ average ages were around 70, so the scans say little about what these regions look like in younger adults.

Miller reported holding equity in AnatomyWorks, a company that develops digital anatomy atlases. The team hopes to learn what is happening at the molecular level around the tau changes in the two regions.

“Once we know more about the molecular neighborhoods, we can develop therapies,” Miller said.

Future research could test whether tracking the two regions in people without symptoms predicts who goes on to develop them.

The full study was published in the journal NeuroImage: Reports.
