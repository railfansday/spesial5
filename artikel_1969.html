A message for my fellow Newcastle United fans.

There is a sound in sport more discordant than the pure roar of opposition.

Our recent Everton home match showcased it.

It was loud, and it was often, and it was heard by football fans all around the world. It is the sound of a home crowd turning on its own. At St. James’ Park, that sound has been heard with increasing frequency in recent matches—sharp, collective, and unmistakable.

Newcastle United fans, among the most passionate and historically loyal in English football, have at times directed their frustration not solely at the referee or the visiting side, but at the men in black and white who wear the shirt they revere. And while discontent is a fan’s prerogative, the practice of booing one’s own players carries consequences that extend far beyond a single afternoon’s dissatisfaction. It damages the athletes who must perform under it’s weight and it slowly erodes the very reputation of the supporters who claim to love the club most.

Consider first the players. Professional footballers are not automata. They are human beings operating at the outer edge of physical and psychological demand, often carrying the accumulated pressure of ownership expectations, media scrutiny, and the weight of a city’s hopes. When the crowd that once lifted them now turns its voice against them, the effect is not motivational theatre. It is corrosive.

Confidence is a fragile commodity in sport; once fractured in public, it is slow to mend. A player (especially a young or new player) who hears the boos after a misplaced pass or a missed chance does not suddenly discover a higher gear. More often he tightens, second-guesses, and retreats into the safety of safer choices. The game becomes smaller. Risk—the very quality that produces moments of brilliance—becomes something to be avoided.

History offers quiet lessons. The great teams of the past were not immune to periods of struggle, yet the bond between player and supporter was understood as reciprocal. Kevin Keegan’s side of the mid-1990s rode waves of belief that were almost tangible. Even in disappointment, the underlying current remained one of shared purpose.

When that current reverses, the club’s identity begins to fray. Players notice. They speak of it privately, if not always publicly. They adjust their body language, their willingness to demand the ball, their instinct to press high. What the stands intend as accountability can register on the pitch as abandonment. For those of us that have played footy at any level (or any public activity) – loud criticism seldom inspires, it creates a heavier load.

There is also the matter of reputation—the intangible but real currency of a club’s supporters. Newcastle United fans have long been celebrated for their loyalty through lean decades, for the sheer volume and colour they bring to the occasion, for the sense that the club and the city are inseparable. That reputation is not automatic. It is earned, season after season, through constancy of support that does not evaporate when results turn. Booing one’s own players, particularly in sustained fashion, risks rewriting that narrative. Outside observers—pundits, rival supporters, potential recruits—begin to form a different picture: of a crowd that is quick to turn, of an atmosphere that can become hostile even to those fighting in the same colours. Perception hardens. The next talented midfielder weighing a move, the young academy graduate deciding whether to commit his future, the manager assessing the environment he would inherit—all of them factor atmosphere into the equation. A club that once marketed itself on the passion of its people can find that passion reinterpreted as volatility.

None of this is an argument for silence or passive acceptance of mediocrity. Criticism is legitimate. I would argue, and I believe that most would agree, a team of players can inspire the crowd but that the reverse is true also. A crowd can create positive energy that propels the team on the pitch. It is a symbiotic relationship – and its this relationship that can make the home field a fortress. A negative reaction from the crowd can also beget poorer play on the pitch. Yes, boards, managers, and players should be held to account. But there is a distinction between demanding better and actively undermining those currently charged with delivering it. The former can be expressed through measured reaction, through pointed but constructive noise after the final whistle, through the continued presence and volume that still says “this matters.” The latter—booing in real time—sends a different message: that the relationship is conditional, and that the players are no longer considered part of the collective struggle. And when coming from the Gallowgate, arguably the most passionate fans anywhere, their negativity sets the tone for all.

Sport at its best creates a temporary community in which strangers become, for ninety minutes, something closer to allies. That community is tested when results fail. How it responds in those moments reveals its character. Newcastle United fans have, through far darker periods than the present, demonstrated a capacity for loyalty that borders on the heroic. The question now is whether that tradition will be preserved when the going is merely difficult rather than desperate. The players will answer on the pitch. The reputation of the fans will be answered in the stands—by the choice of what sound they choose to make when the team needs them most.

I know the players make a lot of money. I also know that buying the ticket gives each fan the right to express themselves in their own way.
