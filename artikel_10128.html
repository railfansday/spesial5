Floppy drives are very long-lived. Now, it is conceivable that the specific brand and model(s) that have NSNs are no longer super available on the secondary market, but I would feel confident in saying that for a very very modest amount of money - under $100 per workstation - the FAA could acquire enough stock today to ensure that every workstation has a working drive for the next fifty years. More, actually. Assumedly these drives are not used to boot and run the system's software and read/write files every hour - they are probably used once a day, or less frequently, to transfer some kind of updates. The key would be preventive maintenance; the failure mode for these drives is mostly related to lubricants drying out, though capacitor decay is an issue too (might be much less of an issue for a 2010-era drive; the ones I've had to repair for cap issues came from roughly the era of the Great Electrolyte Plague).

A more open question might well be - what computer are they using? A PC old enough to have a floppy controller won't run any modern software which means it won't be compliant with any modern security protocols. So the only real option is probably USB floppy drives.

I'm not so sure 50 years is viable. Many drives are rusting at this point. They would need a humidity controlled environment to stand much chance of lasting that long.

The problem is more so the electronics. We're seeing lots of drive failures from late 80s early 90s PS/2 machines because a few tiny electrolytic caps start leaking. You also run into things like grease drying up and turning into glue and tiny nylon gears becoming brittle and cracking. I was so happy when 3.5" floppies went away.
