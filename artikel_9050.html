Hail to the Squash King

Tanggal: 2026-11-22

Hilma af Klint painting shatters records, new Edward Said documentary, the politics of pigment, and how should we memorialize the Trump era?

Swimming in a Peter Bradley color field. Leaping from dot to dot on a Howardena Pindell speckled canvas. A Peter Jemison horse prances toward me. A Pepsi Cherry truck pulls over as I’m having deep thoughts about authenticity.

In the morning glow, a pretty woman walks down the sidewalk with a carton of a dozen eggs. A faded black-and-white Marilyn Monroe poster greets customers at the Organic Deli. A photo in the paper shows the presidents of the United States, China, and Russia against a giant painting of the Great Wall. A Madonna song whispers, “Express yourself, express yourself.”

A well-dressed man cycling to work is nice to look at. A Hilma af Klint painting sells for $12.7 million at auction, 16 times its highest estimate. Meanwhile at the supermarket, just three small items set me back $100. How could that be? If the middle class had any guts, they’d be out there on the streets burning effigies of Adam Smith and Sigmund Freud. But they’re not; they’re waiting 40 minutes in line across the street just to buy a bagel.

At the Ace Hotel, I read something I’ve written for the new edition of The Other Almanac. I wear my heart on my sleeve in front of strangers. The next speaker is a whale necropsy expert. At the end of the evening someone is crowned “Squash King.”

A golden sunset falls over the glass towers of Long Island City. The Kosciuszko Bridge is lit with cotton candy colors. Fog in Downtown Brooklyn, or is it smoke? I can’t really tell. In Chelsea, I follow a group of people into an art gallery just to see what they’re into. It turns out to be a big mistake.

In their (still brilliant) 1990 experimental film Introduction to the End of an Argument, filmmakers Jayce Salloum and Elia Suleiman splice together troves of found footage about Palestinians and Israelis. The result is an exquisite mash-up of Hollywood cartoons and political clichés, a comic pastiche illustrating the common bond between Orientalism and zombies. Both just won’t die. | Moustafa Bayoumi
