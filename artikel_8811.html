Best GPS Bike Computers of 2026: 10 Editor-Tested Picks for Navigation, Training Data, and Battery Life

Tanggal: 2026-11-28

Find the right bike computer for your rides with editor-tested picks for every budget and priority.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Bike computers are one of the best equipment upgrades you can make for your road, gravel, and mountain bike rides. Head units display common metrics like speed, distance, and power, and track and record your data, so you can download it and crunch the info after your rides. They’re also great for ride navigation and following pre-planned routes. Today’s leading head units put features from turn-by-turn GPS navigation to real-time performance analytics at your fingertips.

I use a cycling computer on virtually every ride. I do this to test more devices and log the many miles I pedal on different bikes and equipment. An upside of always riding with a GPS-equipped computer is mid-ride exploration and changing rides on the fly. Now, if I’m curious about a road or potential shortcut, I can check whether it goes where I think. And rerouting features on many computers let me cut rides short and head home if weather changes.

Many new bike computers integrate with popular platforms such as Strava, Trailforks, and Garmin Connect. Our favorite models offer real-time hill profiles on climbs and feature data-rich maps for precise navigation, even when descending switchback roads at speed. Some models even feature solar charging and real-time weather alerts. But beyond the fancy features, every bike computer does the same basic job—record your ride and display the data you need.

Since our editors and test team use cycling computers on nearly every ride, we’re constantly riding the newest models. Brands offer dozens of cycling-specific computers, and many have similar shapes, screens, and button layouts. However, as our testing has proven, the differences are in the details. And picking the right cycling computer for your needs makes rides much more enjoyable.

Buying Advice: Modern cycling computers operate a lot like cell phones (the Hammerhead is actually an Android-based platform). So, think of their operating systems like using a mobile device. What works for someone else might not be right for you, but that doesn’t make it a bad computer. Try computers before buying (if possible), because you cannot change the interface without getting a new device.

The Wahoo Elemnt Bolt V3 combines excellent features at a great price, making it a strong option for a wide range of cyclists. In his review of the Bolt V3, Test Editor Dan Chabanov dug into the revamped computer’s details. Its features include a 2.3-inch high-resolution display with 16 million colors for increased clarity and improved battery life (up to 20 hours). The Bolt’s interface still prioritizes information in an easy-to-read format and uses buttons rather than a touch screen.

The Bolt displays grade and climb profiles that show difficulty by assigning different background colors depending on the hill’s gradient (red being the steepest). This makes it easy to see at a glance what the climb ahead of you will be like.

Wahoo computers feature an easy setup process and an intuitive companion app that connects to third-party tools. The Bolt V3 has 32 GB of onboard storage, which is enough to store maps for the United States and thousands of routes. The Elemnt Bolt V3 gives you everything you need (and nothing you don’t).

Garmin introduced the all-new Edge 850 late last year. It features a slightly larger and brighter screen, an integrated bell, voice prompts, and more. However, it also adds $150 to the price. For this reason, we’re still big fans of Garmin’s Edge 840.

While the Edge 840 features less functionality than the newer Edge 850, it costs significantly less and has a longer battery life. The 840 provides riders with all the data they need to manage a training program and track progress. These include VO2 max output (when paired with a heart monitor), recovery time, training load, and heat and altitude acclimation. It also offers cycling dynamics like position, right/left balance (when paired with a power meter), and bike setup. The Edge 840 provides plenty of detailed info if you like charts and analyzing data.

Overall, the 840 is an excellent option for riders who love training and digging into data. It offers a reasonable balance of price and features in the generally pricey Garmin bike computer lineup. If you need more battery life, choose the 840 Solar, which extends usage up to an impressive 32 hours.

If you want the maximum possible battery life for a cycling computer, Coros’ recently launched Dura 2 is your standout choice. Although the new Dura costs double the original, the Dura 2 features a sleeker design, improved maps and navigation, and clean pairing with devices such as power meters, heart rate monitors, and electronic drivetrains.

Like the original Dura, the second-gen model gets exceptional battery life, and that’s without considering its solar charging capabilities. Coros claims the Dura 2 achieves up to 120 hours of use on a single charge. In Dan Chabanov’s testing, he still had 77 percent battery remaining after 30 hours of ride time—that’s frankly exceptional for a cycling computer.

However, with its great battery life and solar charging come a couple of downsides. Notably, the price: the Dura 2 costs $499 compared to the original model’s $250 price tag. The other notable miss is the Dura 2’s rerouting. It requires a connection to Coros’ smartphone app, and we found it hit-or-miss in our testing.

If you don’t have the budget for a Wahoo, Garmin, or Hammerhead computer, you still have a few solid options. For only $100, Magene offers a full-color touchscreen, turn-by-turn navigation, and even has a hill gradient feature and Strava segment integration (with a firmware update). That puts the mighty little C506 in the league of its big-name competitors at a fraction of the price.

The low price, however, means the C506 isn’t quite as polished as the pricey head units. For example, you must pair your smartphone to upload routes. Some users also report touchscreen lag and software bugs. However, to its credit, Magene’s firmware upgrades have added a lot of functionality and improved shortcomings. If you have a limited budget and don’t mind a few usage quirks, the C506’s price and low weight make it hard to pass up.

Since launching two and a half years ago, Hammerhead’s Karoo computer has consistently remained my go-to head unit for road and gravel rides. Why? Because it’s the right size, has excellent navigation, pairs well with SRAM components, and features my favorite user interface.

The Karoo’s navigation and touchscreen are best-in-class. On-the-fly route planning is easy—I love its ability to navigate to any Google Maps destination (shared via the Hammerhead app) and use the feature often. The Karoo’s UI is very intuitive, and it’s easy to find functions or customize the screens (compared to the bloated interfaces on some competitors’ cycling computers).

While the Karoo doesn’t look much different from its predecessor, the Karoo 2 (yes, it’s called the Karoo even though it’s the third-gen), it offers many solid updates: A dedicated power button, improved battery life (up to 35 hours), an ambient light sensor, and automatic backlight control.

Hammerhead’s smartphone app offers automatic ride uploads and live tracking—you can send a link to family or friends to track your ride progress. Another Hammerhead strong suit is its bi-weekly software updates that remedy bugs and add useful features. Combined with the ability to sideload third-party apps, the Karoo’s function constantly improves.

For AXS-equipped bike owners, the Karoo integrates tightly with SRAM’s electronic components. For example, you can program AXS shift buttons (including the bonus buttons on Force and Red hoods) directly from the computer. Read more about this integration in Senior Test Editor Matt Phillips’ story “SRAM Launches its New Red AXS Road Group.”

If you only need a cycling computer for the basics (speed, distance, heart rate, power, etc.) and don’t want a cellphone-sized device hanging off your bar, the Cycplus M2 is a great option. This tiny computer covers all the bases at a fraction of the cost of big-name competitors. It also has great styling that won’t look out of place on lightweight bikes, and it's small enough to use on the trail.

While the M2 lacks amenities such as a touchscreen display or mapping, it features a 70-hour battery life, GPS tracking, and ANT+ and Bluetooth connectivity to your power meter and HRM. Cycplus’s app will also sync your rides to Strava to track your progress.

Overhauled in 2025, Wahoo’s Elemnt Roam is consistently one of our favorite cycling computers. Updates to the Roam V3 over the V2 include a larger touchscreen display, dual-band GPS for enhanced connection in mountains and dense woods, 64GB internal storage, and improved battery life.

In addition to the V3’s larger size and weight, Wahoo added a speaker for voice navigation and an integrated bell (activated with a quick two-finger tap on the screen). However, Wahoo removed the V2’s fan-favorite LED status lights. Additionally, we found the screen brightness a bit dim in our testing compared to Garmin and Hammerhead displays.

As Chabanov noted in his detailed review of the Roam, beyond its overall solid reliability, the Roam is a standard mid-tier bike computer. It features a slew of handy navigation features, including turn-by-turn directions, navigate to start, back on track, mountain bike trail integration, saved locations, retrace route, and take me to.

After using the Edge 1050 for almost six months, the best word to describe Garmin’s range-topping computer is “big”. This computer is big in size, features, and price. But all of this bigness provides an experience unlike anything found on other cycling computers—and if not for the 1050’s price, it would be vying for the Best Overall Computer title.

There is no getting around the Edge 1050’s size, and at 2.4” x 4.7” x 0.6”, it takes up a lot of real estate on your bar. In fact, the 105 is so long that it’s incompatible with many standard mounts. However, the enormous size makes it easy to read data, follow routes, and scroll screens.

Navigation features include turn-by-turn directions, maps showing the popular routes locals use, and recalculation when you stray from your course. And while they might not like the 1050’s dimensions, mountain bikers will love its integration with the Trailforks app. However, this is merely scratching the surface of the Edge 1050’s superpowers; if you want to see the data, the 1050 is probably logging it, and Garmin most likely has a screen for it buried in the long list of data fields.

The Edge 1050 also offers some thoughtful safety features, including the ability to automatically send an emergency message to your specified contacts when it detects a crash, group messaging, and a ‘follow my ride’ feature that shares your real-time position with friends and family.

If you like these features but want a smaller package (and slightly lower price), check out the Garmin Edge 850. You can dig into the details on the 850 in Dan Chabanov’s full-length review.

Like Garmin’s Edge 1050, Wahoo goes similarly huge with its Elemnt Ace. The screen’s 3.8-inch size is impressive, as is its battery life (up to 30 hours). Plus, the Ace features Wahoo’s signature user-friendliness. Matt Phillips summed up his full review of the Elemnt Ace, saying, “If you crave big and bold, this is your cycling computer.”

The Ace’s super-sized screen offers room for large and easy-to-read data fields. The touchscreen function is also excellent, even while wearing gloves. Wahoo’s signature page zoom function—use the up and down buttons to zoom in on fewer fields or out on more fields—quickly makes prioritized fields even larger. This helps if you want to concentrate on one metric or have vision challenges. It also provides a very big map (or a large map plus additional data fields) and spoken turn-by-turn directions.

Compared to most cycling computers, the Ace is overkill in almost every way. However, if you want one computer with a big screen and impressive battery life, then the Ace is your ticket.

Do you wax nostalgic for the days of simple cycling computers—but still want to upload your rides and training data to Strava? Then the Omata is the computer for you. Years ago, our computers only displayed the basic riding metrics like speed, ride time, distance, and elevation. Your rides weren’t interrupted by constant pinging from notifications, messages, navigation, and target training zones.

Silca’s Omata computer is to your rides like a vinyl turntable is to listening to music. Sure, you can stream your favorite band on Spotify, see the songs’ lyrics in real time, and skip tracks you don’t like, but digital audio lacks the tactile experience of a record player. That’s what riding with the Omata is like; it features dials to display your current speed, distance traveled, elevation gain, and riding time—nothing more, nothing less.

Yet, behind the scenes, the Omata also captures critical riding data, like power, heart rate, and GPS route. This means you can still track your rides and get Strava kudos from your friends. However, the Omata comes at a steep price—$800—but it‘s an experience you will not find in any other device.

One of the biggest differences between GPS cycling computers is the technology ecosystem you’re buying into—much like choosing a smartphone. While you can pair most sensors, regardless of brand, with most computers via Bluetooth or ANT+, these devices work best when used with their corresponding companion apps. Although some budget-friendly computer options require an app to unlock full functionality, premium models from brands like Hammerhead, Wahoo, and Garmin let you perform most tasks without an app.

While bigger isn’t always better, a large, easy-to-read display—like the one on the Wahoo Elemnt Ace—makes navigation and data viewing significantly more convenient. When testing the gargantuan Elemnt Ace, Matt Phillips initially found the screen’s sheer size off-putting, but he eventually grew to appreciate it.

Navigation capabilities vary widely. Even budget models, such as the $120 iGPSport BCS300T, support turn-by-turn directions with downloadable routes. High-end models such as the Hammerhead Karoo and Garmin Edge models take it further, allowing on-the-fly route changes—such as finding a coffee shop—without adjusting settings in the companion app.

Battery life used to be a major concern, but modern bike computers are built to last. Most can handle a full week of riding without recharging. Some, like the Coros Dura and Garmin Edge 840 Solar, even feature solar charging. In fact, the Coros Dura’s battery is so robust that some users report they haven’t been able to completely drain it after months of use, making its claimed 120-hour battery life seem entirely plausible.
