Justices to consider whether noncitizens who lose asylum status may still obtain green cards

The Supreme Court on Monday will hear oral argument in Wassily v. Blanche, a case on whether immigrants whose asylum protections have been terminated remain eligible to become lawful permanent residents, also known as green card holders.

Although the case will almost certainly be overshadowed by many of this term’s other immigration disputes – including Mullin v. Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services, on the Trump administration’s suspension of access to asylum for immigrants at the U.S.-Mexico border – it may hold considerable consequences for noncitizens and bring new significance to the Trump administration’s effort to scale back asylum.

The challengers in Wassily are two men who lost their asylum protections after being convicted of what immigration officials determined to be “particularly serious crime[s].”

Tamer Wassily was granted asylum in 2000 after raising concerns about facing religious persecution in his home country of Egypt. The Department of Homeland Security moved to terminate Wassily’s asylum protections after he was convicted in 2004 of third-degree stalking and child endangerment, beginning the process of deporting him back to Egypt.

Byron Velasquez Arreaga came to the United States from Guatemala in 1995 and was granted asylum the following year. After Velasquez Arreaga was convicted of driving while intoxicated in 2015 and for aggravated driving while intoxicated in 2018, DHS indicated that it would terminate his asylum protections and move to deport him.

Amid their removal proceedings, and after their asylum protections had been terminated, both Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga applied to become lawful permanent residents. This option is available to asylum recipients who meet certain requirements, including remaining in the United States for at least a year after receiving asylum protections, under 8 U.S.C. § 1159(b).

After many years of complicated legal wrangling before immigration judges and the Board of Immigration Appeals, the men’s cases were considered together by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit. The question before that court – and the question that is now before the Supreme Court – was whether noncitizens like Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga remain eligible for adjustment of immigration status under Section 1159(b) after their asylum protections have been terminated, because the statute refers to “any alien granted asylum” but is silent as to whether applicants must currently be asylees.

In a 2-1 ruling, a 2nd Circuit panel rejected the men’s contention that “granted asylum” addresses a past decision – in which case anyone who had ever received asylum would be eligible for status adjustment under Section 11159(b) – and concluded that noncitizens whose asylum protections have been terminated are “categorically barr[ed]” from applying for adjustment to permanent legal status.

Writing for the majority, Judge Richard Wesley emphasized the use of the word “status” in the opening of Section 1159(b), which reads: “The Secretary of Homeland Security or the Attorney General ... may adjust to the status of an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence the status of any alien granted asylum.” According to Wesley, a “natural reading of ‘the status of any alien granted asylum’ is that a noncitizen must presently be granted asylum to adjust to lawful permanent resident status under 8 U.S.C. § 1159(b).”

In January, Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga asked the Supreme Court to review the 2nd Circuit’s ruling, highlighting a disagreement between the federal courts of appeals over whether the phrase “granted asylum” in Section 1159(b) “refer[s] to a completed, historical event” or “a continuing, present status.” Like the 2nd Circuit, they noted, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 4th Circuit has held that noncitizens who have lost their asylum protections “are categorically ineligible” “for adjustment to [lawful permanent resident] status under Section 1159(b).” The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit, on the other hand, has held that such immigrants remain “eligible for adjustment.”

In late June, the justices granted review.

In their brief on the merits, Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga contend that the 2nd Circuit’s decision is out of step with the “[t]ext, context, structure, history, and purpose” of Section 1159(b). In particular, they highlight several nearby passages in which lawmakers explicitly imposed “continuing conditions” that address a termination of immigration status, asserting that Congress “could have and would have” included such a condition here if it intended to unambiguously bar former asylees from applying for lawful permanent residency.

The two men also point to Section 1159(c), which grants the DHS secretary and attorney general the authority to waive portions of the statute “for humanitarian purposes, to assure family unity, or when it is otherwise in the public interest.” Holding that adjustment to lawful permanent residency is categorically unavailable to noncitizens whose asylum protections have been terminated “would take core parts of that discretion away,” the men write. Such a limitation cuts against Congress’ purpose for allowing for discretion, Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga argue. “There are many reasons why Congress would want” these officials “to retain discretion” here, they say, including that termination sometimes stems from factors “entirely outside” an asylee’s control. For example, the immigration status of the spouse and children of an asylee is often linked to the asylee’s status. If the noncitizen who was originally granted asylum loses his or her status, so does the whole family.

Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga ask the justices to recognize that, at the very least, the text of Section 1159(b) is unclear and to apply the rule of lenity, which they describe as a “well-settled and longstanding principle” under which “ambiguous provisions leading to the severe sanction of removal should be construed in favor of the noncitizen.” The Supreme Court “has repeatedly restated and reaffirmed that rule,” they conclude.

In its merits brief, the federal government counters that Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga’s interpretation of Section 1159(b) defies common sense. The text of the statute, according to U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer, describes a “transition from one status (that of an ‘alien granted asylum’) to another (that of an ‘alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence’).” If an immigrant is no longer at that starting point because his asylum status has been terminated, he can’t make the transition, Sauer asserts.

The government contends that the “statutory context” supports this interpretation, because, in nearby passages, “alien granted asylum” clearly refers to a noncitizen’s “present status.” For example, in Section 1158(c)(1), “Congress identified benefits available to an ‘alien granted asylum,’ including protection from removal and work authorization,” according to Sauer. “Those benefits undisputedly cease when asylum status is terminated.”

The government rejects the idea that barring noncitizens who have lost their asylum status from applying for a green card would interfere with the authority of the DHS secretary and attorney general to exercise discretion. Officials, Sauer writes, would remain free to intervene at an earlier point to prevent the termination of asylum status in the first place.

Sauer concludes by urging the justices to reject what he characterizes as a “last-resort appeal to lenity” – that is, Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga’s claim that Section 1159(b) is an “ambiguous provision[]” and should therefore be interpreted in their favor. He argues that the rule of lenity does not apply here, not just because the meaning of the provision is clear, but also because Section 1159(b) does not directly address deportation, which is the context in which the rule of lenity arises.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Wassily v. Blanche is expected by early July at the latest.
