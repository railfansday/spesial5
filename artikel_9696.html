How to Survive (Not Thrive) as a Salaryman

Tanggal: 2026-11-05

Fifteen rules for remaining employed, reasonably sane, and difficult to blame until the train takes you home.

The objective is not promotion. It is not fulfillment. It is not becoming indispensable.

The objective is to remain employed, reasonably sane, and difficult to blame until the train takes you home.

Arriving dramatically early creates expectations. Arriving late creates evidence.

Aim for the narrow zone in which everyone thinks, Yes, he was already here.

You do not necessarily need to work late. You need to understand who notices when you leave.

If your boss is putting on his coat, congratulations: you have suddenly remembered that you also own a coat.

A folder. A laptop. A notebook. Three pieces of paper.

A person walking quickly while carrying a document is almost universally presumed to have somewhere important to be.

The salaryman’s most efficient communication technology.

Someone explains a problem you don’t control: nod.

Someone announces an initiative that will disappear in six months: deeper nod.

Someone asks whether you understand: nod while saying, “Hai.”

Responsibility can theoretically produce promotion.

Blame can definitely produce unemployment.

When receiving an important instruction, create a polite written record of what was decided. You are not building a case against your coworkers. You are ensuring that six months from now nobody discovers that everyone remembers the meeting differently.

Being uniquely useful is also dangerous because nobody will ever let you go home.

The ideal salaryman occupies the fertile middle:

“It would be inconvenient if Tanaka weren’t here.”

A task that takes everyone three hours but takes you 45 minutes does not create 2 hours and 15 minutes of freedom.

You have discovered one of the fundamental laws of salaried employment.

You cannot defeat rain by becoming angry at clouds.

Attend. Take notes. Say one useful thing. Determine whether anything has actually been assigned to you.

The after-work drinking session is allegedly recreational and occasionally neither.

You don’t have to become the drunkest person in the izakaya. Eat. Participate. Pour drinks when appropriate. Laugh. Learn things.

Most importantly, recognize that the organizational chart becomes unusually honest after the second beer.

The train is not merely transportation. It is the membrane between Corporation You and Actual You.

Music. Manga. Games. Books. Staring into nothingness.

Do not voluntarily convert every commute into additional unpaid work.

A company capable of surviving earthquakes, recessions, executive reorganizations and forty-seven PowerPoint presentations can probably withstand your absence until Tuesday.

A family. Basketball. Gardening. Gundam models. A terrible band. Fishing. A neighborhood bar. An obsessive interest in 16th-century maps.

Anything that allows you to answer “Who are you?” without stating your employer.

Some years you acquire skills. Some years you make money. Some years you encounter an excellent boss. Some years you simply avoid the restructuring.

A 30-year career contains a surprising amount of successfully doing nothing catastrophic.

The presentation can be revised tomorrow.

If everything is always urgent, you are not experiencing urgency. You are experiencing management.

The old salaryman bargain was essentially:

Give us your loyalty and we will give you security.

Modern companies cannot always provide the second half.

If the company stops paying adequately, destroys your health, threatens your family life, or offers neither security nor opportunity, remaining loyal isn’t salaryman stoicism.
