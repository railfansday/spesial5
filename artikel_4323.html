Eating less protein could slow aging, review of 350 studies finds

Tanggal: 2026-11-11

Protein is everywhere these days. From breakfast cereals and coffee to bottled water, manufacturers are adding extra protein to an expanding range of everyday products. But while consumers are being encouraged to eat more of it, a major scientific review suggests that cutting back on protein could offer surprising benefits for health and longevity.

After examining more than 350 scientific papers, researchers found evidence that reducing protein intake may help slow biological aging, improve metabolism, and, under certain conditions, extend lifespan. The potential benefits appear to involve several processes that influence how the body uses energy, responds to nutrients, protects cells from damage, and maintains healthy cellular function.

The findings, published in the Cell Press journal Cell Press Blue, raise an important question about the growing enthusiasm for high-protein diets. Could many people be consuming far more protein than their bodies actually need?

"It's absolutely crystal clear that there are benefits of protein to muscle growth and exercise response of active individuals," says Dudley Lamming, the paper's corresponding author, of the University of Wisconsin-Madison. "But because most people are relatively sedentary, many people are likely consuming more protein than they actually need, which probably has negative health consequences."

Why Eating Less Protein Could Promote Longevity

Scientists have long understood that reducing calorie intake can extend lifespan in numerous organisms. Research has also linked calorie restriction to a lower risk of diseases associated with aging, including cancer. However, consistently eating fewer calories is challenging, making it difficult for many people to maintain such a diet over time.

An alternative approach has attracted increasing scientific interest: reducing protein without necessarily eating less food.

Experiments involving flies and rodents have demonstrated that limiting protein intake can increase lifespan even when total calorie consumption remains unchanged. This suggests that the benefits of dietary restriction may depend not only on how much food is consumed, but also on which nutrients make up that food.

Human research has produced encouraging findings as well. Several recent clinical trials have shown that people who reduce their protein intake can lose weight, decrease body fat, and improve fasting blood sugar levels, even when they consume more calories overall. Fasting blood sugar measures glucose in the bloodstream after a period without eating and is an important indicator of metabolic health.

These findings do not establish that protein restriction extends human lifespan, but they suggest that changing protein intake can influence processes associated with aging and disease.

The Debate Over How Much Protein We Really Need

The picture becomes more complicated when exercise and muscle health enter the equation.

Other studies have demonstrated that higher protein intake can support weight loss and help older adults preserve muscle mass, particularly when combined with physical activity. Maintaining muscle becomes increasingly important with age because muscle loss can contribute to weakness, reduced mobility, and a greater risk of falls.

Research highlighting these benefits helped motivate US authorities to revise dietary recommendations this year, encouraging daily protein consumption of 1.2-1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight (0.5-0.7 grams per pound). That represents nearly twice the previous recommended amount.

With protein consumption reaching new highs in the United States and older adults increasingly being advised to eat more, Lamming and a colleague examined decades of research to better understand the relationship between protein intake and aging.

Their analysis brought together findings from more than 350 papers, identifying several biological mechanisms that repeatedly appeared across studies. Together, these mechanisms suggest that lowering protein consumption may improve metabolic function, alter the way cells detect nutrients, limit cellular damage, and help preserve the processes that keep cells functioning properly.

A Hormone That May Help Explain Slower Aging

One particularly intriguing finding involves fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a hormone that becomes more abundant when dietary protein is limited.

FGF21 helps the body adjust to changes in nutrient availability and influences how energy is used. Higher levels of this hormone can increase energy expenditure, support healthier blood sugar regulation, and reduce inflammation.

These effects are especially interesting because metabolism and chronic inflammation are closely connected to aging. Over time, disruptions in these processes can contribute to conditions such as obesity, diabetes, and other age-related diseases.

Studies in mice have shown that animals with elevated FGF21 levels live longer than mice with normal levels. The lifespan benefit was particularly strong in males, suggesting that biological sex may influence how the body responds to this hormone.

Researchers have also established that eating less protein increases FGF21 levels in humans. Whether this hormonal response produces the same longevity benefits seen in mice remains an important unanswered question.

How Certain Amino Acids Could Influence Aging

The amount of protein someone eats may not be the only factor that matters. The types of amino acids that make up that protein could also play an important role.

Amino acids are the building blocks the body uses to produce proteins, which are essential for maintaining muscles, repairing tissues, and carrying out countless cellular functions. Beyond these basic roles, certain amino acids also act as signals that influence metabolism and cellular growth.

The review identifies three amino acids that appear especially important: methionine, isoleucine, and valine.

Research suggests that excessive intake of these amino acids can stimulate biological pathways that encourage growth. Although growth-related processes are essential for normal development and tissue repair, excessive or prolonged activation of certain pathways may contribute to obesity, inflammation, and other conditions associated with aging.

Limiting specific amino acids could therefore produce some of the benefits observed in protein-restricted diets. This possibility has attracted attention because it suggests that the relationship between protein and longevity may be more complicated than simply eating more or less of a single nutrient.

"These studies show that the amount of protein sedentary people are eating today may have negative health consequences, at least at the population level," Lamming says.

Why Exercise Could Change the Effects of Protein

The researchers emphasize that protein requirements are not the same for everyone.

Pregnant women, for example, need additional protein to support fetal development, while some older adults require higher amounts to help maintain muscle and physical function. People who exercise regularly may also benefit from greater protein consumption because their muscles use those nutrients to recover, repair tissue, and adapt to training.

For many adults who spend most of their day sitting, however, adding protein to everyday foods may offer little additional benefit.

Lamming points out that athletes frequently consume large amounts of protein without developing the metabolic diseases sometimes associated with protein-rich diets. One possible explanation is that physical activity changes how the body handles those nutrients.

Regular exercise stimulates muscle growth and repair, potentially allowing the body to put additional protein to productive use while reducing some of the harmful metabolic effects associated with high protein intake.

This distinction may help explain why studies sometimes reach seemingly contradictory conclusions about protein. A diet that benefits someone who exercises intensely may not be ideal for someone with a largely sedentary lifestyle.

Rather than recommending the same amount of protein for everyone, the researchers argue that dietary advice should better reflect individual circumstances, especially physical activity levels and age.

"Recent recommendations have encouraged people to eat more protein, but they've also encouraged people to exercise more," Lamming says. "We probably need to personalize protein recommendations based not just on age, but also on how physically active people are."

The findings challenge the assumption that adding more protein automatically leads to better health. Although protein remains essential, the growing body of longevity research suggests that for some people, consuming less may offer benefits that have been overlooked in the rush toward protein-rich foods.
