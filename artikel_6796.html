The AI Police Are Going After Black Writers

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

The white powers that be have always doubted our intellect. Now they have a new tool.

Picture a waifish enslaved teen—a girl named Phillis, after the slave ship that Middle Passaged her from present-day Senegal and Gambia—trembling before a room of incredulous, austere white men, the most esteemed thinkers and politicians of colonial Massachusetts. The reason: The gifted girl wrote a book manuscript, and the family that owned her journeyed all the way to London to find a publisher for it. That prospective British publisher, a bloke named Archibald Bell, promised to publish the manuscript if the Wheatleys could prove Phillis had written it herself—an attestation tantamount, of course, to whether an enslaved African soon-to-be American possessed the intellect requisite to producing exceptional creative work.

Present at the trial, which has been described by historian Henry Louis Gates Jr. as the nascent scene of African American letters, was Thomas Hutchinson, the governor of the Province of the Massachusetts Bay; Andrew Oliver, the province’s lieutenant governor; and Mr. John Hancock himself, the future first governor of the Commonwealth of Massachusetts. Per Gates, almost all the men forgathered to pass judgment on Phillis Wheatley were both Harvard graduates and slaveholders. No record exists of what transpired, but oh how brilliant Phillis must’ve been as she convinced those 18 eminent Bostonians that she’d indeed written the poems. The confirmation was printed in the front matter of Phillis’s poetry collection—the first book published by an African soon-to-be American—Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral (1773):

We whose Names are under-written, do assure the World, that the Poems specified in the following Page, were . . . written by Phillis, a young Negro Girl, who was but a few Years since, brought an uncultivated Barbarian from Africa, and has ever since been, and now is, under the Disadvantage of serving as a Slave in a Family in this Town. She has been examined by some of the best Judges, and is thought qualified to write them.

As my great-grandmother Edith Larkin Jackson, a woman not two generations removed from chattel slavery, used to say, “There’s nothing new under the sun.” Mama Eddie was a schoolteacher, and I imagine she’d be astounded by artificial intelligence. However, she’d have no trouble whatsoever fathoming a world that called into question the creativity or intellect of her people.

Here we are in this brave new world of AI, an age hurtling headlong toward the peril of superintelligence. Here we are in the 21st century, a time when the ivory-tower panjandrums are engaged in a sinister pattern, if not conspiratorial scheme, of incriminating Black and brown writers with even the suggestion that their work is AI assisted.

For example: In March, Hachette Book Group rendered kaput the U. S. publication of Mia Ballard’s Shy Girl over claims of AI usage.

When Trinidadian writer Jamir Nazir’s story “The Serpent in the Grove” was named the regional winner of the Commonwealth Prize in May, Ethan Mollick, a Wharton Business School professor who serves as the codirector of Wharton Generative AI Labs, ran it through the AI-detection software Pangram, touted as the industry standard, and reported that the platform flagged it at 100 percent. Once Mollick shared his findings on social media, several others joined the chorus bemoaning Nazir’s work as a product of AI.

In July, academics studying AI accused H. M. Wolfe of having used a large language model to write her best seller Daggermouth (though its publisher Simon & Schuster has, thus far, stood by the book). Later that month, writer Jerry Falade had a $2 million book deal with Macmillan collapse after his agents withdrew the submission of his debut crime novel citing AI concerns.

In September, Haitian Canadian writer Thélyson Orélien’s novel C’était Ça ou Mourir (It Was Either That or Die) was proclaimed a standout of the French literary season, won France’s Prix du Roman FNAC, and was long-listed for the Goncourt, the country’s most prestigious literary prize. But then an anonymous X account (later revealed to be owned by a conservative columnist) posted that Pangram had identified all 34 chapters of the book as having been written with AI. That claim spurred accusations that Orélien plagiarized earlier work, leading the Goncourt to nix the book from its long list.

In October, The Atlantic accused writer Simbarashe Kundizeza of using AI to write “The Blue Fighter,” which won the Caine Prize, Africa’s most prestigious short-story accolade.

Germane: While Nazir is Trinidadian, Ballard, Wolfe, Falade, Orélien, and Kundizeza are all Black.

Although all those writers have denied claims of using AI to produce their work, I’m not here to dispute whether they did or to what degree they used it, nor the epistemological paradox of using AI to determine whether a writer used AI to write a story. Of equal importance is that, if we ain’t reached the point where all published writing is scanned for possible use of an LLM, what’s the logic informing which writers are investigated for AI use and which aren’t, as well as the attendant issue of which accused writers suffer the most severe fallout, including the scarlet letter of damaged credibility?

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be judged in light of what’s being described as an era of postliteracy. Of the specter of AI sloptimism becoming so ubiquitous it overruns human output. Of the cataclysmic doom of superintelligence approximating the creative work of a human brain to such a degree that the difference will be negligible to the undiscerning masses.

The plight of Black and brown writers in the age of AI must be apprehended against the beef between the publishing industry writ large and AI. Against 10,000 authors having copublished an “empty” book in protest of their work being pirated to train its models. Against America’s Big Five publishers suing Meta for copyright infringement. Against the recent unsealed documents that reveal OpenAI executives knew they were training their AI on copyrighted material and, furthermore, that AI-authored books were apt to displace in the market ones authored by humans. “There will be a point where a bunch of artists express worry about what we’re doing here, and we’ll likely ignore their concerns and release anyway,” OpenAI policy director Jack Clark wrote in an internal memo back in 2020. The predicament of Black and brown writers should be weighed against the long and calculated history of portraying them as lesser intellects.

Casting doubt on the possibility of Black literary brilliance is in the spirit of a hubristic Saul Bellow asking, “Who is the Tolstoy of the Zulus?” Of 45/47 portraying beaucoup Black people as “not that bright.” Of attacking DEI to paint us as unworthy or incapable, less we are sanctioned by people in power. The same insidious forces—linked to right-wing Cambridge professor James Orr and conservative billionaire Peter Thiel—stoked the allegations that ended in Cambridge professor Jason Arday dying by suicide. (Arday has been posthumously cleared of plagiarism.)

In stark contrast, best-selling novelist and screenwriter Anthony Horowitz has admitted using AI for his work. Best-selling biographer Katie Prescott extolled AI as invaluable in writing her book on Mike Lynch, the late British tech entrepreneur and AI pioneer. And while she’s been called among other things a “grifter” and a “villain,” South African writer Coral Hart has published more than 370 books using artificial intelligence since 2025 and defended her use of LLMs despite criticism and threats. While she later clarified she used it only for research, Nobel laureate Olga Tokarczuk caused a ruckus revealing she queried AI for writing help. Other than Santiago Schnell, a white Latino, resigning as the provost of Dartmouth for using AI in his published articles and The New York Times cutting ties with Alex Preston for an AI-assisted book review, beyond vocal to tepid criticism, white writers aren’t suffering material consequences on par with those of their Black peers.

Poor Phillis Wheatley never published a second book. Despite advertising six times in the newspaper to find a publisher. Despite announcing she’d dedicate it to Benjamin Franklin. Despite General George Washington extolling her as “favored by the muses.” In fact, in 1784, Phillis died at 30 years young, destitute, having placed the last advertisement for her second manuscript just a few months prior.

Because Big Tech is big-time thriving and race is evermore an oppressive tool, critics are bound to keep casting the work of Black writers as AI assisted, in effect electing themselves nouveau versions of those privileged 18th-century Bostonians. Best believe their indictments are a tacit way to portray the work of Black people as unworthy of patronage, acclaim, awards—aka inferior. But be forewarned: If Big Tech succeeds at AI turning the creative work of human brains passé if not obsolete, it’s near foregone that most writers will lose their ability to earn a living. Indeed, the sad-ass upshot is that if AI prevails, all writers—race be damned—will be fated a Phillis.
