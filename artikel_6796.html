The 30 Thanksgiving Movies Actually Worth Watching

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

These are the best films to pop onto the TV before you fall into a food coma.

Most people don’t think of Thanksgiving when they think of holiday movies. Halloween and Christmas? of course. Valentine’s Day? You bet! But outside of John Hughes’s 1987 comedy Planes, Trains and Automobiles starring Steve Martin and John Candy, Hollywood still struggles to really capture the feeling of Thanksgiving on film.

That’s not to say that filmmakers aren’t trying. The thirty films below capture all the fall vibes, family squabbles, and strenuous meal planning that come with Thanksgiving dinner—even if some of them don’t technically take place on Thanksgiving. (Still, I tried my best to keep those at a minimum.) If you’re looking for something to watch with the whole family, start an argument, or just feel thankful that good Thanksgiving movies do exist, these are the Thanksgiving movies that are actually worth watching.

You won’t find a Thanksgiving movie list without Planes, Trains and Automobiles. The 1987 John Hughes comedy is a genuine classic—arguably so good that no one’s made a better Thanksgiving film since. How could they? The story follows a marketing executive (played by Steve Martin) who just wants to get home to New York for Thanksgiving on the busiest travel day of the year. Instead, he spends the entire three-day trip across the country with a chatty shower ring salesman (John Candy). Despite Martin’s prickliness, they’re forced to bond over the holiday while sharing the kind of heartfelt laughs that most movies nowadays just can’t replicate with the same sincerity anymore.

This is one of the movies that that you’ll see on the list that is more about fall than it is about Thanksgiving, but You’ve Got Mail does include a Thanksgiving scene. In this 1998 romantic comedy starring Tom Hanks and Meg Ryan and written and directed by Nora and Delia Ephron, two Upper West Side New Yorkers meet in an online chat room and fall in love over a series of months. At one point, Tom Hanks walks into Zabar's on Thanksgiving, which is maybe the most New York thing one can do.

Jodie Foster's finest directorial effort remains this 1995 comedy about the Thanksgiving get-together of Holly Hunter's thoroughly dysfunctional clan, which includes her mother (Anne Bancroft) and father (Charles Durning), her brother (Robert Downey Jr.), and his friend (Dylan McDermott). What ensues is the gold standard for family-gathering holiday films, full of absurdity, pathos, and ultimately a rousing sense of the ups and downs of dealing with relatives.

An ensemble cast of Vanessa Williams, Vivica A. Fox, Nia Long, and Michael Beach star in this comedy-drama film about a Black American family that gets together every Sunday to have dinner. But when Big Mama (Irma P. Hall) falls ill, her three daughters’ strained relationships take on an entirely new form. While the meals never occur on Thanksgiving Day, the family arguments over a mouth-watering meal do.

Remember the Titans isn't technically a Thanksgiving movie either. But it features plenty of those Thanksgiving elements—football, fall foliage, and large amounts of people gathering for meals. The film stars Denzel Washington as a high school football coach who is brought in to teach at a predominately white school in Alexandria, Virginia. And if it’s not playing on cable on Thanksgiving anyway—that is, if you haven’t cut the cord yet—I’m sure a streaming service picked it up for the holiday.

Maybe it's not as iconic as A Charlie Brown Christmas or It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown, but this Peanuts holiday special is a good holdover between Halloween and Christmas. This Emmy-winning classic sees Peppermint Patty infiltrating Charlie Brown's Thanksgiving holiday, which he naturally scrambles to put together with his trademark anxiety. (Where the hell are these kids' parents, by the way?)

Spike Lee's iconic She's Gotta Have It, following one woman's love square with her three competing lovers, isn't typically included in the wholesome holiday canon. But few films have featured a more memorable Thanksgiving dinner than that of Nola Darling (Tracy Camilla Johns), in which she invites over all three men to celebrate the holiday together as she sits at the head of the table.

Opening and closing with scenes of its characters at Thanksgiving dinner, Woody Allen's 1986 comedic drama—which won him an Oscar for Best Original Screenplay and Best Supporting Actor and Actress statuettes for Michael Caine and Dianne Wiest, respectively—tells a raft of interconnected stories, all in some way related to Mia Farrow's Hannah and her two siblings. Equal parts hilarious and touching, it remains one of the writer-director's crowning achievements.

It’s not the holidays if you haven’t watched a Lifetime movie with the family yet. Still, that doesn’t mean you must subject yourself to the most baseless shlock. How about a simple, magic family film from the creator of the Muppets. Well, posthumously, that is. And yes, that means puppets—but that should only be a plus!

Knives Out just screams cozy with all of its sweaters, wooden decorations, and fallen leaves. Rian Johnson's mystery flick, which is now a trilogy, is a must-watch if you're in a family that loves solving puzzles or escape rooms (or, you know, Daniel Craig). Hopefully, your Thanksgiving isn’t anywhere near as chaotic.

Okay, so, technically this would be more faithful to the Thanksgiving canon if it were Turkey Run. But this feature-film debut from the iconic Aardman animation studio is a Thanksgiving hit for obvious reasons. The stop-motion film, centered on a group of chickens determined to escape execution before being turned into chicken pies, is as hilarious as it is visually spectacular.

Christmas Vacation is in a category of its own, but the entire National Lampoon franchise is usually a crowd-pleaser at family holidays. This one features Malcolm in the Middle–era Bryan Cranston as the eccentric hippie and long-lost cousin of suburban man Mitch Snider. Awkward family antics ensue.

For the Christopher Guest and Eugene Levy fans, this 2006 mockumentary is about three actors played by Guest favorites Catherine O’Hara, Parker Posey, and Harry Shearer, who are making a film called Home for Purim, which is supposedly garnering lots of Oscar buzz. Things come to a bit of a halt when the studio intervenes and renames the film Home for Thanksgiving, changing the entire plot of the film just to chase awards.

This movie jumps around to four different Thanksgiving meals, all thrown by families from diverse backgrounds who have a few things in common: stress, food, and stressing over food. What’s Cooking? also features an ensemble cast composed of Kyra Sedgwick, Joan Chen, Lainie Kazan, Mercedes Ruehl, Maury Chaykin, Julianna Margulies, Alfre Woodard, and Dennis Haysbert.

For a little switch-up, this one is a documentary. Directed by Martin Scorsese, this film follows The Band’s Thanksgiving farewell show, featuring Joni Mitchell, Bob Dylan, and Eric Clapton. Alongside Stop Making Sense, The Last Waltz is often cited as one of the greatest music concert films ever made.

Many films in the ‘40s concerned the difficulties soldiers faced when coming home from the war, but few attempted to bring our heroes home over Thanksgiving. The Very Thought of You, which is more of a romantic drama, stars Dennis Morgan and Dane Clark as they return just for the holiday—before they’re shipped back once the weekend ends. Fair warning: This one’s a bit of a tear-jerker. “You know those advertisements that show pictures of guys in uniform coming back to what they left?,” one character says in the film. “They don’t show what home really means.”

Thanksgiving is anything but jovial in Ang Lee's The Ice Storm, which is set around the holiday in 1973 Connecticut, where two well-off families struggle with all manner of the-times-they-are-a'-changin' upheaval. Adultery and alcohol inevitably play a big factor in their problems, which are dramatized by director Lee—and handled by his cast, including Kevin Kline, Joan Allen, Sigourney Weaver, Tobey Maguire, Christina Ricci, and Elijah Wood—with chilling incisiveness.

In her finest performance, Katie Holmes plays the titular April, who bravely invites her dysfunctional family from suburban Pennsylvania to her tiny apartment on the Lower East Side. Disaster strikes early when her stove breaks and she is forced to find a working oven in her building. Meanwhile, her cancer-stricken mother (Oscar nominee Patricia Clarkson) slowly dreads the trek into the city to see her somewhat estranged daughter.

On Thanksgiving 1983, Marty (Josh Hamilton) brings his fiancée Lesly (Parker Posey) home to meet his family—his dim-witted and horny brother, Anthony; his cold and nosey mother, Mrs. Pascal; and his mentally unhinged and Kennedy-obsessed sister, Jackie-O. All hell breaks loose, naturally, as kitchen knives are hidden, sexual boundaries are tested, and Parker Posey delivers one of the most hilariously intense performances of her career.

Judd Apatow's dramatic comedy stars Adam Sandler as George Simmons, a wealthy movie star who seeks to get back to his stand-up-comedy roots after being diagnosed with leukemia. He meets a young aspiring comic (Seth Rogen) in search of a mentor, and the two tour the country as George performs his new material and reconnects with his ex-fiancée. It's a film about the comedy world and how friendships evolve into familial relationships, highlighted by a Thanksgiving toast George delivers in honor of his chosen family.

Trey Edward Shults made his directorial debut with this indie drama, which stars members of his real-life family—including his aunt Krisha Fairchild in the titular role. Krisha arrives at her sister's house for a Thanksgiving celebration after years of estrangement from her family. Overwhelmed by her return to this seemingly normal life, Krisha starts drinking and popping pills in secret—and all hell breaks loose as emotions fly and family secrets are exposed.

Much like Krisha above, The Humans is for people who really can’t stand Thanksgiving. Based on Stephen Karam’s Tony Award-winning play, the film stars a family played by Richard Jenkins, Jayne Houdyshell, Amy Schumer, Beanie Feldstein, Steven Yeun, and June Squibb as they all just tear into each other over Thanksgiving dinner.

This sequel to the big-screen adaptation of the classic TV sitcom finds the spooky-ooky Addams family once again up to their weird, gothic ways. But their family is thrown into turmoil when a new nanny, Debbie (a pitch-perfect Joan Cusack), has her sights on Uncle Fester—and plans on marrying him for his riches before killing him off. Wednesday and Pugsley know something's up, but Debbie convinces Gomez and Morticia to send them to summer camp—where they are forced to participate in a completely bonkers musical rendition of the first Thanksgiving.

Folk rocker Arlo Guthrie (son of Woody) plays himself in this offbeat comedy film inspired by his song of the same name. Guthrie, a long-haired draft dodger visiting some friends in an uptight Massachusetts town for Thanksgiving, thinks he's doing his hosts a favor by filling his Volkswagen minibus with their garbage and taking it to the dump. A mix-up ensues, Guthrie is busted for littering, and he seeks to prove himself unfit for combat when the draft comes calling.

Nobody's Fool is one of the last great headlining vehicles for Paul Newman, who stars here as an upstate New York construction-worker hustler in constant conflict with a contractor (Bruce Willis) whose wife (Melanie Griffith) he fancies. His routine is upended by the arrival of his estranged son (Dylan Walsh) around Thanksgiving, leading to an amusing (and disarming) holiday-set character study about loneliness, reconciliation, and the unexpected ways people achieve contentment.

Over the course of a Thanksgiving weekend, Chris O'Donnell's prep-school student comes of age while caring for a blind retired Army lieutenant (Al Pacino) in Martin Brest's acclaimed Scent of a Woman, which finally earned Pacino his first-ever Academy Award (for Best Actor). Amid all of its star's blustery hoo-ahing, it's a surprisingly tender tale of an unlikely friendship between two strangers.

Jack Lemmon and Walter Matthau are two bitter lifelong rivals who engage in ludicrous warfare over the affections of the new resident (Ann-Margret) of their Wabasha, Minnesota, neighborhood. That conflict includes an amusing Thanksgiving dinner, in which the two do their best to act like feuding, immature children.

Another cozy classic. Wes Anderson’s stop-motion film about a family of foxes with a killer voice cast—George Clooney, Meryl Streep, Jason Schwartzman, Bill Murray, Wilem Dafoe, Owen Wilson, Brian Cox, and Adrien Brody—couldn’t be a better film to watch with your family this holiday.

There’s never been a Thanksgiving-based superhero that I can recall—nor should there be—but the original Spider-Man film with Tobey Maguire features a particularly memorable Thanksgiving scene. Peter’s Aunt May invites the Osbornes over for Thanksgiving when Norman (Willem Dafoe) notices one of Peter’s injuries and deduces that he’s Spider-Man.

Eli Roth turned an actual joke project into a feature film in 2023 when he released Thanksgiving, a Halloween-esque slasher horror film about a killer let loose on Turkey Day. He wears a John Carver mask, carries an axe, and absolutely hates Black Friday. On the latter, we can all agree.
