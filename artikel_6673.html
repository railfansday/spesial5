12 Of Your Favorite Foods That Were Invented By Accident

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Sometimes the best things happen when everything goes wrong.

Here at Delish, we’re no strangers to a tasty creation. From our viral Marry Me Chicken to our delectable Dubai Chocolate Brownies, our talented food editors are always experimenting in the kitchen to come up with your next great dish. But not every beloved food comes from hard work and endless testing. Some of our favorites actually owe their existence to happy accidents. Here are 12 foods that we have pure dumb luck to thank for.

Named after the English county where it originated, Worcestershire sauce is the accidental brainchild of John Wheeley Lea and William Henry Perrins. In 1835, a nobleman tasked the two chemists with creating a sauce from a recipe he had brought back from Asia. The duo worked on the sauce, but their client was unenthused, so they stored it in a cellar to languish. Over a year later, they found and tried it again, noting that the aging process made it taste delicious. Two years later, they were selling it.

While ice cream cones are a staple in any ice cream shop today, they weren't always. At the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, a concessionaire named Ernest A. Hamwi sold a Middle Eastern pastry called zalabis, or zalabiyeh. Next to him, an ice cream vendor ran out of serving dishes and, thinking on his feet, Hamwi rolled up his waffle-like pastry into a cone so his neighbor could keep serving ice cream. The result? Clearly a success.

That said, Hamwi isn’t the only man credited with the ice cream cone. Just one year prior, Italo Marchiony received a patent in New York City for a mold used to make edible ice cream cups. Hamwi, however, brought them mass recognition.

While the inventor and origins of the potato chip are contested, one of the most popular stories credits a happy accident at Moon’s Lake House in Saratoga Springs, New York. A very particular customer sent back their order of fried potatoes, asking for thinner slices. Annoyed, the chef (often credited as George Crum) sliced them as thin as he could and fried them extra long—as it turned out, the customer loved them. And now we have our beloved potato chips.

Yogurt has been around for millennia, dating back to around 5,000 B.C. Many accounts credit its discovery to an accident: Milk stored in bags in warm weather was exposed to bacteria, causing it to ferment into yogurt. As it turned out, yogurt became a great way to preserve milk.

If you’ve ever been the victim of killer brain freeze due to chugging a Slurpee, this one’s for you. A man named Omar Knedlik is credited with inventing the drink after a soda fountain broke in his store. He put soda in the freezer to keep it cold, accidentally creating slushy drinks that customers loved. He later used parts from a car air conditioner to build a machine that could recreate the frozen beverages. He named the product ICEE, which 7-Eleven eventually licensed and began selling under a different name exclusively at its stores. And that’s how we got our Slurpee.

Nashville hot chicken has one of the best origin stories of any food. In the 1930s, a man named Thornton Prince was known as quite the ladies’ man. One of the women he was seeing decided to seek revenge after Prince had been out all night. For payback, she loaded his favorite fried chicken recipe with every spicy ingredient she could find in her kitchen. Lo and behold, her plan backfired, as Prince loved the result. He went on to open a fried chicken shop with his brothers, and the rest is history.

You have a forgetful 11-year-old to thank for Popsicles. Back in 1905, Frank Epperson accidentally left a glass of water with powdered soda mix and a stirring stick outside overnight. Come morning, the mixture was frozen, but he popped it out of the glass and enjoyed his creation. He began making these ice pops for his friends, and in 1924, he filed a patent. Up until then, he was calling them Eppsicles, but his children changed the name to Pop’s ‘sicles, and the name stuck.

We have a sanitarium to thank for our favorite breakfast cereal, if you can believe it. Dr. John Harvey Kellogg worked alongside his brother Will at Battle Creek Sanitarium, where he accidentally created what is now known as corn flakes. Kellogg was trying to make a wheat-based dough for his patients. After leaving the dough out overnight by accident, he put it through rollers and found that it produced thin flakes, which the patients ended up loving. He later substituted corn and added sugar to commercialize the product.

Raisins date all the way back to around 2000 B.C. Historians believe ancient Persians and Egyptians discovered grapes that had dried on the vine and realized they could eat them. Wall paintings from that time period show dried fruit being both eaten and used as decor.

While the exact origins of pink lemonade are contested, two popular theories trace its creation back to the circus. In the first story, a teenage circus worker invented the drink after accidentally dropping red cinnamon candies into a vat of lemonade. Rather than toss the batch, he went with the flow and sold the now-pink lemonade.

The other story is a bit more off-putting. One circus worker reportedly ran out of water for the lemonade and used a bucket of dirty water that a performer had just used to wash her pink tights instead. He then sold the resulting beverage as pink lemonade. I really hope the first story is the accurate one.

It’s hard to believe that cheese puffs were initially created for animals, not humans. Animal food manufacturer Flakall Corporation created the cheesy snack we love today in 1935. To make animal feed, the company used a machine that ground corn. During the cleaning process, introducing moist corn into the machine produced airy corn puffs. An employee brought some home and experimented with seasoning them to create a product for humans.

The result was called Korn Kurls, and the company began producing them. The snack became so successful that the company changed its name to help distance itself from its humble beginnings in animal feed.

Now a beloved staple on many a Tex-Mex menu, the chimichanga is younger than you might think. The most common legend credits El Charro Café founder Monica Flin with inventing the fried dish. Apparently, she accidentally dropped a burrito into a deep fryer and started swearing at the mistake. Because children were present, she quickly switched gears and shouted “chimichanga”—which translates to “thingamajig”—instead. The rest, as they say, is history.
