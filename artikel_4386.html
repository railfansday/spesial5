6 JRPGs to Play If You Loved Final Fantasy Resonance

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

Murillo Zerbinatto is a JRPG enthusiast, writer, and editor. He has played over 300 RPGs during his gaming career, with 90% of them being JRPGs.

Final Fantasy Resonance is here, and it is already earning high praise from critics and players alike. Boasting an overwhelmingly positive score on Steam, the JRPG has been showered with acclaim for its combat systems, its compelling cast, and its triumphant return to Final Fantasy roots, serving as a love letter to fans of the franchise's 90s golden era.

If you cherished everything Rain and Lasswell's title brought to the table and want more, I have put together a list of other JRPGs to dive into if you enjoyed Final Fantasy Resonance. We have titles featuring modular Job systems, gorgeous pixel art, and secret-packed worlds that will extend your playtime far beyond the main story. It has never been a better time to be a JRPG fan.

Final Fantasy Resonance traces its lineage back to Final Fantasy Brave Exvius, and like many mobile gacha projects, it wears its mainline references and influences proudly. You know who else excels at wearing their JRPG influences on their sleeve? Indie developers. And speaking of phenomenal indie JRPGs, Chained Echoes is a masterclass. If you haven't played it yet, fix that immediately.

First off, Chained Echoes doesn't feature a traditional class system, so if that is your primary draw, the other entries on this list might suit you better. However, if you are looking for stunning pixel art, a gripping narrative, lovable characters, and a nostalgic trip down memory lane, whether through familiar tropes of yesteryear or simply the authentic feel of a 16-bit classic, you owe it to yourself to try Chained Echoes. Its turn-based combat loop is iconic, progression feels clean yet engaging, and its world exploration stands among the finest in the genre. It is thoroughly worth your time.

Plenty of hidden gems in the mobile world deserve a second chance.

Live A Live is delightfully unorthodox, and neither its combat layout nor its narrative structure shares much common ground with Final Fantasy Resonance. Their primary shared trait is the HD-2D visual engine. You might ask why I am recommending it here, and the answer comes down to character focus. Final Fantasy Resonance shines through its incredible cast, successfully translating gacha origins into a complete offline adventure by turning side characters into quest givers and granting them a well-deserved spotlight.

This often-overlooked SNES classic is entirely character-driven. At the start of the journey, you select from several distinct scenarios, each featuring its own protagonist, narrative arc, and setting. The structure feels episodic, but everything weaves together brilliantly by the end. Final Fantasy Resonance places that same heavy emphasis on its cast, so if character development was your favorite aspect, Live A Live will deliver in spades.

Fantasian might be one of the most underrated JRPGs on the market. Developed by Mistwalker under the direction of Hironobu Sakaguchi, the creator of Final Fantasy, Fantasian deserves far more mainstream recognition. Granted, the game originally debuted on a limited platform with Apple Arcade (where I first played it), but the release of Fantasian Neo Dimension brought it to consoles and PC, and you should definitely give it a shot.

Comparatively, the main parallel it shares with Final Fantasy Resonance is its turn-based foundation, though even the combat has its own distinct flavor. Fantasian introduces a unique trajectory targeting system where abilities feature varied area-of-effect paths. Each character follows their own skill tree, and while offering roughly three distinct build directions per party member, it lacks the deep modular customization of Resonance. Still, that comforting sense of playing a classic JRPG from a bygone era, bringing warmth to your heart as you revisit retro design sensibilities, is undisputed. If you want pure nostalgia, Fantasian is the way to go.

One of my favorite parts of watching new players jump into Final Fantasy Resonance was seeing them unlock fresh Visions and map out synergy builds for their party. I could practically see the gears turning as they calculated which abilities would mesh best with each character. If that customization loop was your favorite part of the experience, I highly recommend picking up Bravely Default.

Any entry in the series will do, but both Bravely Default II and Bravely Default Flying Fairy HD Remaster will scratch that build-crafting itch (I personally find the second half of the original game a bit tedious, hence my primary recommendation for Bravely Default II). The Job system functions almost identically to Visions in Final Fantasy Resonance: the more you level up your Jobs, the wider your build options and combat potential become. Who knows, you might just discover your next favorite Bartz-style combo in Bravely Default.

If Final Fantasy Resonance left you feeling nostalgic, it is only fitting to revisit the pinnacle of pixel remaster releases. This is doubly true if you have yet to play Final Fantasy VI, as you will recognize countless references while discovering why so many fans rank the sixth entry as the franchise's absolute peak. Final Fantasy VI is simultaneously accessible and deeply complex across all of its design layers.

The combat uses the classic Active Time Battle system, but each party member has unique innate strengths that can be further customized through Espers. The story avoids focusing on a single protagonist, instead distributing screen time across an ensemble cast to build a living, captivating world. During the game's second half, the path forward is left entirely in your hands. Ultimately, you will get to experience one of the true masterpieces of the JRPG genre, or reconnect with it if you have not checked out the Pixel Remaster edition yet.

Final Fantasy Resonance proves that classic Final Fantasy ideas still work, from turn-based combat and jobs to overworld exploration and dungeons.

The most obvious choice on this list is Octopath Traveler II. While I still suggest playing the original first to avoid getting too comfortable with the sequel's refinements, nothing stops you from diving straight into the second title. Octopath Traveler II takes the foundation established by Square Enix's inaugural HD-2D release and elevates every aspect, from storytelling and combat mechanics to visual polish.

Octopath Traveler II previously held the highest review score for an HD-2D title before Final Fantasy Resonance arrived, which speaks volumes about both games' quality. Both titles offer unmatched character customization, with the Visions in Final Fantasy Resonance mirroring the flexibility, dynamism, and build variety found in Octopath Traveler II's Job system. It offers more HD-2D charm, more heart, and endless fun
