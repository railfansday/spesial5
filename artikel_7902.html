The Safest Way to Shovel Snow, According to Experts

Tanggal: 2026-11-21

Avoid injury with these expert-backed snow shoveling safety tips.

With autumn in full swing, it’s time to start thinking about preparing for the looming winter months. If you live in a snow-prone climate, this means getting out the shovels, salt and snowblower before the first flakes fly. Even if you’re not new to shoveling, it’s worth learning how to do it safely, as injuries can occur at any time.

Shoveling may seem simple, but can lead to strains, pulls and cardiac events if not done properly. Ahead, learn expert-backed tips to shovel snow the right (and safe) way with insights from cardiologist Dr. Bradley Serwer.

The safest way to shovel snow is to push it as much as possible and avoid lifting. If you must lift snow to remove it, bend at the knees and lift with your legs instead of your back.

“The most important tool when preparing to shovel snow is good common sense,” Serwer says. “Dress appropriately and consider the risks of the job at hand.”

Dress in layers that allow you to remove or open up a layer of clothing as you warm up to avoid getting overheated. Opt for a moisture wicking base layer, a mid-weight layer and a jacket that is wind-resistant and waterproof. Wear warm, waterproof gloves, a hat, warm socks and thick-soled winter boots. For particularly cold temperatures and high winds, add ski goggles and a balaclava to protect sensitive facial skin.

Consider a quick warm up and stretch session for a safer shoveling session. Get your blood pumping with some light activity like jumping jacks, walking in place or climbing up and down the stairs in your home.  Gently stretch major muscle groups before heading outside.

Snow shovels are not all created equal. For safer shoveling, choose one that is less demanding on your back, joints and heart. Ergonomic shovels feature a curved handle that reduces back strain when lifting. Snow plow shovels are meant to push snow only, not lift it. And battery-powered shovels act like mini-snowblowers to quickly remove snow without any manual input.

Keep your back straight, bend at the knees and use your legs to lift snow for safer shoveling. Keep the loaded shovel close to your body, then, without twisting, turn your body toward the direction you place the snow. If snow is wet or deeper than six inches, remove it in smaller layers, rather than one heavy shovelful.

“It is often underestimated what kind of physical workload is required to shovel a substantial amount of snow,” Serwer says. That’s why it’s important to pace yourself, stop regularly and plan ahead to avoid overexertion. Opt for a slow and steady pace, especially if you’re typically sedentary. Rigorous snow shoveling will quickly lead to overexertion and cardiac strain. Going slow allows you to focus on safe shoveling technique, preventing possible muscle strains and pulls. Stay well-hydrated when shoveling and take regular breaks to allow your heart rate to return to normal and your muscles to recover.

Stop shoveling if you’re experiencing cardiac distress, cold-related conditions, dehydration or back strain.

“Shoveling snow can be a very strenuous activity, placing notable strain on the cardiovascular system,” Serwer says. Know the signs of a heart attack before picking up a shovel, like chest pressure, shortness of breath, nausea and radiating arm pain. Stop immediately if you experience these symptoms and seek medical help. “Hire someone else to shovel, especially if you are at a high cardiac risk,” Serwer says.

Dress in layers and cover exposed skin in freezing temperatures and wind. Even with these efforts, frostbite and hypothermia are real risks. Frostbite begins in extremities like fingers and toes, and exposed facial skin. Early signs include pale, cold skin and prickling or numb sensations. Hypothermia happens when core body temperatures drop too low and begins with clumsiness, foggy thinking, fatigue and intense shivering. Stop immediately and go inside to warm up if you experience these symptoms.

“It is very easy to become dehydrated while working in cold climates,” Serwer says, as the human body suppresses the thirst response and dry winter air pulls additional moisture from our bodies. Notice signs of dehydration like fatigue, dry throat, cracked lips or a headache. “Dehydration is often overlooked, and people don’t replace the fluids as needed,” Serwer adds. Stop and take regular water breaks and continue drinking fluids for the remainder of the day if you notice these signs.

Shoveling puts strain on your spine and back, and can lead to serious, long term injuries like herniated disks, sciatica and lower back strains if you’re not careful. Beyond always lifting with your legs, stop shoveling if you feel the early signs of spine and back strain. These include a dull, aching in your lower back, sharp pain when bending or twisting, muscle cramping or shooting pains.

It is safer to push snow rather than lift it.

Individuals with heart disease, obesity, diabetes and high blood pressure should not shovel snow. Those over 45 years old should also exercise caution.
