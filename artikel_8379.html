Causing a Scene: North Meets South on the AT

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Even though the Franklin free hiker shuttle isn’t until late morning, all of us at the Rock Gap shelter wake up early. We only have 3.7 miles to hike, but are feeling the eagerness of Town Energy. Ahead of the pack, Show and I descend the final steps to Winding Stair Gap at little after 8:30AM. There’s nothing to do but wait, for the next couple hours. Unless…

“What the hell,” I say, walking away from the woods towards the highway shoulder. “It doesn’t hurt to try. What else are we gonna do for two hours?”

As an approaching vehicle appears, rounding the corner in the distance, I stick out my thumb and spread a calculated I-want-something-from-you smile on my face that will hopefully be perceived instead as genuine friendliness. The car speeds past, making no efforts to slow down. Its windy wake rustles my hair across my cheek.

Usually I hate hitch-hiking. The stakes of needing to get somewhere and not knowing if or when you actually will get there feel too high to my control-freak brain. But in this scenario, with the fail-proof backup plan of the town’s shuttle coming, eventually, for certain, I’m willing to test my luck.

I drop my pack on the ground in front of me, so I don’t have to keep standing there with it on, and so cars can more easily identify me as a thru-hiker. Show walks over to join in, standing behind me so I’ll be the first person in view. The age-old trick of using the female hiker as bait.

I’m not convinced this actually works. I’ve been in many a scenario where a single guy or two men have gotten rides quicker than Show and I have. And yes, I have tried various womanly wiles such as letting my hair down when it was long and standing there in a sports bra. Neither has definitively provided a quicker ride. We do it like this anyway, just in case we happen to get a driver who perceives picking up a woman as less threatening. Or believes me to be a “damsel in distress” and wants to come to my unnecessary rescue.

Ten minutes later we’re still standing there. Raising our thumbs and curling our lips every time a vehicle appears, dropping them immediately as it blows past us. I’m not discouraged, though. We’d expected to be waiting here anyway.

As a promising pickup truck rushes by, and I swear, may have even sped up at seeing us, Rufio, Cous Cous, and Noah pop out of the woods at the trailhead. They walk over to join in on the hitching, the five of us standing in a line with our thumbs out. Now we really need a pickup to fit everyone.

A few minutes later, a silver truck approaches from the opposite direction. We don’t bother to display our thumbs since it’s not heading the way we’re trying to go. To our surprise, it begins slowing down and pulls into the parking lot. Are they here…for us? That awkward moment of trying to decipher whether you should be presumptuous and start walking over towards the vehicle, or if the person is coincidentally arriving to go for a hike.

Fortunately this driver makes it obvious by pulling up to where we’re standing and rolling his window down. A gentleman in his 60s or 70s pokes his head out and says, “When I first drove by there were only two of you. Then I turn around and there’s five! If someone’s willing to ride in the bed I can take everyone.”

We quickly decide amongst ourselves that Cous Cous is going to take the bed. He’s the greenest of all of us and has never ridden in the back of a pickup truck before. Let’s get this kid some life experience! We load up the bed with all of our packs and Cous Cous climbs in next to them. Show, Noah, and I take the back seat. Rufio rides shotgun.

As we drive past vistas of evergreen mountain views towards Franklin, the driver shares with us that God wanted him to pick us up. He further explains that he’s always on the lookout for ways to provide random acts of kindness on His Holy’s behalf. Seeing us hitching was today’s “God shot.” Hey man, whatever got you to pull over, I’m here for it.

Observing the driver and Rufio sitting next to each other, a devout Christian and a hippie ex-con, strikes me as humorous. An unlikely duo to be en route together. But with Rufio addressing him politely as “sir” in his slight Texan drawl, it kind of works anyhow. It’s a beautiful thing that the trail brings so many folks together who ordinarily wouldn’t have anything to do with each other.

When the man drops us off in town he insists on a prayer circle before departing. We kind of owe him one so no one objects. We gather round behind the tailgate and bow our heads. He thanks God for providing an opportunity to help us and asks Him to bless our journey ahead. We close with an Amen, thank the man, and head towards the local diner for breakfast.

The small town vibe in the Kountry Kitchen was palpable. It’s the kind of joint where the elderly townies gather at 6AM open for a cup of Joe seven days a week. They banter with the waitresses and everyone knows each other by name. The sort of place where your local-ad-decorated mug is never fully empty because a kind woman always seems to appear with a carafe at your side by the time you’re running half full. “More coffee, hon?”

We get seated at a large table towards the back, segregating us and our smell from the other patrons. A reasonable choice by the wait staff. On the wall above the table is a poster that reads “All I need is a little bit of coffee and a whole lot of Jesus.” It makes me laugh to myself because I’ve seen it before in an entirely different context.

There’s these hilarious gay husbands who have made an Instagram Reel series out of going into Homegoods stores and reading out signs such as these to each other in an exaggerated, sassy manner. You can’t help but smile at the dramatics and the way they crack each other up. This exact sign made an appearance in one of the videos, so I can’t take it seriously. Here, down south, though? These people didn’t hang that up ironically. They’re really that big on Jesus.

Another thing they’re apparently big on down south? Eavesdropping. I can’t remember what exactly Rufio was doing or saying, but it doesn’t really matter. He was talking loudly (likely with some cuss words thrown in), and just generally being his boisterous self. Noah, a born and raised southern boy, became visibly uncomfortable with this occurring in the restaurant. “Hey,” he said to Rufio, “just keep it down in here, we don’t want to cause a scene. People down here love to eavesdrop and they’re definitely going to be judging us if we’re acting out.”

“Bruh, I AM the scene!” Rufio responded unapologetically, but he turned it down a notch, as best as he personally could.

“Yo!” Show exclaimed. “Now that should be your trail name. The Scene!” We all agreed it would be funny to have both “The Show” and “The Scene,” an inevitably dynamic duo, in the group. But alas, he’d already fully embraced the name Rufio and was unwilling to change it. We did, however, occasionally refer to him as “The Scene” going forward as a sort of secondary trail name since it was so fitting to his outspoken and flamboyant nature.

I found this interaction to be an interesting example of how different the social culture is in the southern versus northern US. Going off what I learned from Noah, it seems southerners have a tendency towards listening-in, gossiping, and generally being all up in each others’ business. At least in small towns such as this one. As a lifelong northerner, in my experience these behaviors are not non-existent, but much less common. Up north we tend to keep to ourselves more, mind our own business.

We also may have different definitions of and tolerances for “causing a scene.” Personally, if I were to go into a casual restaurant in New England and someone was being a little loud and/or swearing in public, I wouldn’t think much of it. Unless of course it was something that was continuous for a long period of time, that’s obnoxious anywhere. But I wouldn’t bat an eye at a brief raised voice or an expletive shouted out here and there. I also wouldn’t think to intentionally listen-in on a conversation happening at the next table, nor consider that others in my surroundings are purposefully trying to listen-in on mine. And even if they are, why the hell would I care? It’s just not a thing here.

If you’re from either the north or the south in the US, weigh in! Do you agree with these differences in social norms around public politeness and business-minding (or lack thereof)? Let me know your experiences in the comments!

I recognize that may be a jarring headline for all of my southern readers. “Forgive my northern attitude,” as Noah Kahan would say. Promise I’m not being a deliberately crass northerner for titling this section that. Believe it or not, Ballsack is actually a person I want to tell y’all about.

If you follow a lot of thru-hiking content, you likely know that in recent years there are have been a number of Calendar Year Triple Crowners out on these trails. In case that title itself isn’t self-explanatory, these are folks who attempt to hike all three Triple Crown trails in one calendar year. Some even do what’s called a “Border to Border” Calendar Year Triple Crown (B2BCYTC) by extending the AT in both directions to touch the Maine/Canadian border and the southernmost tip of the US in Key West, Florida.

In 2025, we had Peg Leg and Punisher. This year, among the B2BCYTC-ers out there was (well, still is, as far as I know, since it is November at the time of this writing) a woman by the trail name of Ballsack.

I’ve been following Ballsack on Instagram for a number of years now, ever since I discovered her account while she was posting about her first AT thru, which I believe was back circa 2021. A woman named Ballsack? Immediately I was amused and intrigued. I came for the name, but stayed for her delightfully silly personality and raw, honest content.

Not only was she posting photos from her various thru-hikes, but sharing a lot of writing about them. Anyone who posts well-written, long-winded captions on Instagram captures my attention, and she was no exception. I noticed one thing in particular about her content creation that she was doing differently than most others in the thru-hiking community. She was posting after as opposed to during.

Ballsack has been highly unapologetic and forthcoming about this choice of hers to do so. I regularly saw posts explaining her desire to live in the moment during the hike, and then separately process her experiences in writing, in hindsight, later on. If this idea sounds familiar to my style of trail content creation, well, this is the source.

On the CDT, when I was struggling to come to terms with the fact that blogging and hiking simultaneously wasn’t working for me, I drew on Ballsack’s example. I knew instinctively that this way was the answer, but was afraid if I posted after the fact that my audience would care less about my content because it wasn’t being presented in real time. As if time would make it obsolete. Why would anyone want to read about last year’s hike when other people are on to posting about this year’s?

Witnessing someone else doing it and maintaining a decently large following on socials made me realize that it can be done. People still care. They’re willing to wait for quality content if they like it enough, regardless of a timing gap between the happening of the thing and the recording of it. Following Ballsack’s example, I was finally able to grant myself permission to do things my way instead of how I “should” be doing it. I have no regrets. The blog is better for it, the hikes have been better for it, and I’m better for it.

Huge shout out to Ballsack—if you happen to read this, although I’m sure you won’t, because you’re a little busy right now hiking 10,000 miles!!—you’ve been a huge inspiration to me and my trail writing! Thank you for normalizing hindsight content so I could feel confident doing it too.

Blogging my CDT hike from home. I may not be able to walk and blog at the same time figuratively, but hindsight content allows me to do it LITERALLY.

While Ballsack and I follow each other and had interacted once or twice on Instagram, we’d never crossed paths in real life. Until, with no prior warning, the door to Kountry Kitchen opened and she walked in.

I’d of course known she was a couple months into her B2BCYTC by now. I figured there was a good chance she’d pass us at some point on the AT this year. But in my own busyness with pre-trail preparations I’d fallen off with keeping up with any updates she was posting online. The last I’d known she was still on the Florida Trail. So even if she’d finished that, she still had everything between its northern terminus and Springer to complete. It could be a bit until she caught up to us. Wow, she’s already here? She’s cruising along! I remember thinking. (As it turned out, she had skipped ahead to start the AT and would return for the mileage gap later on.)

Having finished our breakfast, I got up to pay at the register. After checking out, I turned to walk back to our table and saw Ballsack standing next to it, chatting with our gang. As I approached, I exclaimed in excitement, “Omg it’s Ballsack!” saying the word far too loud for the southernness of the space. I’m sure any eavesdropping old ladies brunching after Bible study clutched their pearls and Noah internally cringed.

Ballsack turned to greet me. I could see searching recognition on her face. I knew she knew she knew me but couldn’t place me. “I’m Toddler Snacks,” I let her off the hook, “we follow each other on Instagram.” She nodded as it clicked and we expressed our IRL nice to meet yous.

“I’m Cous Cous,” Cous Cous said when it was his turn.

“But what’s your full name…?” I prompted, goading him playfully.

“Ughhhh,” he groaned in angst, as we all giggled. “It’s Cous Cous Sunset.” He rolled his eyes, his disapproval evident in the emphasis of “sunset.” He hadn’t fully accepted the full name yet at this point.

I don’t think Ballsack knew what to make of this with no context. After a few more minutes of chatting she said she had to get going to do some town chores, but would see us later at the hostel, where we were all staying that night.  The rest of us got up to do the same and we parted ways.

Our first errand in downtown Franklin was stopping by Outdoor 76, a famous outdoor outfitter that caters towards AT thru-hikers. It’s a place many novice NOBOs rely on to swap out gear that hasn’t worked out after its 110 mile field test, myself included. In 2020, I remember purchasing a new sleeping pad here. The one I’d started out with, and confidently elected not to take on any shakedowns beforehand, turned out to be far more uncomfortable than my theoretical imaginings of sleeping soundly on it for 2200 miles.

Shoes are another major gear item NOBOs buy here. Just over 100 miles in, hikers often assess their foot pain status and decide whether their pre-chosen footwear is worth giving more time or switching up. The Outdoor 76 staff seem to be particularly knowledgeable in this department. I remember witnessing my 2020 tramily member, Spreadsheet, getting the full run down from one of the employees.

The gist of his shoe spiel that doubled as a pep talk was that by nature of hiking over 2,000 miles, we classify as “elite athletes” (his words) and we ought to look at ourselves that way. He stressed both wearing the correct footwear, and perhaps even more importantly, keeping our mileage down in the beginning, to prioritize recovery and prevent overuse injuries. I recall him specifically suggesting that our daily mileage not exceed 10 miles yet, which seems like a rather low number to many thru-hikers, especially the younger generations.

Having the experience I do now, I agree with this. Not the ten mile limit in particular. I think that should be determined on an individual basis according to your fitness foundation going in, thru-hiking experience level, pack weight, age, and other personal factors, as necessary. But I agree with the general sentiment of starting SLOW; more so than you think you need to. You can always speed things up later on once you have trail legs. I’ve seen enough people burn themselves out in the first few hundred miles to know that slow is the way to go at the start of a thru.

This time around, in 2026, I was dialed in on my starting kit, so there wasn’t anything that I needed to replace this early on. As I browsed the store for shits and giggles, something caught my eye. Are those…dino nugs??

I looked longingly at the dino nugs pair thinking, ugh, I love these. They literally have toddler snacks on them… But I didn’t think I could justify carrying another pair of shorts in my pack. With sadness I left them behind. Show and I signed our names on the banner beneath the register and walked out of the store towards the hostel.

The hostel was different from what I remembered of it on my 2020 hike. It had changed ownership, names (slightly), and layout. Six years ago it was called “The Gooder Grove” and was run in the upstairs portion of the single-family house by an eccentric man by the name of Zen, who seemed nice enough but had gone down one too many rabbit holes of conspiracy theories. Now, the hostel resided in the basement of the house, was being hosted by a hospitable former thru-hiker couple named Willow and Bluebs, and had been renamed simply “The Grove.”

After dropping off our packs, gathering our laundry for the staff to wash, and taking showers, Show, Noah, and I headed back out for our resupply run at the Ingles. We wandered down the streets of Franklin in assorted loaner clothes and ended up getting a bit lost trying to figure out how to get around a widespread construction zone that the grocery store was on the other side of. Lucky for us, Bluebs spontaneously pulled up alongside us in his SUV. He informed us we were going a roundabout way and offered to drop us off at Ingles. We graciously accepted the ride.

At Ingles we ran into Sauce, Bill, and Cous Cous. Once done with our shopping, all of us besides Cous Cous (who was still inside) loitered outside the store next to the “No Loitering” sign, waiting for him so we could all walk back to the hostel together. The topic of Cous Cous’ broke nineteen-year-old status came up. We were all in agreement that, as older and more financially stable adults, we wanted to help him out here and there. We’d all heard him express his money woes and concerns over whether or not he could feasibly finish the trail on a modest $1,500 budget.

“I’m going to run back in and swipe my card for his resupply,” Sauce announced, and walked back towards the doors.

“Good one!” I said. “Let’s get this kid to Maine!”

A few moments later, the two of them emerged together, Sauce looking triumphant and Cous Cous irritable. “I don’t like when you guys do that!” he protested futilely. We paid no mind to his disapproval. I’d already bought his breakfast that morning, which he didn’t like either. It seemed he was quite uncomfortable accepting what he perceived as handouts. From what little I knew of his history, he’d been financially independent from a young age and worked hard for everything he had.

I want to mention that I hesitantly included this last anecdote. I neither want to sing my own praises, nor present Cous Cous as merely “the kid” or a charity case. Cous Cous was an integral member of the group. Speaking for myself, I enjoyed witnessing his genuine care for others, intellect and depth (especially as a rather young person), and his growing self-confidence as a backpacker. Sure, we all liked to joke around, bust his balls, and mildly embarrass him occasionally in an older sibling way.

But the truth of the matter is, and I believe I can speak for the whole group when I say this, we liked having him around and viewed him as an equal. And thus, all of us shared the sentiment that we wanted to share our resources with him. When it’s no setback to you personally, why wouldn’t you want to help someone else out that you care about? Hopefully he’d get used to the help. Because it was going to keep happening.

Later, back at the hostel, we repackaged our resupplies and organized our freshly laundered belongings for the next day. It was a relaxing evening of preparation and socialization. Show even got some doggo snuggles in with Willow and Bluebs’ good boy.

I can’t remember his name – if you know, comment it down below!

We hung out with the other hikers, including Ballsack who showed up later on. I enjoyed talking shop with her about thru-hiking and discovering, as two New Englanders, we both shared the same loving appreciation for the Appalachian Trail. Both of us feel a sense of protectiveness bordering on defensiveness towards it. It’s our attempt to dispel the narrative that the AT is nothing but a more miserable and/or less beautiful experience than other trails that Triple Crown hopefuls bemoan having to “get through” to get their title. This is wholly untrue in my opinion, and it’s nice to have an ally in this belief.

She asked me what I thought of the CDT. At this point that was the one Triple Crown trail she had yet to do any of, as far as I know. I remember responding that it was beautiful but “a grind.” Which was my truth, as I experienced it. However, Show pointed out to me later on that this may have been in poor taste to say to someone who was attempting to hike over 10,000 miles in one year. Now THAT’S a freaking grind. My apologies to Ballsack if this came across as tone-deaf or discouraging. I’m still thinking about it eight months later.

In the morning Show and I got on one of the first shuttles back to Winding Stair Gap. We rode with Ballsack and a few others, I have no recollection who. Ballsack mentioned she was attempting to hike all the way to the NOC (Nantahala Outdoor Center) that day. It would be a little over 27 mile day for her, a distance we planned to cover in two full days and a nero, for perspective.

We figured she’d be long gone as soon as she entered the woods ahead of us, and told her as much when we unexpectedly bumped into her one more time at a stream crossing. She explained that she likes to take it slow and steady, hiking for more hours of the day rather than a fast pace to get all the miles in. Definitely a strategy I could get behind. It’s probably smarter not to push yourself too hard when you’ve got to do this nearly every day for an entire year.

Bear scat seen just north of Winding Stair Gap. No source teddies were seen.

Around lunchtime Show and I reached Wayah Bald, a popular day hike vista with a stone observation tower you can ascend to take in the 360 degree views of Nantahala National Forest. On my 2020 thru-hike this viewpoint was socked-in; I recall hiding out in the tower to escape the wind and mist while eating lunch. Today, there was some cloud cover and general moodiness in the skies, but the layered mountain views were on full display. I was grateful to again (as on Blood Mountain) get to see views I missed last time. Two for two so far on the view re-dos!

On account of it being early afternoon and our bodies feeling good, Show and I decided to push past our initial plan of staying at the Wayah Shelter. I texted the Dump Truckers group chat letting Leigh, Noah, and Cous Cous know our change of plans since we’d agreed to meet up there that night. While we were at The Grove in Franklin, Leigh had stayed somewhere else with family. We’d left Noah behind a few hours ago as he was waiting at a road crossing for a friend who was coming out to hike with him for a night. Cous Cous I believe had gotten on a later shuttle that morning and hadn’t yet caught up to us.

As we continued hiking to our new home for the night, the Cold Spring Shelter, we suddenly began to see an increasing amount of burned forest floor alongside the trail. Soon, we were in a charred wasteland, blackened leaf duff and singed rhododendrons all around us. Only the tree stumps and the smooth dirt trail had remained unaffected. What the heck?

Burn zones aren’t an uncommon thing to see on a thru-hike out west. I’ve walked through many before on both the PCT and CDT. They are, however, much rarer to see on the AT, and east coast in general. Due to the wetter, more humid climate, there are simply less forest fires over here.

But this didn’t look like the aftermath of a rampant wildfire based on my experience. Typically wildfires leave behind massive swaths of inches-deep dusty ash mixed with chunks of charcoal, as well as blackened dead trees, some still standing and many as downed logs. Here the forest floor was too neatly charred at surface level, as though someone had walked through with a blow torch. And the trees had been too conveniently spared, all upright, appearing alive and well. A controlled burn perhaps?

Downed logs in an old wildfire burn scar in the Bob Marshall Wilderness in Montana on the CDT in 2025.

Charred trees in a Northern California burn scar on the PCT in 2023.

Not long later we reached a sign that confirmed my suspicions, warning hikers that the area beyond was closed for a prescribed burn on March 6th. I checked my watch, knowing we were right around that date. Shit, hopefully it’s not the 6th, I thought.

“Today is…the 7th. Ok good. So they did this yesterday. Wow, good thing our schedule just so happened to put us here today. Had no idea this was even happening,” I said to Show.

“Yeah,” he agreed, “that would have sucked if we got all the way out here and had to turn around…”

We pressed on past the sign further into the burn zone and began to smell campfire smoke. From a ledge where there was a break in the trees we could see a large plume of ashen gray smoke coming from the woods at the bottom of the cliff. “Guess they’re working on that area down there today,” I observed.

Further down the trail we came upon a jumble of blackened logs that was emitting small wisps of white smoke. There were no flames, but everything else we’d seen up to this point had been fully extinguished. This was, albeit minimally, still actively burning.

Uhhh, is this supposed to be smoking still?” I halted and looked back at Show.

“Well, seeing as we know it’s a prescribed burn… I’m sure it’s fine to just… leave it alone?” I reasoned.

Had we found this smoking log just in and of itself, outside of that context, I probably would have felt more compelled to intervene in some way. I was further comforted in this choice not to a half mile or so later when we passed a couple members of a wildland fire crew. Cool, I thought, so they are still out here monitoring this situation. That’s good to know.

After a solid 15.8 miles of hiking, five of those extra and unplanned, Show and I were the first to arrive at the Cold Spring Shelter. It was a smaller than average three-walled log cabin lean-to with a metal roof. We’d seen a chance of overnight rain in the forecast so we were thankful there was still shelter space. By the looks of it, I guestimated four hikers could fit comfortably, five snugly, six sardines if you were really desperate.

We ended up with five in the shelter that night: myself, Show, Cous Cous, Noah, and his friend Matt. Leigh had texted us back that she was only going to make it to the Wayah shelter that night so luckily we didn’t have to squeeze one more. We actually ended up apologetically turning away a few people who showed up later. With all of our pads set up it turned out that even five was pushing it.

Everyone bedded down for the night, I read a book on the Kindle app on my phone, as I always do to help me fall asleep. My eyes grew heavier and it became increasingly hard to focus on the page and comprehend what was going on in the story—my cue reading time was over. I set my phone down on the shelter floor by my pillow and drifted to sleep.

Rustling sounds. I startle awake. Something crinkling above. My eyes dart to my pack, hanging up under the roof awning, suspended a few feet above my head. I strain to hear against the light rain plopping down on the metal roof and to see through the blackness. The rustling starts again. Yup, its coming from my pack, I confirm. Grabbing my headlamp, I shine its red light towards my pack. The bottom of my pack cover is twitching slightly. Fuck, I think, mice.

I look over at Show. He’s wide awake, as I expected. “I think there’s a mouse in my pack cover,” I whisper.

“Yeah, I can hear it,” he whispers back, “One ran across my pillow earlier. I haven’t slept at all.”

As quietly as I can manage, I slither out of the confines of my sleeping bag and shake my dangling pack. A small fluff ball drops to the shelter floor and scurries out of sight. I remove my pack cover and stuff it in the outer pocket of my pack. At least now if the mouse gets back on my pack it won’t sound like a potato chip bag crinkling anymore.

I get back in my sleeping bag and try to settle down, attempting to push down the anxiety around Show having a sleepless night. He absolutely hates mice. I knew right then this might be our last shelter sleep. Prior to the trail he had wearily conceded to give the shelters a chance on my persuasion. I had compromised that if we encountered any mice issues we would from there forward strictly stick to the tent (his preference). Welp, it was a good almost two week run. Better mentally prepare myself for packing up a cold, wet tent from now on. Possibly sometimes with rain actively falling on me while doing so. Lovely…

I’m just fading into new sleep when I jolt awake, again. This time it isn’t a mouse, but some commotion occurring outside the shelter’s opening. Someone is setting up a tent directly in front of the shelter. It’s still lightly raining; I can see the drops illuminated in their headlamp. I look at my watch: 10:30PM.

I roll over to face Show. He’s on his stomach, head propped up on his hands, looking out the shelter at the tent-pitcher.

“What is he doing?” I ask, flopping onto my stomach for a better vantage point. The tent is partially erected, and Noah is continuously running out if view into the darkness of the woods, then returning and securing a stake down, repeat. Ohh-kayyy…?

“Dunno,” Show responds. “I guess he didn’t want to be in the shelter anymore. I’ve been watching this whole setup process. It’s been pretty entertaining since I still can’t sleep anyway.”

“Sorry,” I sympathize. “We don’t have to sleep in the shelters anymore.”

“Yeah,” Show sighs. “I think I’m done with them.”

Tent finally pitched, Noah dips back into the shelter. “All good man?” Show asks him.

“Yeah, it’s just too squeezy in here. I couldn’t sleep so I decided to just set up my tent. But I forgot my stakes at the hostel so I had to find sticks.”

Ah, that explains the trips back and forth from the woods. We all bid each other another goodnight as Noah grabs his sleeping setup and trots out to his tent with it. By this point my mind is fully awake and alert. I figure the only way to shut it back down is to do the sleep routine all over again. I feel around for my phone, find it, and open the Kindle app.

After another hour or so of reading, my eyes once again grow heavy and out of focus. Show is snoring lightly next to me. All is well with the shelter dwellers. I fall asleep.
