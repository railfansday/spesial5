Andrew Skurka on Vapor Barrier Liners for Winter Camping

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

This article is adapted from Andrew Skurka’s first-person video for The Trek’s YouTube channel, part of a weekly series. Edited for clarity and readability.

On a multi-day winter backpacking trip, your own sweat can become one of your biggest problems. Moisture builds up in your gloves, boots, insulated jackets, and sleeping bag. When temperatures stay below freezing, drying everything out can be nearly impossible.

That’s where vapor barrier liners (VBLs) come in. These intentionally non-breathable layers keep perspiration from reaching your insulation. They can feel clammy and take some getting used to, but in the right conditions they can make a remarkable difference.

I learned this the hard way. I was the first person to complete the Sea-to-Sea Route, and during that trip I spent the winter on the trail, snowshoeing 1,400 miles through Michigan, Wisconsin, and Minnesota. In January 2007, a few months before the Great Western Loop, I hiked about 380 miles along Minnesota’s North Shore from Duluth to Ely, a trip I called Ultralight in the Nation’s Icebox. And I started the Alaska-Yukon Expedition in mid-March in Kotzebue, north of the Arctic Circle. I still remember that for the first three days, the temperature never got above 15 degrees below zero.

The Sea-to-Sea Route was my first legitimate winter backpacking experience, and I wore a pretty traditional layering system. It was basically an extension of what I’d wear in three-season conditions:

When I say cold, I mean daytime temperatures that never get above freezing, ideally no warmer than the 20s, and nighttime lows in the single digits or teens. The same problem applies at anything colder.

You’re active during the day. In the heat of the day, when it’s 20 degrees out and you’re really moving on snowshoes or skis, you might wear just a midweight base layer, or maybe you need a fleece. Either way, you’re perspiring from the activity. And at night, your body is still losing moisture through insensible perspiration.

If you’re wearing all of your layers at night inside a sleeping bag, that moisture moves out through your layering system. It usually gets trapped in my active insulated jacket, or on the inside of my waterproof-breathable shell, or, if I’m in my sleeping bag, inside my big arctic puffy. As the moisture moves away from your body, it gets colder and colder until it hits the dew point, and that point is often inside your layering system or inside your sleeping bag.

Even if you’ve never backpacked in winter, this has probably happened to you. After a day of alpine skiing at a resort, you notice the inside of your gloves is pretty wet. So is the inside of your plastic ski boots.

That’s not a problem if you’re out for the day, because you get home or into a hut and dry everything out. On a backpacking trip, you can’t. You can’t dry out your sleeping bag, your boots, your gloves, or your big belay parka, so the moisture stays trapped. All your stuff gets wet and stays wet, and when it’s wet, it’s also cold.

That was my failing on the Sea-to-Sea Route back in 2004 and 2005. I could go out for a night or two, but by nights three, four, and five, moisture was accumulating in my layers and my sleeping bag. After a couple of days out, I needed to get inside somewhere warm and dry so I could reset all of my systems and safely go out again.

A vapor barrier liner is a non-breathable layer worn just outside a thin or midweight base layer and underneath your insulating layers. The fluorescent orange jacket I’m wearing in the video is an example. Instead of allowing perspiration to move into your fleece, parka, or sleeping bag, the barrier keeps that moisture closer to your skin.

There’s a tradeoff: the sweat doesn’t magically disappear. It stays inside the barrier, where your base layer may become damp or clammy. That’s why I pay close attention to how hard I’m working, and I vent or adjust layers before I start sweating heavily.

If you’re considering multi-day backpacking or thru-hiking in winter with daytime temperatures consistently around 20°F or colder, I think vapor barrier liners are well worth looking into. They can significantly reduce the moisture buildup I just described, although they don’t replace good temperature management or protection from outside moisture.

The first benefit is that a vapor barrier liner protects your exterior layers. It keeps them warm by keeping them dry.

With the VBL on, I’m still perspiring a little, but much of that body moisture is stopped by the barrier instead of moving into my fleece or insulated parkas. At night, I can wear my insulating layers inside my sleeping bag while greatly reducing how much perspiration reaches the bag’s insulation. It won’t make my sleeping bag waterproof or eliminate every source of condensation, but it helps protect against moisture coming from my body.

If you’re wearing what’s almost a trash bag next to your skin, you’re going to be very aware of how much you’re perspiring, because it starts to feel clammy. When you feel that clamminess, it’s your sign to moderate your effort, so maybe you go slower, or to start taking layers off.

Sometimes you’re in between. You don’t want to take a layer off because you’re not that warm, but you definitely need to expel some heat and moisture. That’s when you ventilate. RBH Designs makes a fantastic vapor barrier outer layer, like the one I’m wearing, with all sorts of venting features: a full front zip, pit zips, and zippers on the arms. You can keep wearing the layer but really open it up so the heat and moisture escape.

In very cold weather, you don’t want a lot of ways to lose heat, which is why you carry lots of puffy layers and multiple layers. Without a vapor barrier liner, moisture moves through your layers, and as it gets close to the outside, it evaporates. That evaporation steals heat away from you. A VBL does an excellent job of keeping your heat next to you and preventing that evaporative heat loss.

Here’s a classic scenario that even three-season backpackers will relate to. You hike up a mountain without moderating your effort much. As you climb, it gets cooler and cooler, maybe you enter the clouds and lose the sun, and when you finally reach the top, your back is drenched and your hair is wet with sweat. Then, as you’re signing the summit register, you start getting cold, because all that sweat is evaporating off your body. A vapor barrier liner keeps that moisture inside. You can think of it as its own microclimate, so you don’t get that evaporative heat loss.

If you want to wander into vapor barrier liners, start with gloves. My layering system for really cold weather starts with the DeFeet Duraglove wool glove. It’s definitely a liner glove, but it has more heft than most. Over that, I go straight to the RBH Designs mitts.

When I notice my hands starting to get clammy from sweat, it’s easy. I take the mitt off and loop it onto a carabiner on my backpack’s shoulder strap. Then I can ski or snowshoe for a while without it, and when my hand starts to get cold again, I put the mitt back on.

Again, the idea with vapor barrier liners is that you become sensitive to your rate of perspiration, and you either moderate your effort or take off or adjust layers so that you sweat less.

Then all of that goes into my boot. For those curious, mine are old-school leather three-pin telemark boots, where the telemark binding clamps down on the duckbill at the front.

Vapor barrier liner socks become important because otherwise the sweat from your feet gets into the outer layers of the boot, or gets trapped inside it, and then it’s tough to get the boot dry.

The very last thing you’d add to your vapor barrier liner system is a pair of vapor barrier liner pants. It has to be pretty cold to warrant them, with daytime highs in the low to mid teens and ideally in the single digits. Your legs generate so much heat that it has to be really cold before you’d want running tights with vapor barrier liner pants on top.

As with the top, you want the pants to be really well ventilated, maybe with full or three-quarter zips on the legs. That way you can easily open them up to let the heat and moisture out, and keep moving without having to take the pants off completely because you’re overheating and sweating too much.

With all the temperature ranges I’ve given you, keep in mind that they depend on the other conditions: how much sun there is, how much wind there is, and whether you’re walking on dirt, which absorbs a lot more solar radiation, or on ice or snow, which reflects the sun back at you. You might adjust these recommendations up or down for when to start using vapor barrier liners.

Vapor barrier clothing is a niche category, so you won’t find many options in mainstream backpacking stores. Rab makes vapor barrier socks, and Western Mountaineering offers a vapor barrier liner for sleeping bags.

For purpose-built VBL clothing, I recommend looking at RBH Designs. I’ve had excellent experiences with its equipment, including vapor barrier mitts, socks, and ventilated jackets. As with any specialized winter gear, check current availability and sizing before purchasing.

In addition to jackets, pants, socks, and mitts, there’s another commercially available option: a vapor barrier liner for your sleeping bag. You wear your base layer shirt, pants, and socks, then sleep inside the VBL, which goes inside your sleeping bag.

That’s an effective way to reduce perspiration getting into your sleeping bag’s down or synthetic insulation, because most of the body moisture stays inside the liner’s microclimate. It won’t, however, protect your sleeping bag from moisture coming from outside the liner.

My big hang-up with a vapor barrier sleeping bag liner is that you can’t use it during the day. You still end up with the same situation: you have on your liner gloves and a normal mountaineering mitt, your hands are sweating, and that sweat is making its way into the insulation of the mitt. That’s not going to help you on day two. I think it’s better to have vapor barrier liner technology not only at night, to keep your sleeping bag from getting wet, but also during the day, to keep your outer layers from getting wet.

What’s the minimum trip length where I’d want vapor barrier liners? My answer is anything of one night or more.

Suppose it’s a two-day, one-night trip, and it’s two full days. If you follow the conventional wisdom of using breathable layers, you have your liner gloves and a standard overmitt without a vapor barrier liner. At the end of the first day, your mitt is very wet and very cold. When you wake up in the morning and put it back on, it’s not going to be as nice to wear as it was on the first day.

With boots, you’re perspiring through your feet all day long, and that moisture gets trapped inside your boot. When you wake up in the morning, your boot is going to be frozen. I’ve done a lot of very uncomfortable things in the outdoors, but one of the most uncomfortable is putting your foot inside a frozen boot.

If you ever end up with wet shoes, either from your sweat or from external water like a creek crossing, the key is what you do when you pull into camp at night. Open up the shoe, really extend the tongue, and loosen the laces as much as you possibly can. Then just let it freeze overnight. In the morning, jam your feet inside, and the warmth of your feet will sort of thaw it out. If it freezes in that open position, you can at least get your feet into the shoe.

One additional advantage of using vapor barrier liners is that I can rely more confidently on down insulation during long winter trips. You don’t need VBLs to use down in winter, but limiting the body moisture that reaches it helps preserve its warmth over consecutive days.

You’ve probably heard the usual pros and cons of down versus synthetic insulation. Down generally offers more warmth for its weight. And while synthetic insulation tends to retain more warmth than down when damp, the idea that it stays fully warm when wet is misleading. Wet synthetic insulation still loses insulating performance, just usually not as much as wet down.

With a vapor barrier protecting my outer layers from perspiration, I can use a down sleeping bag, a down belay parka, and even a down active parka with less concern about that particular source of moisture. Those pieces are generally excellent for warmth-to-weight, and good down insulation can last for many years. My GoLite down belay parka dates back to 2009. A VBL doesn’t protect any of this gear from snow, rain, or other outside moisture, though, so keeping it dry still matters.

I’ve been talking about vapor barrier liners for a long time. I used them extremely successfully on Ultralight in the Nation’s Icebox in 2007 and the Alaska-Yukon Expedition in 2010. Yet in the last 15 or 20 years, the market hasn’t changed very much. There are a few good products out there, but the selection is still small.

That’s fine. I think there’s a very small market for vapor barrier liners because, let’s be honest, not many people go out in the wintertime on multi-day or multi-night trips. The days are short, the nights are long, it’s very cold, and it can be kind of lonely.

But winter can also be a beautiful time to be in the backcountry. It extends your season if you want to do more than backpack between June and September. It’s a way to expand your skill set, and you may learn things you can apply to your three-season trips. Getting out at all times of the year, not just when the weather’s nice, will definitely make you a better backpacker.

There’s not a lot of information out there about vapor barrier liners. I wrote a blog post about them years ago that now has more than 200 comments. If you have questions about vapor barrier liners, ask them in the comments on the video, and I’ll try to get to them.

On multi-day winter trips, body moisture can build up in your layers and sleeping bag night after night, and drying everything out may be impossible. A vapor barrier liner worn just outside your base layer reduces how much perspiration reaches your insulation, makes you more aware of when you’re sweating, and helps limit evaporative heat loss. I consider VBLs especially worthwhile when daytime temperatures stay around 20°F or colder: start with mitts, consider socks when highs are in the teens, and save pants for particularly cold conditions. Even for a single overnight trip in those conditions, they’re worth a serious look. You still need to manage your sweat and keep outside moisture off your gear.
