The Mavericks Band Members ‘Saddened’ by Name Rights Lawsuit From Raul Malo’s Widow

Tanggal: 2026-11-18

Paul Deakin, Jerry Dale McFadden and Eddie Perez say Betty Malo has been paid handsomely for their current tour.

The remaining members of Americana band The Mavericks have responded to a lawsuit brought by the widow of their late frontman Raul Malo.

Drummer Paul Deakin, keyboardist Jerry Dale McFadden and guitarist Eddie Perez wrote in a social media statement on Wednesday (Nov. 18), “We were saddened to learn that Betty Malo filed a lawsuit against us.” Betty sued the trio last week, alleging they should be prohibited from performing as The Mavericks following her husband’s 2025 death from cancer.

The crux of Betty’s lawsuit is a clause in the Mavericks’ operating agreement stating that the band could only use that name so long as Raul was involved. She says the surviving members are now violating this by touring with guest singers as “The Mavericks & Friends.”

Attorneys for Deakin, McFadden and Perez claim they amended The Mavericks’ operating agreement before their current “The Music Lives On” tour to remove such restrictive language. And in Wednesday’s statement, the musicians say Betty is actually being paid handsomely for these shows.

“To clear up any speculation on this topic, we have honored the financial agreement reached with the Malo estate before the tour began, and to date, Betty has received more compensation from the tour than any individual partner in The Mavericks,” wrote the band members on social media. “We’ll continue to honor this agreement, just as our ‘Music Lives On’ Tour has honored the music and legacy of our brother Raul at every turn.”

Betty’s lawyers did not immediately return a request for comment on the band’s statement Wednesday.

Legal disputes over band name rights are not uncommon in the music industry. In the past, such melees have broken out between the members of Stone Temple Pilots, Journey, Jefferson Starship and The Beach Boys. As Billboard previously reported, the question of who truly constitutes the band can be a thorny one, especially when groups change lineups over the years or operate under a web of contracts and LLCs.

As one expert put it to Billboard, “There can be a real disparity between the legal answer, about who owns the trademark, and the emotional answer as to what the name of the band means to the members and to the public.”
