In an era marked by broad economic uncertainty, mass layoffs, and high cost of living, a focus on resilience in the workplace has become paramount. What can employers do to foster employee resilience in the workplace? To answer this question, I reached out to Danielle King Jordan, a Professor of Industrial-Organizational Psychology and the Founder and Principal Investigator of the WorKing Resilience Research Lab at Rice University. Her research focuses on employee resilience and stigma, and organizational resources that support employee thriving. I asked Danielle what I think are the four most important questions to help guide employers as they seek to foster employee resilience in the workplace.

DKJ: There are three main approaches I would advocate for employers who want to support employee resilience: First, organizational decision-makers should look at problems, challenges, and adversities as an opportunity to support employees, not as a time to “test” resilience. Offering both proactive (before adversity) and reactive (throughout and after adversity) sources of support is ideal. What these resources look like depends on the types of adversity and on employee needs, so listening to your team is critical.

Second, before enacting resilience interventions, leaders can interrogate whether the adversity or challenge can itself be reduced or removed. This is the greatest source of support for future resilience to unavoidable issues. This is part of an adaptive, rather than a maladaptive, approach to resilience that we talk about in research.

And lastly, it is valuable to understand that we do not all experience adversity in the same way, so we should be willing to take a tailored, rather than one-size-fits-all, approach to supporting resilience. Taking time to find out what different employees are struggling with, even those who belong to the same identity group, and what kinds of support they would like to have is a good use of employers’ time and effort.

DKJ: Leaders are very important for organizational resilience. Leaders often shape the climate of a place and determine access to resources. As an example of their important role, in one study, we found that two significant predictors of team resilience are voice climate and leaders’ learning goal orientation. Voice climate is whether people feel they can speak up safely with ideas and will be listened to if they offer suggestions. This voice process requires that employees feel psychological safety. And leader learning goal orientation is about whether leaders focus on learning, and not just performing.

High voice climate and greater leader learning orientation were shown to together foster team resilience. And in another study, we found that even when leaders are saying “no” to an idea or suggestion, it matters that they respond in a manner that is considerate and sensitive. Otherwise, people will stop speaking up, and this can be costly and dangerous. Overall, research shows that leaders can shape psychological safety and are a key part of the employee resilience puzzle.

DKJ: This is a great and complex question. Measurement in psychological sciences is hard—this is why I say we are not a “soft” science! How you measure a thing needs to be guided by and aligned with how you define it.

So, we have published a chapter called “What is resilience” to theoretically ground our definition and offer more clarity, and we recently developed and are currently validating a new measure of employee resilience. We did this because we uncovered some limitations within existing definitions and measures and wanted to contribute knowledge to this conversation. What is most important is that decision-makers define resilience clearly and then choose a measure that aligns with that conceptualization. It is also useful to consider measuring individual experiences, such as goals and adversity, when measuring this concept.
