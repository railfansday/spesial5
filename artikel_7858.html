Your peace lily (Spathiphyllum) may well have spent the early part of fall thriving in its regular corner with little more than a weekly drink. But this can all change sharply when November arrives. Suddenly, the lower leaves droop, that trademark glossy sheen can look dull, and the leaf tips might turn dry and brown, even if you haven't changed your routine. The real shift is taking place in the house around the plant, and that’s easy to miss when the plant hasn’t moved an inch.

Peace lilies are naturally resilient, but they are sensitive to environmental shifts. The days are shorter, the sunlight angles have dropped, and central heating systems are kicking into gear. Good peace lily care through winter is largely about keeping up with those changes. The good news is that the fixes are small. It’s just about recognizing that your plant’s needs change as winter starts to set in.

By tweaking your habits and pausing unnecessary chores that may have been essential just a month earlier, you can protect root health and ensure your peace lily remains a centerpiece of lush green calm throughout these next few chilly months. Not only will these adjustments protect your favorite houseplant in winter, they will help to set the stage for more striking white spathes come spring. So here’s how to keep your peace lily healthy once the heat comes on…

Indoor winter living often creates conditions that clash with tropical houseplant health. We seal our doors, turn up the furnace, and seek out cozy, dry warmth. For a peace lily, that same forced-air heating strips ambient room humidity down to single digits while leaving drafty windowpanes ice-cold. Coexisting happily indoors through winter means balancing these conditions, providing the warmth your peace lily needs while protecting it against parched air, dark corners, and soggy soil.

Diagnosing leaf symptoms early in November prevents minor seasonal stress from escalating into root damage. If your entire plant suddenly collapses into a limp heap, check the soil before reaching for the watering can. If the potting mix is bone dry, a thorough drink will perk the foliage up within hours. If the mix feels soggy and heavy while the leaves hang limp, waterlogged roots are suffocating: stop watering now!

Brown, crispy leaf tips usually stem from low humidity, uneven moisture, or mineral salt accumulation from tap water. Occasional yellowing on a leaf lower down is normal ageing. However, multiple yellow leaves can indicate insufficient light or waterlogged compost or insufficient light. A long-probe soil moisture meter can help you determine deeper root moisture. I’m a big fan of the XLUX Long Probe Soil Moisture Meter, available from Amazon, which delivers instant readings, so you can address overwatering and soggy potting mix before fungal rot sets in.

As for flowers, don't expect too many right now. Winter usually isn't when peace lilies bloom. A plant that rarely flowers at all, though, probably needs a brighter spot, unless it's still young (under a year old, some won't bloom yet). The quickest way to harm a peace lily in November is sticking to a rigid summer watering schedule. As natural day length shortens and light intensity wanes, your plant enters a semi-dormant rest period. Transpiration slows, cell division halts, and roots need less moisture from the soil. A potting blend that dried out in 5 days during July can easily stay damp for 3 weeks in November. Keeping the soil constantly saturated starves roots of oxygen, eventually leading to Pythium root rot.

This step is easy: insert a finger a couple of inches (5cm) into the potting mix. Water only when it feels dry and crumbly to the touch. When you do water your peace lily, soak the root ball thoroughly with room-temperature water until it drains freely from the base, then empty any runoff collected in the saucer. Never leave a peace lily sitting in stagnant, chilled water. If your plant droops, check soil dampness first.

A nifty way of reducing the risks associated with poor drainage is to give your plants some elevation in the drainage tray. Kakuna Plant Pot Feet, available from Amazon, can be inserted between the pot and tray to minimize chills and waterlogging.

Peace lilies have an enduring reputation as indestructible low-light houseplants, but there is a difference between surviving and thriving. While they tolerate the subdued light of a shaded summer room, the dramatic drop in November’s daylight hours and sun intensity can throw low-light corners into near-darkness. Without help, your peace lily cannot produce the energy needed to support its broad leaf surface, leading to faded foliage, drooping stems, and a halt in flower spathe production.

Take time now to reassess where your peace lily sits and check how much light reaches the plant around noon. If needed, move the plant closer to an east or north-facing window, where it can absorb hours of bright, indirect winter daylight. Keep the leaves a few inches (8-10cm) back from direct contact with windowpanes, as freezing outdoor temperatures radiate through cold glass and will chill leaf tissue.

If placing your plant near a south or west-facing window where bright afternoon sun beams through, hang a sheer curtain to diffuse the rays; direct, unfiltered sunlight can still scorch foliage even in winter. For darker rooms lacking bright exposures, supplemental artificial light provides an easy fix. The GE BR30 Grow LED Light Bulb from Walmart fits a standard socket and lamp fixture, emitting balanced, warm-toned full-spectrum light that sustains photosynthesis through dark winter months.

Indoor central heating is a double hazard for peace lilies: it strips moisture from the air, while creating extreme temperature pockets. Peace lilies thrive in relative humidity levels between 50% and 60%. When heating systems get switched on, indoor humidity frequently plunges below 20%. This dry air saps moisture from leaf margins, causing crispy brown edges and papery tips. Position your plant away from forced-air vents, radiators, space heaters, and wood stoves. The blast of hot air will quickly scorch foliage. At the same time, keep plants shielded from exterior door drafts and chilly single-pane windows, maintaining a range of 65-80°F (18-27°C).

Skip leaf misting, which provides only a brief humidity spike and can encourage fungal leaf spotting. Instead, place your pot on a wide tray filled with pebbles and water (keeping the pot base resting above the water line). Alternatively, group multiple houseplants together, or run a small ultrasonic room humidifier nearby. To help you track things, buy a digital sensor like the TempPro TP50 Digital Room Temperature and Humidity Monitor, available from The Home Depot. This compact digital sensor monitors relative humidity and ambient temperature fluctuations, helping you pinpoint cold drafts and heating hotspots.

During the dark days of late fall and winter, feeding a peace lily does far more harm than good. Because diminished daylight slows metabolic activity, the plant is not producing new tissue or developing roots. Adding liquid houseplant fertilizer during this semi-dormant time means mineral salts can build up in the potting soil. Over several weeks, this salt buildup can burn roots and brown leaf tips. Now is the time to withhold all supplemental feeding until active spring growth returns in late March.

Similarly, postpone repotting until the warmer, brighter days of spring. It doesn’t matter if you see a few roots poking out of the drainage holes: peace lilies tolerate being slightly pot-bound and recover faster in spring. Moving a sluggish plant into a larger pot now can shock them. The only exception is emergency repotting. If a plant has collapsed from severe root rot, gently tip it from its pot and rinse away old soil. Trim off brown mushy roots with sterile shears, and repot into fresh, well-aerated potting mix. A great lightweight blend for emergency winter repotting is Espoma Organic Houseplant Potting Soil Mix, available at The Home Depot.

In just a few weeks, broad peace lily leaves collect a fine film of household dust, pet dander, and airborne grease. Dusty foliage can be severely limiting in winter. Dust acts as a barrier, scattering what little winter light reaches the leaves and reducing the plant's already limited photosynthetic capacity. Cleaning the foliage is a quick, high-reward task that keeps leaves breathing and photosynthesizing efficiently. Support each leaf from beneath with one hand, and gently wipe the upper and lower surfaces with a soft cloth moistened with room-temperature water.

As you clean, inspect the leaf undersides and stem junctions for pests. Dry winter heating can encourage rapid outbreaks unless you catch them early. Bits of white fluff could be citrus mealybugs (Planococcus citri). Flat tan bumps are often brown soft scale (Coccus hesperidum). Faint speckling and webbing usually mean twospotted spider mites (Tetranychus urticae), which thrive in dry, heated rooms. Gently dab mealybug clusters with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, or treat leaf undersides with a gentle organic insecticidal soap or neem oil wash. Bonide Captain Jack’s Neem Oil Spray, available from Amazon, is a brilliant organic way of controlling spider mites, scale, and mealybugs.

By giving your peace lily a little focused attention in November, you provide the support it needs to navigate the winter months with ease. Dialing back the watering can, finding a brighter pocket of filtered daylight, raising ambient moisture levels, and letting the roots rest will keep the foliage healthy and glossy.

Set up well in November, a peace lily usually comes through winter looking much like it did in summer, just growing a little slower. The fertilizer and the bigger pot can wait until spring, and so can you. When longer spring days return, your well-rested peace lily will be primed to surge back into active growth, rewarding you with fresh green leaves and a striking display of signature white blooms.
