What pricing and execution inefficiency really costs mortgage lenders

Tanggal: 2026-11-12

MBA data shows the top 20% of lenders earned $4,551 per loan in Q2 2026 while the bottom 20% lost $2,551. The column argues PPE engines should be evaluated on accuracy, execution and scalability, not only implementation cost.   AI Summary

The gap between mortgage lenders that are making money and those that are losing it is striking: According to the Mortgage Bankers Association, the top 20% of lenders earned an average production profit of $4,551 per loan in the second quarter of 2026, while the bottom 20% lost an average of $2,551 per loan.

MBA Vice President of Industry Analysis Marina Walsh says revenue, rather than expenses, appears to be “the more prominent differentiator” in lender profitability, pointing to product mix, pricing discipline and secondary marketing strategy, among other factors.

For mortgage executives, the findings underscore the importance of evaluating technology investments not only as expenses, but also as potential contributors to revenue and profitability. That is particularly relevant to product, pricing and eligibility (PPE) engines, which play a central role in determining which loan products lenders can offer, how accurately they price them and how effectively they execute their secondary marketing strategies.

Those are reasonable questions. They are also relatively easy to place in a spreadsheet. Implementation labor, training, internal resources and transition requirements can usually be identified, estimated and assigned to a defined period.

The largest economic issue may not be the cost of changing a PPE engine. It may be the cost of continuing to operate with pricing inaccuracies, inefficient workflows, inconsistent execution, avoidable exceptions, high product-maintenance demands, limited operational scale, slow product launches and unrealized revenue opportunities.

Transition costs are typically finite. Performance costs can repeat with every transaction.

That distinction should change how executive teams evaluate PPE engine investments.

Mortgage technology evaluations often begin with product automation: how much time a system can remove from a process or how many manual steps can be eliminated.

Time savings matter, but they are only one component of financial impact and often not the most impactful one.

Focusing only on automation is insufficient because risk reduction and transaction execution can be larger drivers of total financial impact.

This is particularly important in a PPE engine decision. The economic value of the engine is not limited to the minutes it removes from a pricing task. It also includes the quality and consistency of the decision produced, the accuracy of eligibility determinations, the efficiency with which lenders can manage margins and translate pricing strategies into execution, the effort required to maintain products and rules, the speed and accuracy with which new products can be introduced and the organization’s ability to scale and respond to market volatility.

A lower visible technology cost does not necessarily mean a lower enterprise cost.

Executive teams should separate the business case into two categories.

This category includes implementation, training, integration work, data migration and temporary disruption. These costs should be measured carefully, but they generally have two defining characteristics: they are finite, and they are primarily incurred during the transition.

A prudent analysis should identify the internal resources required, external costs, expected timing and operational dependencies. Those numbers belong in the investment model.

They should not, however, become the entire investment model.

The second category includes errors, inconsistent pricing, reduced execution quality, workflow friction, exception management and lower productivity. It also includes opportunity costs, such as the inability to deploy products quickly, fine-tune margins, improve closing performance or respond effectively to market volatility.

These costs may be less visible because they are distributed across business units and individual transactions. Pricing leakage may appear in secondary marketing results. A process deficiency may appear as extra lock-desk work. Weak integration may appear as duplicated effort. Inaccurate eligibility may appear as an exception, a rework event or a lost opportunity.

Individually, each event may appear manageable. Across an enterprise loan population, recurring differences in precision and execution can become economically significant.

Pricing and eligibility accuracy are often discussed as matters of control, compliance or user experience. An inaccurate decision can create rework, exceptions and downstream adjustments, but its effect can also extend to the economics of the loan itself.

While lenders may not be able to calculate the exact cost of every error, recurring inaccuracies can add up quickly across thousands of transactions. Treating accuracy as only an operational benefit can therefore understate its financial importance.

A PPE engine should return accurate pricing quickly. Its broader value lies in helping an organization execute its pricing strategy consistently. This includes enabling teams to respond to market movement, manage margins efficiently, evaluate options and maintain consistency from pricing through delivery.

Execution is therefore a core consideration in a PPE investment decision. Organizations should evaluate whether the platform enables accurate, timely and consistent decisions that protect margins and support an effective response to market volatility. In that context, incremental differences in licensing costs may be less significant than the platform’s effect on financial performance.
