AI can help moms. It can’t replace the village

Tanggal: 2026-11-25

Susan (pseudonym) regularly goes to AI for parenting help. When her child is sick, she checks whether she needs to call the doctor. When her toddler is acting out, she looks to AI to find out if it’s normal. But then, she takes the question to her online parenting group at Peanut to double-check.

She already has an answer. So what is she still looking for?

AI is always there. When we have a question at 2 a.m. and no one else is awake, we can ask Claude. When we fear judgment, ChatGPT will almost always validate. Is AI going to replace the “parenting village” we were all supposed to find?

Maybe, but probably not.

Curious about how this is all playing out, I went looking for answers in a few places. I started with Michele Battersby, president of Peanut, an online community platform for mothers. Her team looked at public conversations on the platform mentioning AI over 90 days. Mothers were asking about everything: health, development, sleep, feeding, and all the life logistics that go with parenting.

But while they were asking these questions, it seems AI was often just a starting place. AI can give us information. It can be a place to ask a question that we might otherwise feel embarrassment about. It can even help with the mental load (more on that later). But even with all of that, mothers on the app were still going to other mothers for support and to talk about their actual experiences.

Because sometimes we’re looking for more than an answer.

I’ve been thinking about this as AI becomes part of what we’re calling the parenting village. I recently asked my LinkedIn community whether they were using AI for parenting support. Of 133 people who responded, 30% said they regularly used it for the mental load, 21% regularly used it for parenting questions, 20% used it sometimes or rarely, and 28% weren’t using it for parenting at all.

It’s a small, self-selected poll, but the conversations were interesting. Some women talked about how much AI was helping them. Others worried about what we might lose if we started turning to technology instead of each other.

Both things are probably true.

But perhaps the bigger question is this: Are we creating a world where mothers have more kinds of support, or one where we’re asking technology to compensate for the human and institutional support that’s missing?

Because filling those gaps doesn’t negate the need to fix what created them in the first place.

Why mothers are turning to AI

The Surgeon General’s 2024 advisory on parental mental health found that 48% of parents reported feeling completely overwhelmed by stress on most days, compared with 26% of other adults. 

And mothers are bearing a disproportionate share of the work. A 2025 study of 2,133 partnered American parents found that as women’s employment and earnings increased, their physical household labor decreased, but their cognitive labor didn’t decrease in the same way.

Parents are stressed, and mothers are carrying an enormous burden without enough support. So it makes sense to me that they’re looking for help wherever they can find it.

The mental load seems like an easy place to start. The New York Times recently wrote about AI family assistants that help parents manage school emails, calendars, and household schedules. And in a 2026 survey from Lurie Children’s Hospital, about two-thirds of parents said AI had reduced their mental load. Parents reported saving an average of 58 minutes a week, and 91% said using AI increased their confidence as parents.

If something can help with all those logistics, why not let it?

The appeal of AI goes beyond organizing the family calendar.

Motherhood is rife with judgment everywhere you look. Something as simple as diapers can become some sort of moral crisis. And let’s not get started on how we feed or discipline our children.

Reaching out with questions as a mom can feel terrifying with good reason. When I was a new mother, I remember trying to get advice about breastfeeding and returning to work. I was struggling to figure out how to keep my milk supply up, and I turned to an online parenting group hoping for some practical help. Instead, I was essentially told that if I wanted to breastfeed, I shouldn’t be working. It was devastating.

AI has an obvious appeal. It’s available, and it doesn’t judge.

That can be valuable, but it’s not the same thing as having support from another person.

We still need the village 

AI can validate, but it can’t coregulate with us in the way another person can. It can’t feel with us or share its own vulnerability. And especially when it comes to parenting, human connection is an undervalued part of the village. 

We think we’re looking for an answer, but what we also need is to hear from another person who has been through something similar, who can tell us what it was like for them, or admit that they struggled, too.

Of course, that doesn’t always happen. Other people can be judgmental. They can give us advice we didn’t ask for. They can misunderstand what we’re going through. Yet when we do find someone who understands, there’s something different about that experience.

Emerging research sheds some potential light on this, too. A 2026 study in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology found that college students who interacted regularly with another student over two weeks experienced greater reductions in loneliness than those interacting with a supportive AI chatbot.

Meanwhile, another study comparing perceptions of AI companions with support from friends and family found that young adults who reported stronger support from the humans in their lives were less likely to view AI companionship favorably.

Neither study was about mothers, and the second doesn’t establish cause and effect. But I find that really important; it raises the possibility that AI becomes more appealing when we don’t have the human support we need.

AI can fill in where we have holes in the village, but it’s not a replacement for the people who understand what we’re going through.

What happened to the village?

But what about this mythical village?

You know, the group of mom friends who all have kids the same age, who parent the same way we do, who are always there when we need them. We’re supposed to find these people, become best friends, and have this amazing support system.

Maybe we even all move into a commune together and live happily ever after.

But I don’t know that I’ve ever really seen that village exist in quite the way we imagine it. And I wonder if part of the problem is that we’re thinking about the village too narrowly.

When we talk about finding our village, we’re usually talking about the social village. Friends, other parents, extended family, and the people who understand what we’re going through. However, too often, finding a social village can feel like another item on an overflowing to-do list.

Instead of telling mothers to try harder to find their people, we need to make it easier for those connections to happen.

I recently joined Fast Company‘s M Suite, a community for working mothers, and was struck by how quickly I felt connected to women I’d never met. Women who were thinking about the same questions of ambition, careers, motherhood, caregiving, and identity. And I found myself wanting to meet some of them in person.

Peanut’s 2026 Motherhood Index found that conversations about friendship and community increased 82% year over year, while conversations about offline meetups and playdates increased 53%. Those numbers reflect changes in conversations on Peanut, not necessarily changes in how all mothers are feeling. But I think they’re interesting, particularly the growing interest in meeting offline.

In response, Peanut is also helping mothers make those connections happen. Its Village Fund, in partnership with Coterie, a maker of hypoallergenic diapers, provides grants to mothers who want to organize gatherings. In funding in-person experiences, it’s making the social village more accessible.

But there’s also what I think of as the structural village. Affordable childcare that actually has openings, paid leave, workplace flexibility, community resources, and help at home with the tangible work of parenting.

The support AI can’t provide

There’s a difference between adding AI to our village and expecting it to make up for everything that’s missing. we are now telling moms, “Go to AI,” but that doesn’t mean AI can replace help at home, help at work, or government policies around childcare and paid leave.

A 2026 Pew Research Center study found that 71% of working parents said having flexibility over their working hours would be highly helpful, but only 25% actually had substantial flexibility. And according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, only 49% of private sector workers had access to paid family and medical leave in 2026.

Meanwhile, even in families where both parents work, mothers are far more likely to be the primary caregivers. Federal Reserve data found that 49% of mothers in dual-earner couples said they were usually responsible for caring for their children when they were home, compared with just 8% of fathers (the remaining 43% said they shared childcare equally).

These aren’t problems we can solve by becoming better organized, and they’re not problems AI can prevent or solve for us.

AI can help with some of the mental load. But why is so much of that load falling on mothers in the first place? AI can draft us the perfect script to ask for flexibility at work, but that doesn’t mean our workplaces will provide it. AI can help us find all the day cares in our area, but it doesn’t make childcare affordable or available. 

Let’s embrace AI even when it only solves one thing. As mothers we need all the help we can get. But let’s not confuse making an unreasonable amount of responsibility more manageable with actually changing how much responsibility mothers are expected to carry. Because we can’t let AI be the thing that lets the system off the hook.
