How a tiny Italian soccer team plans to beat the biggest clubs in Europe

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Como 1907 is a tiny European soccer team. It plays in a town of 85,000 people on Lake Como in northern Italy, where some Hollywood royalty live for part of the year. Its stadium holds only 12,000, which makes it a school playground next to Real Madrid’s 83,000-capacity Santiago Bernabéu. The owners of Como, Indonesia’s billionaire Hartono family, aim to beat Real Madrid, along with the likes of Paris Saint-Germain, Arsenal, and Bayern Munich.

Not on the pitch, necessarily, even though this year Como made it for the first time to the UEFA Champions League, the top club competition in Europe and the world. Its president, Mirwan Suwarso, wants to turn the little team into a financial powerhouse. Since Como will never match the TV and ticket money of Europe’s giants, he is trying to outgrow them as a brand. He wants Como to play in Disney’s league, not only the Champions League.

Suwarso sees soccer as the attraction. “There is a merchandise arm, the same as Disney,” he told The Observer in October. “The stadium, coming to a game, is the theme park. It is where you can touch and feel it, where you can experience the magic of Como.”

He expects nonsoccer revenue to overtake soccer revenue within two or three years, and already values the club at $1.1 billion. Como already has a fashion and hospitality business. You may have seen A-listers in its stands on Instagram. It sells match-day hospitality in rented lakeside villas, and its sister companies run a travel agency, a property arm, a youth academy, and a digital-content unit. Eventually, the club will offer more than that, as the brand’s presence expands across the world.

Como’s latest money-making move is kits. Adidas, current maker of its game jerseys and shorts, pays no sponsorship fee to Como, unlike some of the top teams in the world. European teams like Real Madrid get about $139 million a year on average. In addition to that, Real Madrid gets a 22.5% cut on anything with its brand sold through Adidas channels once sales pass a set threshold, along with bonuses for winning major titles like the Champions League or the Spanish league, La Liga. Como, like most teams, gets none of that.

Instead, Como and its small team brethren get gear for their teams. For Como, that’s $300,000 to $400,000 worth of it, which probably costs $100,000 to make, says Suwarso. He points out that they need to buy millions of dollars in kits from Adidas to sell to fans. From those sales they get a cut. Suwarso thinks this type of deal is upside down for small clubs. Adidas declined to comment for this story.

Suwarso’s answer is to own the whole chain, from the shirt’s design to the shop shelf. And they want to share this design, manufacturing, and distribution business with other small clubs so they all can get more money.

Como’s Adidas deal ends after the 2026-27 season. The club won’t renew it, and it plans to launch its own kit brand in June. Suwarso doesn’t pretend Como will make Real Madrid money this way. Real Madrid will make more because “of course, they’re bigger,” he said.

Peter Rohlmann, a German sports-marketing consultant who runs PR Marketing, a firm in Rheine, thinks Como is overreaching. In-house jerseys have failed within a few years in the vast majority of cases, even at big clubs, he says. Fans resist little-known labels, clubs underestimate the costs, and production never reaches real volume, he points out in an email interview. Napoli, another Italian soccer club, is the only one that survived with its Armani-designed kits. “All others have failed,” Rohlmann told the Sports Business Journal back in August.

Lecce, another team that plays in Italy’s Serie A league, tried to launch its own homemade brand, M908, starting in 2018. M908’s sales were only around $2.3 million a year, according to News Tank Football. This July, the club announced that Adidas would take over starting with the 2026-27 season.

Como’s management believes they can fix this volume problem by selling to other clubs. The club will join forces with “a historic Italian label”—Como hasn’t identified it yet—to make its new kit brand, according to FashionNetwork, while it dresses clubs across Europe and South America. Its Rhude 4 Fans line, launched in April 2026, already makes collections for Tottenham Hotspur and Everton in England’s Premier League, and Los Angeles FC, in Major League Soccer. The Observer counts 24 clubs that Como helps with merchandise. The kits are for “fellow unwanteds and undesirables,” says Suwarso. The teams that Nike, Adidas, and Puma won’t pay.

Como will carry the bill, says Alexandra Bell, the club’s chief communications officer. “We will underwrite production, marketing, and distribution costs, making it a no-risk proposition for partner clubs,” she tells Fast Company in an email. The kit’s design will be the product of “a collaboration between our creative team and our partner’s designers,” she says.

Bell tells me that only Como’s players will wear the new kits next season, as the company fine-tunes the manufacturing. “On the lifestyle side, this newly formed brand have already reached agreements to support nine other clubs next season, and that number continues to grow,” she says.

Como has its work cut out for it, as brands like Skechers, On (which got the French star Kylian Mbappé to promote its gear), New Balance, and Under Armour all try to gain a foothold in European soccer. Still, Bell is confident, given Como’s background in design and production.

“Just like any business, risk cannot be eliminated,” she tells me, but the club’s retail team already handles everything “from design, production and supply chain to logistics, distribution and e-commerce,” including six shops in Como itself and 585 affiliate shops across the province. In four years, she says, that operation grew from about $59,000 a year to a business that’s targeting about $44 million in annual revenue in 2026.

Perhaps Como will succeed where others failed. The club has a special allure, a je ne sais quoi that makes it very attractive to people. Part of it lies in its David-versus-Goliath story. The Hartonos helped the club mount a formidable comeback, taking the team back to Italy’s top division. (The club had been as low as the fourth tier of the Italian game at one point.) They surprised everyone last year, when Como finished fourth in Serie A. This got the club into the 2026-2027 Champions League, where it is near the top of the standings as of November. 

Another part of the club’s attraction is the high-class setting of Lake Como and all the glamour the stars who vacation there bring to the public’s collective imagination. Hugh Grant, Keira Knightley, Michael Fassbender, and Florence Pugh have all turned up at the Stadio Sinigaglia at one point. Many came while vacationing on the lake. The French soccer icon Thierry Henry and Como’s coach, Cesc Fàbregas—a FIFA World Cup winner with Spain—own stakes in the club. According to Goal, a former employee told La Gazzetta dello Sport, Italy’s leading sports daily, that the club treats famous guests to all-inclusive weekends. This helps Como position itself as an aspirational brand. 

Selling the experience

The Hartono brothers, Robert Budi and Michael, bought Como in 2019. Back then, the team was stuck in Italy’s fourth division, playing semi-professional soccer. It seemed like a losing team, but the brothers saw opportunity: a potentially huge business that could go well beyond the sport. Fashion, travel, hospitality, all built around the club and the picture-perfect Lake Como brand. 

The Hartonos set up Sent Retail, a sister company that shares a holding with a property arm and a travel agency. According to Gazzetta, the company is separate from the club. It also has its own retail and fashion arm. Called Curva, it sells its own label alongside other clubs’ merchandise. AC Milan gear is its best seller, Suwarso says. Curva also stocks Venezia, Bologna, and AS Roma gear, and more clubs are coming, Bell tells me.

“Our vision is for Curva to become a multibrand fashion destination inspired by football culture—think Kith for football culture,” she says, referring to the New York streetwear store.

They got Rhuigi Villaseñor—founder of the Los Angeles label Rhude and a Como shareholder—as its chief brand officer. He has been busy ever since. Villaseñor’s streetwear label Rhude worked with Adidas to drop limited-edition fourth kits. The club also worked with the Indonesian couture designer Didit Hediprasetyo to create the club’s main home and away jerseys, incorporating Lake Como water and marble patterns painted by the Switzerland-based artist Golnaz Jebelli.

The company got the Italian luxury menswear house Brioni to design the suits for the squad and the Milanese luggage maker Bric to make cobranded leather duffel bags and travel accessories. Hublot, the Swiss watchmaker, created a 50-piece limited-edition Classic Fusion black ceramic chronograph featuring the club crest.

The company also produces its own resortwear and leisure apparel via the Official Como 1907 Store under fictional country club labels like Como Sailing Club, Como Running Club, and Como Padel Club. There are Lago di Como lines for running, tennis, padel, and the outdoors. To give you an idea of price, they sell a quilted leather bomber for about $1,740.

Is it working?

That postcard travels well in every sense of the word. Only 38% of its merchandise is soccer gear, according to the Sunday Times. More than 40% of lifestyle sales come from the United States, versus around 17% from Italy, Suwarso said at the Pambianco-PwC Fashion Summit in Milan on November 11, according to FashionNetwork. Como is hunting for a flagship store in London, and Paris and Milan are next, Bell says.

According to Suwarso, more than 80% of the group’s nearly 300 employees don’t work in the club itself at all. The club, however, is still the key to it all. Its social following grew 202% in a year, to nearly 4 million. Stadium revenue nearly tripled in a year, from about $5.5 million to $15.5 million. Almost all of that jump came from hospitality, Suwarso told Business of Sport. While Como fans pay $23 for basic tickets, top hospitality packages reach about $2,900. “With a 12,000-seat stadium, there are limits to how much we can grow through match day revenue alone,” Bell says.

For all of Suwarso’s optimism, Rohlmann sees a new Borussia Dortmund. The German club went public on the Frankfurt Stock Exchange in 2000, and Rohlmann says Como, like Dortmund back then, is trying things “out of euphoria and because of the need for storytelling.” Dortmund spent its IPO money on an expensive squad and a bigger stadium, sold the stadium and leased it back, and by 2005 was hours away from bankruptcy, with debts of around $140 million.

So far, however, the numbers seem to be on Suwarso’s side.

These aren’t audited figures but, in addition to the $44 million in annual retail revenue this year, the club itself claims its revenue also jumped from $11.4 million in the 2023-24 season to $64.3 million in 2024-25. But so did the losses: $122 million for the club itself in 2024-25 and $153 million for the whole group, according to Gazzetta.

Costs nearly tripled that season, to $184 million, with staff costs alone at $99 million, according to Calcio e Finanza, an Italian sports finance site. The Hartonos have put in about $450 million, much of it on players: Como has spent about $470 million on transfers since the 2023-24 season, according to Outside Sports, a sports business site. In June, Gazzetta predicted that Como would “inevitably exceed” the financial rules of UEFA, the European federation. These rules ban clubs from spending too much money beyond a certain level of financial loss. 

These rules are designed to avoid billionaires from using their resources to win competitions, even if their teams are losing money, thus making other clubs with no financial backup less competitive. “If you have to fine us, fine us,” Suwarso told Business of Sport. The club is expected to agree a settlement with UEFA around April or May 2027, according to Outside Sports.

Suwarso promises that the club will break even by the end of next season. “Our priority is a sustainable club,” Bell says. “Performance on the pitch is unpredictable, and commercial growth does not automatically translate into results.”

We will see soon enough. If Suwarso is right, Como will continue making more money selling its brand than playing with its crest. If Rohlmann is right, they will end up with the most expensive gift shop south of the Alps.
