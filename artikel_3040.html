Usman Khawaja makes explosive claim about Austalia 'trying to keep up with' South Africa's ball-tampering during Sandpapergate

Usman Khawaja's book Sabr puts South Africa's own ball-handling methods under fresh scrutiny.

A newspaper extract reveals sandpaper was used in a Test before the Cape Town scandal.

Cricket Australia says it remains comfortable with its original findings despite the new claims.

Usman Khawaja has made an explosive claim that Australia were merely trying to keep pace with South Africa when it came to altering the ball in 2018.

It is a suggestion that shifts some of the spotlight from Cape Town to the home side's own methods. He says the South Africans were "far better" at working on the ball, and that his team were struggling to match them.

The Australian batter has also stated that the entire squad was part of the sandpaper plot, and that sandpaper was used on the ball in the Gqeberha Test, which came before the infamous match at Newlands.

All of this is set to appear in his autobiography, Sabr, which goes on sale on Tuesday. Khawaja has already briefed Australia captain Pat Cummins and Cricket Australia chief executive Todd Greenberg about what the book contains.

A report in The Age of Melbourne, which will carry an extract from the memoir on Sunday, says Khawaja reveals that Bancroft first used sandpaper on the ball during the second Test at Port Elizabeth. That detail suggests the plan was already in action well before the third Test began.

The batter then describes the events that followed in the next match at Cape Town. He was sitting beside Bancroft when a conversation with a coach took place, and that exchange set off the decision to use sandpaper once again. The television cameras picked it up, and that is how the whole matter came to light.

It is the Port Elizabeth revelation that stands out as the most startling part of his account, because it undermines the idea that Cape Town was a one-off lapse. It is also likely to put pressure on others who were there to explain what they knew and how far the cheating actually went.

Khawaja was asked to explain his claim about the opposition, and he pointed to something he had heard from a player in the South African camp. He did not name that player in the material released so far, so the full picture will only emerge once the book is out.

"They were so good," he said. He then added that he goes into "a little bit of detail" about "what I'd heard from one of their players and what they were doing".

The South African captain at that time, Faf du Plessis, has been open on several occasions about his side's search for reverse swing through unconventional means.

He had also been punished twice before for ball tampering, once for using the zip of his trouser pocket and once for using a mint during a Test against Australia.

Du Plessis and his camp argued strongly that using sweets or gum to produce saliva and keep the ball shiny had been an unspoken habit in international dressing rooms for years. Steve Smith, then Australia's captain, even backed him publicly during that series, saying the practice was widespread, a gesture du Plessis is said to have deeply appreciated.

The legal position was murky. Law 41 forbade any artificial substance on the ball, yet players had long treated sugary saliva as a grey area, until the footage from Hobart pushed the ICC towards a stricter line.

In his autobiography and later interviews, Du Plessis has been candid about how elite teams operate close to the edge of the rules.

South Africa, he has said, threw the ball into rough patches on the square and kept one side heavily shined while letting the other dry out and scuff.

He also recalled that in the first Test at Durban, Australia's bowlers Mitchell Starc and Pat Cummins found reverse swing around the 25th or 26th over, which the South Africans considered unnatural on a soft outfield. In his view, both teams were locked in a tactical battle over the ball, and Australia, feeling they were losing it, crossed the line at Newlands by taking sandpaper onto the field.

Du Plessis also showed sympathy for Bancroft, whom he described as an inexperienced player pressured into doing something clumsy with cameras everywhere. He dismissed as "absolute nonsense" the idea that bowlers could be unaware of ball tampering, since any top fast bowler knows straight away when a ball has been altered.

Khawaja spoke warmly about Smith, whom he believes has fully recovered but has never forgotten that year.

"Even Steve Smith. Steve's lucky, the greatest batsman of my generation," he said. "He came back from that, and he's come back as good as he ever was in that first Ashes series, and it's kind of been forgotten for him in some respects. But I know he has never ever forgotten that year and how it all panned out."

He then explained his motives, insisting that this was about fairness for the trio who were punished.

"But I'm not doing this for any other reason than I think it's the right thing to do. I've always felt like there's more to life than just cricket, and these guys, in some respect, deserve that justice. And it just doesn't feel like anyone's going to give it to them."

He added that none of the three wanted to speak first. "None of those three want to be the first to say anything. And rightly so, it will just be hearsay and 'their word' if they do. They needed someone else to say something."

Between the Cape Town and Johannesburg Tests, then chief executive James Sutherland sent head of integrity Iain Roy and team performance manager Pat Howard to Cape Town for a code-of-conduct inquiry. That process led to the punishments handed to Smith, David Warner and Bancroft.

Roy lost his job three months later, and Sutherland resigned the same week after 17 years in charge. Howard left in November, following a critical cultural review of Australian cricket.

After Khawaja's interview with Nine, Cricket Australia released a statement defending the process. It noted "a great deal of speculation" in the eight years since the Cape Town Test, including "extensive media coverage" during last month's series in South Africa.

"During this time there has been the ongoing opportunity to present any relevant information, and this remains the case," the board said.

"All relevant information presented to CA to date has been properly considered, and we remain comfortable with the findings of our integrity process."

Khawaja's remarks about South Africa are bound to attract attention, but they need to be weighed carefully. His comparison rests, for now, on something he says he heard from a player, and until the book is available, the details cannot be tested.

There is a reasonable case for taking the claim seriously. Du Plessis was sanctioned twice, and his own comments about reverse swing suggest that South Africa were pushing at the limits of the laws. If both sides were manipulating the ball, then the story of Sandpapergate becomes broader than the three Australians who were banned.

However, the opposite view is just as important. Du Plessis was punished in the open, with the evidence on camera, and he paid the penalty. Bending the rules in a way that has been caught and sanctioned is not the same as organising a team-wide plot, and it does not excuse what happened at Newlands.

The reported use of sandpaper at Port Elizabeth, a Test earlier, only strengthens that point, because it shows Australia's conduct was not a response to a single moment of pressure. Australia's actions remain Australia's responsibility, whatever the opposition may have been doing.

It is also worth noting that much of the South African material comes from Du Plessis's own recollections, and recollections can be shaped by hindsight. Khawaja's account, too, is one voice, and it will be read alongside Cricket Australia's insistence that its findings stand.

The fairest approach is to wait for the book, hear from the South African players involved, and let evidence rather than assumption decide how far the blame should spread.
