When Did Tracking Our Teenagers Become Normal?

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

As thousands of Australian teenagers head off to "Schoolies"—a traditional holiday celebrating the end of high school—I imagine plenty of parents are feeling a mixture of pride and apprehension. For many young people, this is their first holiday without parents nearby. An important step toward adulthood.

Parents no longer have to wonder where their teenagers are. Location-tracking apps allow them to follow their movements throughout the day and night.

A recent media release from Life360 caught my attention. The company reports that 94% of school leavers planning a trip intend to use location sharing, with 74% planning to share their location with a parent.

Of course, this is company-commissioned research linked to their tracking apps. But what struck me was how readily location tracking is becoming an accepted part of parenting teenagers.

A recent article in The Atlantic, titled "The Parents Who Never Stop Tracking Their Kids," explores how location tracking has become commonplace in families, even extending into the college and university years.

In my book The Parenting Paradox, I explore how we've arrived at today's intensive parenting culture, where good parenting increasingly means anticipating risks, monitoring well-being, and stepping in before difficulties arise.

Technology has taken this to another level. We can now keep watch over our children even when they're miles away.

But here's the paradox. The more responsibility we take for managing our children's lives, the harder it becomes to give them space to manage their own lives.

Location sharing may be useful at times, particularly when friends are traveling together. But I wonder what happens when parents become accustomed to always knowing where their teenagers (or indeed younger children) are.

We feel uneasy, check the app, and experience some reassurance. Then another worrying thought arises, and we check again. The more we rely on monitoring to settle our anxiety, the less opportunity we have to give space for our teenagers to grow their capacity to manage themselves.

Knowing where a teenager is doesn't tell us whether they're making wise decisions or developing the judgment they'll need in adult life.

Any opportunity for young adults to travel independently, such as "Schoolies," provides an opportunity for sensible conversations about safety, friendships, and responsibility. We should have good, open communication about arrangements made and how our teen has thought through contingencies. But it's also an opportunity for parents to practice something important: managing our own worries rather than continually investing in managing our children.

Perhaps the question isn't simply whether we can track our teenagers. It's whether we're prepared to let them take another step toward adulthood without feeling we must follow their every move.

And maybe that's a growing-up opportunity for parents, too.
