Millennials living in rural areas have 42% more documented chronic conditions on average compared to those in other environments, a new report from athenaInstitute found.

And, millennials in rural communities are 47% more likely to have at least two chronic conditions. This heavier chronic disease burden extends to other generations as well, analysts note.

Half of Baby Boomers (50.4%) in rural areas have three or more documented conditions, which the report says is “on par” with non-rural individuals in the Silent and Greatest generations.

The 2026 Rural Ambulatory Care Report (PDF) analyzed athenahealth network data from 12.3 million rural patients between 2021 and 2025, along with more than 30,000 clinicians and 1,861 rural practices across the U.S. It examined factors such as appointment activity, workforce composition, payments, and more.

Athenahealth CMO and athenaInstitute co-chair Nele Jessel, M.D., said in a statement the findings show that while rural practices are not “standing still in the face of enormous challenges,” there are “limits to how much more they can absorb as patient needs continue to grow.”

“They are adapting how they deliver care and finding ways to keep patients connected to the care they need,” Jessel said. “With renewed focus on rural healthcare, there is a real opportunity to build on what is working by investing in the workforce, technology and models of care that can help these practices continue serving the communities that depend on them.”

Primary care physicians in rural areas have to wear multiple hats, as specialty care needs at practices are higher in rural communities.

The report found that primary care handles 68.7% of visits involving anxiety or depression diagnoses versus 55.1% in non-rural areas. Moreover, 25% of rural patients with documented anxiety or depression had a substance use disorder—up from 19.8% in non-rural communities.

Data shows rural and non-rural healthcare practices collected roughly the same share of what they were owed by insurers in 2025, but analysts note a difference in patient balances.

Rural practices collected about $70 of every $100 owed, while non-rural practices collected about $78—which analysts say is about a 10% gap.

Another potential source of financial vulnerability for rural practices is Medicaid. Medicaid covered 27.5% of rural visits, which analysts note leaves these practices more exposed to eligibility changes set to begin in 2027.
