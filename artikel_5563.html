GLP-1 Use Linked to Increased Breast Density on Mammograms

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

— Majority of patients had increases in breast density but cancer risk implications remain unclear

Weight loss associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists may alter breast density on mammography, potentially complicating interpretation of the images, an analysis involving more than 100 patients suggested.

Mammographic breast density by automated assessment increased by at least one category in almost two-thirds of patients after a year of treatment with a GLP-1 agonist. None of the automated assessments showed a decline in breast density. Greater weight loss had a modest association with greater increases in automated breast-density assessment. Density-change assessments varied among three radiologists who reviewed each mammogram.

About a fifth of the patients had recalls for additional assessment (at least twice the national average), and four underwent breast biopsies, all of which proved to be negative, according to Eva C. Gombos, MD, of Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston. She will report the study at the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA) meeting, which begins Nov. 29 in Chicago.

"The findings do not suggest that patients should be concerned about taking GLP-1 medications," Gombos said in an RSNA press release. "An increase in mammographic density after substantial weight loss does not necessarily mean that the amount of fibroglandular tissue has increased or that breast cancer risk has increased. The long-term risk implications of these imaging changes remain uncertain and require further study. However, substantial weight loss may change the mammographic appearance of the breast, which could lead to additional imaging or a screening callback."

Women taking GLP-1 receptor agonists should continue routine breast cancer screening according to existing recommendations, she added.

Changes in breast density after weight loss are not a new observation, but they have become more common with the growing use of GLP-1 drugs, said Joseph Villavicencio, MD, of the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine, who was not involved in the study. A recent case review that he co-authored showed that "breast imaging findings following the use of GLP-1 agonists may vary, including both significant and insignificant increases in breast density. Patients with increased breast density may warrant further evaluation with supplemental screening."

"There is published data that increased breast density has been associated with an increased risk of breast cancer," Villavicencio told MedPage Today. "It's not clear that it applies to this situation."

A study reported at the 2026 American Society of Clinical Oncology meeting suggested the opposite effect, as patients treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists had a 25% lower breast cancer risk, he added. Another study reported at the meeting showed that GLP-1 agonist users had a significantly lower risk of progression in four different types of cancer, including breast cancer.

"I think the implication really is that we may need patients to come back after screening -- patients who previously didn't require supplemental screening -- as their breast density changes," said Villavicencio.

"The [current] study mentions that contextualizing information is important, and I think that's extremely helpful," he added. "There are different reasons for changes in breast density, and knowing what's causing it can help us understand if this is something that's worrisome or not."

Gombos and colleagues performed a retrospective longitudinal analysis to investigate within-patient changes in mammographic breast density after GLP-1 receptor agonist-associated weight loss. Additionally, they examined the relationship between magnitude of weight loss and breast density changes, associated mammographic findings, and impact on recall rates.

The 107 patients had a mean age of 57.5 and had mammograms before starting GLP-1 agonists and at least 1 year afterward. Changes in breast density were assessed by an automated software program and by three breast radiologists. Using American College of Radiology BI-RADS criteria, the radiologists assessed breast density categories, subjective changes in density, breast size change, and skin thickening.

By automated analysis, 65 of 104 (62.5%) patients had an increase in breast density. Most of the patients (57 of 64) had one-category increases, and the remaining eight had two-category increases. No patient had a decrease in breast density by automated analysis.

A 30-lb median weight loss was associated with no change in breast density, whereas a 38-lb loss was associated with a one-category increase and 40 lb with a two-category increase, resulting in a modest association between weight loss and automated density-category increase (P=0.0366).

Visual assessment by the radiologists varied, as reader-specific decreases in breast size ranged from 57.9% to 78.5% and magnitude of skin thickening from 9.3% to 17.8%.

The screening recall rate was 22.4% (24 of 107 women). According to the American Cancer Society, the national recall rate averages about 10%, and the American College of Radiology has a target range of 5-12%. Gombos noted that the technical recall rate, unrelated to a suspicious finding, was 1.9%.
