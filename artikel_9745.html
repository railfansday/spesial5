A data model, a KPI taxonomy and a 90-day plan for the stretch when nobody can tell you what “normal” looks like.

Three weeks after launch, someone senior will ask, “Is it working?”

If your answer is a dashboard with forty tiles, you’ve lost the room. If your answer is “we don’t have a baseline yet,” you’ve lost it more politely.

Most of the time the problem isn’t the engineering. It’s the order of operations. Teams instrument everything, argue about definitions in week six, and then find out that the numbers they’ve been quoting can’t be rebuilt. I’d rather you skip that part.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who has launched a data product. It goes like this. A product ships on a Tuesday. The team has page views, sign-ups and a dashboard somebody built over a weekend. By week three an executive asks whether it’s working, and the honest answer is that nobody has agreed what “working” means. By week six two teams are quoting two different activation numbers, and both are calculated correctly from two different definitions. By week ten someone proposes rebuilding the pipeline and finds that the aggregates they kept can’t answer the new question, because the raw events were summarized away on day one.

Nothing in that story is dramatic. No outage, no villain. The measurement design was simply decided by default, and defaults are expensive. That’s the failure this article is meant to prevent.

Every number below comes from one worked example with made-up data: a self-service reorder feature in a mobile app. I’ll flag it each time so nobody mistakes it for a benchmark.

Before any KPI gets a name, write down the decisions the numbers have to support. Not “what can we measure.” What will someone do differently on Monday because of this number?

Most KPI lists fail because everything sits at the same level: revenue next to page load time next to a survey score. Nobody can say which number moves which. I use four tiers, and the fourth is the one teams skip.

One outcome. Three or four drivers. A guardrail for every way you could “win” by breaking something else. If you can’t draw your KPIs as a tree like this, you have a list, not a taxonomy.

A pipeline problem that looks like a business trend is the most expensive kind of wrong.

That is why data health gets its own tier. In a new product, a dropped event or a late batch shows up as “customer behaviour changed,” and somebody builds a strategy on it.

Every KPI gets a card. One page, versioned, stored where people can find it. Here is one filled in:

Of customers who completed a first reorder, the share who completed another within 30 days

repeated ÷ activated, by weekly cohort (view: m_repeat_reorder_30d)

Internal test accounts; reorders later refunded in full

v1.0, 2026-11-10. Every change needs a dated entry

A cohort is incomplete until 30 days have passed

The version line matters more than it looks. A chart that quietly changes meaning is the fastest way I know to burn an executive’s trust.

Ownership matters as much as the fields. The named owner approves any change to a definition, and the change log records who asked and why. It sounds bureaucratic until the first time two dashboards disagree and you can settle it in five minutes instead of five meetings.

The caveats line earns its place too. A 30-day metric for a cohort that activated 10 days ago has only seen a third of its window. Report it as final and you will “discover” a retention problem that is really just arithmetic. Only publish a cohort once its window has closed.

“We have no baseline” is a starting condition, not a verdict. You manufacture one, deliberately, in three moves.

Observe first. Say out loud that weeks one to six are for learning. Report the numbers, set no targets, and label the dashboard “baseline in progress.” Early users are your most curious users, so the first weeks flatter you. In the worked example, activation opens at 31%, peaks at 36% in week two, and settles near 26% by week six.

Freeze it as a range. Pool weeks 7 to 12: 6,550 customers saw the feature and 1,692 completed a first reorder. That gives a baseline of 25.8%, with a 95% interval of 24.8% to 26.9%. Publish that, dated and versioned, and measure change against it from then on. One more number to hand your stakeholders: a single week of about 1,100 viewers carries roughly ±2.5 points of noise on its own. A week at 24% against a baseline of 25.8% is not a decline. It’s Tuesday.

Know how much data you need. For a rate near 26%, the sample for a 95% interval is about n = 1.96² × p × (1 − p) ÷ e². Half-width ±3 points needs roughly 820 customers. ±2 points needs about 1,850. ±1 point needs about 7,400. If your weekly volume is smaller than that, widen the window instead of pretending the weekly number means something.

Somebody who screenshots the day-one number and puts it in a slide has just reported a drop that never happened. Two fixes. Show data-health metrics beside every KPI, and don’t publish a week until its late-arrival share has flattened. Then you don’t restate in public.

Borrow proxies, and label them. A similar feature, an industry figure or a pilot can give you a rough range before you have your own. Call it an assumption every time you show it.

Ship the event contract. Turn on the raw log and the data-health checks. Write the decision table.

Observe. Build the metric views. Write definition cards for the outcome and drivers.

KPIs labelled “baseline in progress”, no targets

Freeze the baseline as a range. Add guardrail thresholds. Version everything.

Review the taxonomy. Retire KPIs nobody used. Set the first targets.

Back to the executive in week three. You don’t have a baseline, but you can still give a useful answer. I use three sentences: what we know, how sure we are, and when we’ll know more.

“Activation over the last two weeks is 27.8%, give or take about 1.8 points. Early weeks run high because the people trying a new feature first are the keenest, so I expect it to settle nearer 26%. We’ll freeze a baseline at week seven and I’ll bring you a range then.”

That answer does something a bare number can’t. It says the figure is provisional, shows the uncertainty, predicts the direction of travel, and gives a date. (In the worked example those two weeks cover 2,450 customers who saw the feature, of whom 682 completed a first reorder.) Executives will forgive a number that moves if you told them it would. They won’t forgive one that moves without warning.

Decide before you measure. Store events, derive metrics. Tier your KPIs and give each one a card. Treat the first six weeks as baseline-building, then freeze a range and version it. Expect your first reads to be wrong and have a plan for restating them quietly.

A product with no baseline is uncomfortable. It’s also the only time you get to design the measurement properly from the start. Most teams only get that chance once.
