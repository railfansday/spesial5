Washington Commanders 2026 Week 10 Stats Roundup – Defense

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

This just-post mid-season, two-part stats roundup takes a look at what advanced stats and productivity metrics can tell us about how the Commanders' players compare to the rest of the league through Week 10 of the 2026 regular season.

The first part examined individual player performances on offense. Now we'll turn our attention to the defense. Apologies to special teams fanatics, I simply ran out of time. The following graph from RBSDM.com plots run defense efficiency against pass defense efficiency for every NFL team through Week 10:

Like on offense, the Commanders have been just a little below league average in efficiency in both phases on defense this season. Through Week 10, the run defense lags just slightly more than the pass defense. But neither aspect says championship contender. There have been a few more bright spots on defense than offense, although some have been muted by injury. But it won't come as any surprise that the standout performances have been outnumbered by mediocrity and worse.

Let's take a look at what the advanced stats say about the Commanders' individual player performances. I'll introduce the key metrics as I work through the player groups.

Edge defenders (ED) make crucial contributions in the pass rush and run defense. Advanced stats provide greater insight into player performance in both phases than traditional production totals.

Pass Rush Pressure Rate provides a more complete account of pass rush impact than sack totals. Sacks are fairly rare events. Their totals are prone to effects of sparseness, which can limit their utility as performance metrics. Pressures measure the impact of the pass rush beyond sacks, and provide more robust data sets to work with. Pressure rate in the pass rush is simply the percentage of pass rush attempts on which a defender generated a pressure.

As discussed in the OL section, scoring pressures has an element of subjectivity and requires making judgement calls. Consequently, even expert observers will have differences of opinion on some plays. To make matters worse, PFF and PFR clearly have different thresholds for counting hurries, which make up the majority of pressures. Consequently, it is not possible to cross check between sources. I have chosen to go with PFF pressure counts because their lower threshold results in larger data sets.

Run Stop Rate is a major improvement over tackle counts for measuring impact in run defense. It places tackles in context of down and distance to reveal their effect on drive success. It is not technically an advanced stat, since it simply repackages official stats in more meaningful form.

A stop is a tackle which results in a failure for the offense. It is defined as a tackle which holds the offense to under 40% of the yards to gain on first down, 50% on second down, or prevents a conversion or score on third or fourth down. Stops on later downs are potentially drive-ending plays, with similar impact to turnovers.

Run stop rate is the percentage of running plays on which a defender generated a stop. It is worth noting that it is an incomplete measure of an ED's impact run defense, since it does not reflect plays on which an ED forces the run inside. To the best of my knowledge, there is no quantitative metric for edge setting.

Odafe Oweh leads the Commanders in sack total (5), but lags his fellow EDs in generating pressure on a consistent basis. Different fans will look at that in different ways. He is also among the worst EDs in the NFL at generating run stops, though he still has some way to fall to reach the level of former Commanders Jacob Martin (2.6%, 73/74) and Chase Young (1.9%, 74/74).

Oweh's numbers have fallen off the cliff in his first season with Washington. In 2025, he generated a 13.8% pass rush pressure rate, which ranked 29th out of 120 EDs, and an 11.4% run stop rate, which ranked 3rd out of 120 EDs. Throughout his career, he has always been a productive run defender until this season.

K'Lavon Chaisson is second on the team with 3.5 sacks, but has been the most impactful ED in terms of generating pressure. He ranks a little above league average in that regard. His run stop rate is also down from previous seasons, but not as dramatically as Odafe's.

Dorance Armstrong's pressure rate this season has fallen from 15.4% prior to his season-ending injury in 2025. He has continued to be the best run stopper among the Commanders EDs, registering a 7.4% run stop rate, but only played 82 run defense snaps, which fell just short of criteria for inclusion in the ranking set. His run stop rate is just below the 75th percentile for EDs.

Joshua Josephs has played sparingly. To date, he has generated a 5.0% pressure rate in 60 pass rush attempts, and 4.4% run stop rate in 46 run defense snaps. The Commanders might be bringing him along slowly for a reason.

Commanders' interior defensive linemen were ranked by pass rush pressure rate among 96 iDL with 220 or more defensive snaps, and by run stop rate among 98 iDL with 98 or more run defense snaps.

Whatever one thinks about the value of his contract, Daron Payne was the Commanders' best interior defender in both phases before his season-ending injury. Through 6 games, he logged 7 tackles, 7 assists, 2 TFL, 1 FF, 1 sack, 5 QB hits, and 17 total pressures. His pressure rate sits at the 71st percentile for iDL, and just lagged teammate Charles Omenihu at the third decimal place. Payne is the team's top run stuffer, and still leads the Commanders with 10 run stops, despite missing three games.

Javon Kinlaw is not far behind Payne in run stop rate. He was less productive in the pass rush, before ending his season on IR.

Tim Settle's pressure rate seems fairly low compared to all iDL, but it's not bad for a nose tackle, whose job frequently involves making opportunities for teammates. He is more of a lane clogger than a pursuit defender in the run game.

Charles Omenihu gets less playing time than the starters, as a rotational DE. But he has consistently applied pressure in the pass rush, with 1 sack, 7 QB hits, and 16 total pressures to date. He generated stops on 4.0% of 76 run defense snaps, placing him at the bottom 10% for iDL.

Shy Tuttle was signed as the rotational nose tackle, but has taken on new responsibilities across the front following the loss of Payne and Kinlaw. His run stop rate is not bad for a nose tackle. He does not generate much pressure on the QB, directly.

Deatrich Wise has not played enough to qualify for the rankings. He has achieved a 5.6% pressure rate in the pass rush, and 4.8% run stop rate.

Johnny Newton has played just 41 defensive snaps in 2 games since returning from IR.

At the risk of sounding like a broken record, the way CB performance is usually measured just doesn't make sense. Interceptions are high impact plays, and pass breakups can be. But at best, they account for less than 1% of the coverage snaps of the league's most productive ballhawks. Where else in football do we put so much weight on a what a player does less than 1% of the time? Well, fumbles, but other than that, not often.

Receivers are usually evaluated based on receiving yardage. So it makes sense to base the primary metric of coverage performance on yardage containment. To bridge that gap, I have adopted Rate of Yardage Allowed (Yards/Coverage Snap) as my primary CB performance metric.

Rate of yardage allowed is an advanced stat, derived from film analysis. Assigning coverage yardage to defenders requires, without knowledge of coverage assignments, requires analysts to make judgement calls. Often, there is no clear correct answer, such as when receivers cross zones. Consequently, even expert observers will differ on marking some plays. In the weekly Stats and Snaps, I have taken to addressing this variability by cross-referencing between PFF and PFR, and only reporting numbers when there is some level of consensus. In this roundup, I am just reporting numbers PFF.

To readers some comfort, the often significant game-to-game variability in individual player coverage stats tends to average out across an entire season. Based on previous experience, by Week 10, only a small handful of CBs will have substantially different numbers between services.

That is not to say we can completely ignore interceptions, even though fewer than 50% of CBs make one in a season. To give them their due, I have included Interception Rate (INT%), calculated as the percentage of targets which are intercepted.

The final metric included for CBs and safeties is Stop Rate. Rather than breaking this down into run stop and pass stop rates, I decided to leave it at total defensive stop rate. My reasoning is that, stopping the run is an ancillary part of a CB's responsibility, while it is more important for some safeties. Containment of yardage gains after completed passes is a major and underappreciated part all DBs' rolses. Total stop rate tells us about containment impact on every down, and is therefore more reflective of a CB's overall performance on defense.

Commanders' CBs were ranked in all three categories among 77 CBs with 300 or more defensive snaps.

Free agent addition Amik Robertson is having a career-best season with the Commanders. This is the first time he has ever got below 1.0 Y/Cov Snp in coverage. While he has yet to make his first interception, he leads the team in pass breakups (10). He has also been good at containing gains after the catch and against the run. He ranks fourth on the team in combined tackles (53) and third in defensive stops (17). His 2.87% stop rate is at the 76th percentile for CBs, just edging ahead of Max Melton by a few thousandths of a percent.

Late offseason addition Rasul Douglas has been a pleasant surprise to many, but not yours truly. The 31 year old journeyman CB continues to perform well in coverage with his 6th NFL team. At 1.01 Y/Cov Snap, his rate of yardage allowed has been slightly above average for starting CBs. He also leads the team with 3 interceptions, and is in a three-way tie for second most INTs among CBs. He needs 1 more to catch up with the leaders. His defensive stop rate places him at the 65th percentile for the position.

In 4 games since returning from injury, Trey Amos has allowed the 2nd lowest rate of yardage among Commanders' CBs in coverage. His current rate of 0.84 Y/Cov Snap would place him at the 71st percentile for CBs, with more playing time. His 1.73% stop rate leaves some room for improvement.

Thankfully, Mike Sainristil missed the playing time cutoffs to appear in the table. In 7 games on defense and 2 starts, he allowed 1.68 Y/Cov Snap in coverage, ranking in the bottom 10% of CBs. His 2026 coverage numbers are consist with his first two seasons. Unlike his first two seasons, he has not made any interceptions or pass breakups. His 1.66% defensive stop rate is below average for CBs.

Backup Fabian Moreau also featured in my offseason free agent CBs roundup. He has only played 72 defensive snaps in 3 games, not enough to generate meaningful statistics. That said, if he could sustain his current 0.80 Y/Cov Snap rate of yardage allowed and 4.17% stop rate as a full-time starter, he would be Commanders best CB.

Commanders' safeties were ranked by the same metrics as CBs among 81 safeties with 250 or more defensive snaps.

When healthy, Nick Cross has been one of the Commander' best player on defense. His rate of yardage allowed in coverage seems fairly high. That is in part because he plays over 1/3 of his snaps in slot coverage, much more than the Commanders' other safeties. Nickel safeties have coverage stats closer to CBs than box and free-safeties. Cross is one of four Commanders with a INT this season. Cross is currently 6th on the team in combined tackles and stops, despite only playing 6 games. His 2.95% stop rate is at the 81st percentile for safeties.

Jeremy Reaves is the type of competitor who plays to the limit of his abilities. In seeming defiance of gripes about his athletic limitations, he has established that he is not the problem on defense. Reaves is second on the team in combined tackles (62) and third in defensive stops. According to PFF, Reaves' rate of yardage allowed in coverage sits at the 84th percentile for safeties (although, I do need to break protocol at this point to note that PFR has him closer to the 30th percentile at 0.71 Y/Cov Snap). Reaves has also been impactful as a tackler, with a 2.69% stop rate putting him at the 76th percentile for safeties.

Quan Martin has had an up and down season, but seems to be playing better in recent weeks. He plays mainly at free safety. For the season, his rate of yardage allowed ranks at the 37th percentile for safeties. He has only 1 PD and no interceptions. He ranks 5th on the team in combined tackles (43), but has only made 1 TFL and 4 total stops.

Percy Butler is mainly as special teams player, and has only played 36 defensive snaps.

Linebackers come last because I had to introduce all the metrics first, and it just didn't seem right introducing Y/Cov Snap for any other position than CBs.

Commanders' linebackers were ranked by run stop rate among 77 LBs with minimum 100 run defense snaps, by pass rush pressure rate among 69 LBs with minimum 20 pass rush attempts, and by rate of yardage allowed among 79 LBs with minimum 200 defensive snaps.

Sonny Styles has shown consistent improvement through his rookie season. His stats from recent games look better than the cumulative season stats. Even so, the season stats are not bad. Styles currently leads the Commanders in combined tackles (80), stops (24), and run stops (14). His 7.3% run stop rate is exactly on league average for LBs. His pressure rate on blitzes is just above average. And his rate of yardage allowed in coverage is just below average.

While Styles does not stand out by any individual metric yet, it is worth pointing out that few linebackers are good in all three phases on defense represented here. Most are good at some aspects of the position and not others (e.g. Nate Landman). To be performing at the level of average starters in run defense, pass rush and coverage, midway through his rookie season is very promising. But I suspect the best is yet to come.

Frankie Luvu is third on the team in combined tackles (55) and second in defensive stops (22). His run stop rate is just slightly ahead of Styles, for now. He has flashed his pass rush ability at times, with 2 sacks, 4 QB hits and 10 total pressures in 7 games. But his overall pressure rate has been below average. He hasn't been a liability in coverage, but his rate of yardage allowed is a little below average.

Leo Chenal's season was cut short in Week 3 by a horrific neck injury. Prior to that, he was producing at elite pace, with an off-the-charts 14.7% run stop rate. Kain Medrano, Jordan Magee and Ale Kaho have played too little to generate reliable statistics.
