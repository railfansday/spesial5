How I Got Benighted (Twice) on Yosemite’s ‘Lost Arrow Chimney’

Tanggal: 2026-11-04

In the first installment in our Benighted series, Shino Jomoto shares with Climbing the weird and spooky tale from her night in the chimney on September 17, 2023—and what all climbers can learn from her epic.

With over two decades of climbing experience, Jomoto specializes in epics, which she defines as getting sandbagged on adventure multi-pitch climbs and big walls that take far longer than anticipated. But her biggest epic of all occurred in the storied chimney route (5.10a) up Lost Arrow Spire in Yosemite.

Earlier this week, a Yosemite Climbing Association (YCA) newsletter detailed the history of the Lost Arrow Spire, including the chimney route. First ascensionist John Salathé himself bailed near the top in 1946. The next year, he came back “for an all-out effort” that took over five days. “Pushing through the claustrophobic shadows of the Lost Arrow Chimney, Salathé and [Anton] Nelson endured searing heat, dehydration, and brutal physical exertion,” the YCA shared. “They spent four bivouacs dangling from crude hammocks tied to pitons driven into the wall.”

The Lost Arrow Chimney is also believed to contain a human skeleton. On March 19, 1961, 17-year-old Irving Smith died in a rappelling accident from the top. An American Alpine Club trip report shared that Smiths’ body remains in the chimney: “While a ranger and volunteer rescue party was preparing to attempt the difficult and dangerous removal, Smith’s parents and Park authorities determined that no further life should be risked, and that the body should remain at rest where it lay.”

A comment on the Mountain Project page of Yosemite’s Lost Arrow Chimney (5.10a) foreshadowed what was to come: “Don’t let the moderate grade fool you. After pitch 7, you are halfway done.” The Lost Arrow Chimney is 10 pitches.

Poor fitness, dehydration, and sleep deprivation conspired, and by pitch eight we needed headlamps. Too tired to continue, we did the only sensible thing: slept inside the chimney.

I started climbing in 2005, mostly out of a hatred for my corporate job. That led to years living out of a station wagon, trying to figure out how to rock climb. Fast forward decades later, I’m in the business of going on epics—a lot.

Jim Herson said you could write an entire print edition on just my epics alone. I’ve had near-death epics like lightning and hypothermia on a big wall. But I’ll put it this way: I can climb El Cap faster than I can climb the Lost Arrow Chimney.

The reason why I wanted to share this is that nine out of ten climbers will have an epic on the Lost Arrow. You might be able to climb 5.13, but try to climb 10 pitches of old-school chimney—see if you can still call yourself a 5.10 climber. I think everyone goes in with the confidence of the grade. Then they just get gut-punched by the old-schoolness of the climb.

On my Lost Arrow Chimney epic, we weren’t in danger. We never called YOSAR (Yosemite Search and Rescue). You’re just humbled in a silly way because it’s such a silly grade.

Aside from the likes of Chuck Pratt or old-school climbers, I don’t know anyone who’s ever redone this route. When you do a climb, you usually redo it. You want a redpoint or you go back again. Nobody is dumb enough to redo this climb. We’ve done it three times, so it really goes to show either how incredibly stupid we are, or how memorable of an epic it was that we just needed to clear the record.

The lore that Jim Herson shared with me was he knew a guy who climbed the Lost Arrow Chimney and then never rock climbed again. The reality is he went to med school and gave up climbing. But that was his last-ever climb. It just embodies the spirit of the Lost Arrow Chimney.

The first time John and I did Lost Arrow Chimney in 26 hours on October 5, 2019 as a party of three, and we were actually in decent shape.

The time we slept in the chimney a few years later, we were a party of two. We thought we were fit. But we did all the classic gumby moves. We got up too late and got lost on the approach. To get back on route, we had to rappel. It’s obvious on a topo where you should go, but at four o’clock in the morning, you get lost. And we didn’t bring enough water.

On the climb, we started losing daylight. My boyfriend started dry heaving from dehydration and lack of fitness. He stopped mid-pitch in a chimney—he couldn’t go on.

There’s nothing you can really say in the dark. We’re not Rocky Talkie people. We have too much pride for that. So we just had to communicate with a rope. Finally, I was on belay. I went up to him. We were mid-chimney—this wasn’t even a belay station.

John was like, “I can’t. I can’t do this anymore.” And he’s a 10-times better climber than I am. So I was like, “All right, I’ll go.” He was like, “Are you sure?” I was like, “We can’t sleep here. We can sleep in a lot of places. This is not a place to sleep.”

It’s always scary when a very experienced person says, “I’m scared.” John rarely says he’s scared. It takes courage to say that. When you’re in the dark, there’s nothing you do. You can’t start yelling and placating, you know? You just realize in that moment like, it’s a serious climb. And you’re still having to reconcile that with 10a.

So I went questing and found this chimney that was adjacent to the climb. It wasn’t a chimney you go through on route, but everyone goes by it.

We were two pitches from the notch. Should we suffer for six hours, then rappel and call it a day? Or suffer through the whole night? You make those calculations. We decided we might as well stay put, rather than burden resources to call for YOSAR or any sort of search and rescue. I think that’s the number one thing: Can you self-rescue?

We had brought a salad that we thought we were going to eat at the base. So we ended up at the bivy with a salad, but no water. It’s kind of hard to eat a salad without water. So all of my swear words were salad- or foliage-related. It doesn’t matter if you have a whole buffet, if you have no water, you don’t want to eat anything. So we were just staring at this salad in the chimney. It’s one of those things with the Maslow’s hierarchy—if you don’t have water, you can’t do anything.

We laid out both climbing ropes so that we were a little bit insulated from the ground. We did the bivy dance. It was flatter than most bivy spots I have slept on. We were really depressed, just waiting for daylight.

Back sleeping was impossible, so we were relegated to side sleeping for the next six hours. There was no way to sleep besides big spoon, little spoon. The walls were very cold. I put a giant sad face in chalk where I was. Another group went and did it later and said, “We knew where you bivvied ’cause there was a giant sad face.”

But we were so thirsty, the epic wasn’t over. We could hear Yosemite Falls. Our brains went: Maybe we can just hike over there. Another lesson is you’re not rational when you’re having an epic. So we lost another half day going to the falls. We were sitting in a pool of water that we couldn’t drink. And we realized we couldn’t cut over. So we had to go back. A day and a half later, we got back to the car.

Finally, on June 2, 2024, we got our redemption. We did it in a day and tagged the summit where people do the Tyrolean. It still took us all day—and we didn’t even free it. We had to pull on gear. It makes you respect the history of the first ascensionists—the lineage of people who have tried it and who have bailed. There’s an insane amount of really, really old bail slings.

You’re in there and it’s a vortex. It’s spooky. I’m not a mystical person, but there are hexes that have been kind of swallowed by the rock. It’s a freaking spooky museum of gear. When you’re in there, you have an appreciation for who climbed this before you and the style in which they climbed it, because it is terrifying.

I feel like I’ve gotten my chimney badge in Yosemite. There should be a club for it: the Lost Arrow Chimney Support Group. If you can summit the whole climb and rappel in a day, I would have mad respect. More people climb the Nose than the Lost Arrow Chimney. Anyone who’s dumb enough and adventurous enough to sign up for that level of masochism will resonate more with me than if you’re C1ing a 30-pitch climb.

If you survive nine or ten pitches of chimneys with your partner, you will be bonded forever, for better or worse. They’ve done all these studies about reality TV shows, like for love matchmaking. You put people in intense situations and they confuse their endorphins as romance, but it’s really just adrenaline. Part of it is trauma bonding. Some couples are more willing to climb the Lost Arrow Chimney than go to couples therapy.

The beauty of epics is there are way more fun memories than sending. Every sending story is so ephemeral. But when you’ve been sucker-punched so hard by something scary, or that requires lack of sleep, or incredible amounts of suffering, you remember those experiences for decades.

If you don’t have some delusional optimism, you will never get off the ground. That delusion will catch up to you at some point. But you have to have a little bit of fuel. As long as it’s not over your head or dangerous for you and others. Can you live well and safely enough to live another day? Go do it.

Delusion meets reality at some point, like when you’re having an epic. Benighted is a subcategory of epics—it’s in the taxonomy. There has to be some darkness involved, generally speaking, but you don’t need to have slept.

The easy way to know is when you end up at the parking lot, you see your best friends, and they’re like, “How was it?” The face doesn’t lie. You actually don’t need to say words. They see your face and they’re like, “That was an epic.”

John and I have an epic grading scale. It’s the reverse GPA.

Every time we finish, we rate them. The Lost Arrow Chimney was like a 5.0.

As told to Maya Silver. This story has been edited for length, structure, and clarity.
