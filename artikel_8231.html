Appalachian Trail Gear Breakdown (Part 2)

Tanggal: 2026-11-16

Here is Part 2 of my Appalachian Trail Gear Breakdown. Did it take me a month to write and finally publish this post? Yes. Do I have an excuse? No.

The T-shirt I wore for the first 500 miles was from Patagonia’s Capilene Cool line, though I’m not sure what material blend it was made of. By the time I reached Damascus, it was already in tatters, so I switched to the SmartWool Merino Short Sleeve Tee.

I was stoked about this purchase, but those who read my Day 44 post know mice chewed holes in it almost immediately. I still wore it for the remainder of the hike, and loved it. It was lightweight, breathable, and odor-resistant. It needed to be tossed by the time I finished the AT (partly from the mice, and partly from wear and tear), but I would repurchase one for a future thru-hike.

My biker shorts and sports bra were cheap Walmart purchases, but both have held up incredibly well. The Avia sports bra was only $9 but is still in perfect condition after 2200 miles. It was a spandex-and-polyester blend and NEVER STUNK, though I know that’s incredibly hard to believe.

I ran in my shorts for a few months before starting the trail, so by mile 600 they had holes along the seams. I bought the exact same pair from the Pearisburg Walmart to replace them, and they lasted the rest of the trail with no issues.

My fleece hoodie was from Squak, a small cottage brand based in Utah, and I found it to be warm, fairly lightweight, and very affordable. I never hiked in it, always keeping it 100% dry to wear as my baselayer at camp. My puffy jacket was the Patagonia Nano Puff, and I often wore this over my fleece on cold nights, or even while hiking on particularly chilly mornings.

The Nano Puff used synthetic insulation rather than down, making it slightly heavier but better at retaining heat when exposed to moisture from rain or heavy sweat. I found this beneficial for a trail as wet and humid as the AT. My only complaint is that the jacket did not have a hood, and I felt like I lost warmth without one.

I sent my fleece home in Daleville, VA (late May), and never felt like I needed it after that. My puffy jacket stayed with me the entire hike, and I think it’s important to keep one warm layer for the whole hike (even in the middle of summer). You just never know when you might need it.

Frogg Toggs were my rain gear of choice for my entire AT thru-hike, and I found them affordable and effective. The rain jacket was a great outer shell on cold days, though it traps a lot of moisture when you sweat. I wore the rain pants for the first time at Neel Gap (Day 5), but they ripped up the butt after about five minutes.

Other hikers said this is common, and I never replaced them. My advice would be to buy only the jacket and skip the rain pants altogether (but to each their own). I replaced the jacket once in Harpers Ferry, WV, and it was so cheap at Walmart that it was no sweat. You can also routinely patch small holes in the fabric with Leukotape.

I brought three pairs of socks for the trail, slept in one pair, and alternated the other two for hiking. Darn Toughs are the most popular option, and the lifetime warranty is definitely a perk. Numerous outfitters along the trail let you exchange worn-out pairs, no questions asked. Merino wool wicks away moisture and keeps your feet warm even when wet.

My trail runners were the Salomon XA Pro 3D V9 (say that five times fast). I first picked these up in Damascus, VA, and went through three pairs. For those interested in my journey to finding the perfect shoe, I wrote a full post about it (Day 99).

My camp shoes were Xero sandals, and it was so nice to have something lightweight and breathable to wear each night. An added bonus of camp shoes is that they keep your trail runners dry through water crossings and give you something to wear when soaking sore feet in creeks and streams.

Last, but certainly not least, is my beloved AT buff. I got this for free at Outdoor 76 in Franklin, NC. This was the town where I stayed in my first hostel on the trail, and took my friend Cesare to the ER for his broken wrist. I started wearing it on Day 14 and didn’t take it off until after summiting Mount Katahdin (except for the occasional wash). It will likely come with me on any future hikes.

I collected water from natural sources in a 2L CNOC Vecto, then filtered it through a Sawyer Squeeze into two “clean” 1L Smartwater bottles. This method worked well and was what I noticed most other hikers doing. When I started the trail, I didn’t have the CNOC, so I collected water in one “dirty” Smartwater bottle and filtered it into one “clean” Smartwater bottle.

This was annoying because the bottles are made of hard plastic, and squeezing water through the filter makes them crinkle and collapse. The bottle’s suction also frequently pulled the O-ring out of my Sawyer Squeeze, which it needs to filter correctly. I didn’t have any of these problems when I switched to the CNOC Vecto.

It’s a good idea to carry a few extra Aquatabs in your first aid kit just in case something happens to your water filter. It’ll taste like you’re drinking pool water, but at least you won’t risk getting sick. Aquatabs also kill norovirus, which the Sawyer Squeeze won’t remove. I used the tabs for a few days on the trail when everyone was getting sick, and I was worried that the water might be contaminated.

My hygiene kit included a contact case, toothbrush, tweezers, nail clippers, one pair of glasses, and travel-sizes of toothpaste, Dr. Bronner’s unscented soap, hand sanitizer, and a hairbrush.

This suited my needs, and I wouldn’t remove anything from my kit. The only item I would consider adding is a case for my glasses. They were scratched and basically destroyed by the end of the hike.

In my first aid kit, I kept a tick key, EpiPen, extra contacts (enough for the entire hike), my personal medications (all in labeled Ziploc bags), Tylenol, Aleve, triple antibiotic ointment, diclofenac gel, Band-Aids, Leukotape, and travel-sized bug spray. I did not use sunscreen on my thru-hike (bad, I know).

For pain relievers, I think it’s wise to keep both an NSAID (Aleve or Advil) and a non-NSAID (Tylenol) on hand so that you can diversify what you use. I probably prefer Aleve to Advil because it lasts 12 hours instead of 8. Diclofenac gel is also an NSAID, so it can act as a topical anti-inflammatory with less absorption into the bloodstream and less stomach irritation than oral pills (it worked great for my bursitis).

A small tube of triple antibiotic ointment helps prevent blisters and bug bites from getting infected. Band-Aids work for scratches, cuts, and blisters, and act as a good buffer between Leukotape and damaged skin. Leukotape holds bandages in place for long periods and can also repair gear and clothing.

My advice would be to start with a three-month supply of your personal medications, because the pills weigh very little and getting off the trail to pick up prescriptions is a headache. This way, you will only need to resupply once during a thru-hike.

If you wear monthly or biweekly contact lenses (this won’t work for daily lenses), I recommend bringing a supply for roughly 6 months on the trail. Again, they weigh almost nothing and can fit in a small first aid kit. I was glad I didn’t have to worry about picking these up in a resupply box.

Within the first week on the trail, I ditched both my Kula Cloth and trowel. I never really reached for my Kula Cloth and found the whole concept a little gross (though I had friends who loved them). I much preferred packing out baby wipes I could use for numbers 1 & 2 and to wipe my legs at the end of the day. Instead of a trowel, I used my trekking pole, hands, or a stick to dig a cat hole.

For “personal protection,” I carried a pepper spray keychain on the front strap of my backpack. I never felt like I needed it, and considered throwing it away many times. But I think it gave me peace of mind on nights I camped alone.

Most hikers carry a Garmin inReach satellite communicator. You will likely have cell service at least once every day that you spend on the trail (with some rare exceptions). But I still had many nights camping without cell service. The Garmin was useful for sending my husband the occasional satellite text with my sleeping location and who I was with. It’s obviously also beneficial because you can hit the SOS button in an emergency, and rescue crews can locate you.

Per my Gear List, my base weight was 15.2 lb. Obviously, the gear you carry over a thru-hike varies by season, weather, etc. I sent home my mid-layer hoodie and wool leggings at the beginning of June, which lightened my load. But some weeks I also carried nail polish and temporary tattoos (lol), among other frivolous items.

I do think it’s important to carry as little weight as possible to prevent injury. You can really feel even just a few extra pounds when climbing mountains all day. But also, hikers can go overboard with the ultralight obsession. There is definitely a happy medium between weight, comfort, and having fun. Each individual has to figure out what that is for them.
