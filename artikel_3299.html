Apple among the companies facing pressure from US senators over ‘nudify’ apps [U: Apple, TTP respond]

Tanggal: 2026-11-23

The Guardian reports that three US senators have sent letters to the CEOs of Apple, Google, and Meta, demanding answers about their failure to prevent access to AI-powered ‘nudify’ apps. Here are the details.

Last month, Apple updated its App Store rules in an attempt to crack down on apps that enable users to generate nonconsensual nude images.

According to the updated rules, apps that allow users to create AI-generated content must include safeguards against abuse, including tools to filter objectionable material, report offensive content, and block abusive users.

Apple also warns that apps used primarily for pornographic content or the objectification of real people may be removed from the App Store without notice.

While definitely well-intended, Apple’s changes came amid growing reports of AI-powered nudify apps, which easily allow users to generate nonconsensual intimate images.

Two months before these changes, Apple removed an app that allowed users to create pornographic deepfakes of real people after WIRED reached out for comment, which in turn came one month after San Francisco City Attorney David Chiu demanded that Apple and Google remove several nudify apps from their app stores.

Apple also removed several apps following investigations by the Tech Transparency Project (TTP), which published reports in January and April revealing that such apps were widely available on the App Store, sometimes even appearing in sponsored search results.

Throughout these investigations, Apple has maintained that nudify apps have always been prohibited under its App Review Guidelines, and that it proactively rejects and removes apps that violate its policies, including those flagged through user reports.

Apple, of course, is not alone in this challenge, with Google facing similar criticism over their availability on the Play Store, and Meta coming under scrutiny for allowing developers to advertise these apps on Facebook and Instagram, as highlighted in the WIRED report mentioned above.

Which brings us to today’s report from The Guardian.

According to The Guardian’s exclusive report, “three Senate Democrats sent letters to tech CEOs saying safeguards against nudification apps are insufficient on their platforms.”

A trio of US Senators say Apple and Google are failing to effectively detect and remove apps that can be used to generate nonconsensual nude or pornographic imagery. They also say Meta is continuing to profit off of ads for these so-called nudification apps that “victimize women and girls worldwide”.

The senators, namely Ron Wyden of Oregon, Ed Markey of Massachusetts, and Ben Luján of New Mexico, claim that in addition to failing to remove these apps, the companies may actually be profiting from their distribution and promotion.

Commenting on the letter, both Google and Meta said they prohibit such apps or their promotion and work to detect and remove violations from their platforms, broadly echoing what Apple has repeatedly stated when reached for comment in the past over these same issues.

However, Wyden’s office argues that simply reaffirming, or in Apple’s case, updating these policies, means little if the companies continue to struggle to enforce them.

Keith Chu, the chief communications adviser and deputy policy director to Wyden, said that clarifying policies is not the same thing as enforcing them. Despite the companies reaffirming the prohibition of these apps, they continue to proliferate across Apple and Google app stores or be promoted in ads on Meta platforms, he added.

In its latest investigation, published just last month, TTP found 135 apps capable of generating nude or pornographic imagery on the App Store and the Play Store, using accounts registered to 13-year-old boys.

Additionally, researchers found that even accounts configured with parental controls were able to download these apps without requiring parental approval.

Despite Apple and Google removing the apps identified in the investigation, The Guardian noted that these apps continued to work on devices where they had already been installed.

Now, Senator Wyden warns that Congress may need to step in if the companies fail to do a better job of cracking down on these apps.

Update, 8:40 p.m. ET: In a statement to 9to5Mac, Apple said:

“We designed the App Store to be a safe and trusted place for users. A number of the apps in the TTP report are mainstream photo and video editing tools with legitimate, widely used functionality, and do not have nudification or deepfake capabilities. We prohibit nudify apps on the App Store, and remove developers who add capabilities like it after the App Review process — a malicious tactic known as bait-and-switch. We previously removed 28 of the apps referenced in this report before it was shared with us and have taken action on the remaining apps found to violate our rules. The report also relies on testing from older software that does not reflect current Apple safety features, including expanded protections for teens and parental controls in Screen Time that give families the ability to block kids and teens from downloading apps rated above their age.”

Expanding on the safety features mentioned in its statement, Apple noted that teens aged 13 through 17 now have certain protections enabled by default, including web content filters, even if their parents haven’t configured any restrictions, and pointed to ⁠this support article for more information.

The company added that children under 13 are required to use Child Accounts, and that the report relied on its previous 17+ age rating system, which was replaced last year with a more granular system that includes 13+, 16+, and 18+ ratings.

Apple also noted that in its report, TTP counted apps depicting people in swimwear or lingerie, which the company said does not constitute nudification or, in itself, violate its guidelines.

As for parental controls, Apple explained that, although apps that exceed a child’s configured age restrictions won’t appear in curated App Store sections, they can still be found through search, allowing children to request permission to download them through Ask to Buy. Without parental approval, the download remains blocked.

Apple added that Ask to Buy is supposed to require parental approval even for purchases made using gift card balances, including subscriptions. The company said that if a purchase didn’t trigger an approval request, that would be unexpected behavior, and that it is investigating further.

Update, November 19, 4:00 p.m. ET: In response to Apple’s statement, TTP director Katie Paul reached out to 9to5Mac with the following comment:

Apple’s statement on nudify apps sidesteps important issues and leaves out key context. The company says it prohibits nudify apps but doesn’t address how its age rating system for apps contributes to the problem. TTP has shown how Apple relies on information provided by app developers to set age ratings—a self-reporting system that allows many nudify and pornographic apps to be misleadingly labeled a suitable for minors. Apple also continues to talk about removing nudify apps from the App Store, without mentioning that apps that are “removed” continue to be fully functional on people’s phones.

There are many questions about Apple’s policy on nudify apps that the statement fails to address. Apple has adopted a limited definition of “nudify.” The company defines nudify apps as capable of showing someone naked, not stripping them down to a bikini or lingerie. But the company also says it bans “creepy” and “nonconsensual intimate imagery.” What if an app is used to depict a woman in a bikini without her consent? We have asked Apple to clarify that and have not heard back.

As for Apple’s comment that TTP relied on “older software,” that may refer to the fact that Apple rolled out new child safety features in the fall. Our research was conducted in summer 2026. But many of Apple’s new features pertain to parent-supervised accounts on Apple, which have to be set up by a parent. Apple relies entirely on parents to go out of their way to locate and enable these settings. This raises obvious questions about how many people actually do this.
