Bitcoin ETFs Lose $2.35 Billion in Two Weeks as Outflow Streak Extends

US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds have now suffered two consecutive weeks of withdrawals exceeding $1 billion, a remarkable reversal for products often treated as the clearest gauge of institutional demand. The latest weekly tally also extends the run of daily redemptions to seven trading sessions. That does not mean every institutional holder is selling Bitcoin, but it does remove one of the market's more visible sources of buying support.

The numbers cover the trading week ending Friday, November 13, and are based on reported fund-flow data. They should not be confused with changes in fund valuations caused by Bitcoin's falling price.

According to The Block's analysis of SoSoValue data, US spot Bitcoin ETFs registered approximately $1.07 billion of net outflows for the week. The previous week had already recorded around $1.28 billion, putting the combined two-week figure at $2.35 billion. Seven consecutive negative trading sessions accounted for roughly $1.91 billion of the retreat.

The daily pattern is revealing: withdrawals were not limited to one frightening session. Monday through Friday each ended in the red, with Wednesday and Thursday particularly heavy. NewsBTC covered the earlier five-day outflow streak, but the completed week's figures show that investors had not yet turned the tap back on.

BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust, IBIT, accounted for about $143.5 million of Friday's approximately $185.5 million in net withdrawals. Fidelity's FBTC made up roughly $41.9 million, while the other funds tracked reported no net flows for the session, according to the same dataset. It is tempting to read that as a judgement on either manager, but the more plausible interpretation is that investors use the largest and most liquid vehicles when adjusting exposure quickly.

The fact that a particular ETF experiences redemptions does not tell us whether shares were held by hedge funds, retail brokerage customers or other institutions. Nor does it reveal their reasons for selling. Portfolio rebalancing, risk limits and macro hedges can look identical in an aggregate daily flow table.

Bitcoin trades around the clock on spot exchanges and in derivatives markets. US-listed ETFs operate during market hours, and their creation-and-redemption processes connect the share market with underlying Bitcoin exposure. ETF data therefore offers a useful window, not a complete map, of demand. Large holders outside the products can still buy while funds lose money, and vice versa.

There is also a distinction between a withdrawal and a loss. If Bitcoin falls while ETF shares are held, the value of assets declines even without net redemptions. Conversely, a fund can experience inflows during a falling market. Keeping those measurements separate makes analysis much more useful than treating any red number as interchangeable.

The first signal to watch is whether the next US trading week brings even a modest return to positive daily flows. A second is the distribution across products: broad-based inflows would suggest more than one investor cohort is stepping back in. Finally, price stability without significant ETF buying would show whether the market has found demand elsewhere.

The broader institutional picture is not limited to daily flows. Changes affecting companies such as Strategy and Metaplanet underline how benchmark decisions and portfolio rules can influence crypto-linked exposure. For now, the sustained outflows are a warning about current appetite, not proof that the long-term ETF market has failed.
