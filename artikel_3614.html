Let’s Throw “Maxxing” Into the Dustbin of History

Tanggal: 2026-11-08

There was a land of Usenet forums and AOL keywords, LiveJournals and Blogspots and single-topic Tumblrs. It was called the internet, and it was a patchwork quilt sewn together by delightful little freaks—a collective fabric woven from psychologically damaged but individually discernible threads.

With time, that changed. “It had also once been the place where you sounded like yourself,” Patricia Lockwood, poet laureate of the terminally online, wrote of the internet in her wonderfully bizarre 2021 novel No One Is Talking About This. “Gradually it had become the place where we sounded like each other.”

Never has that been truer than in the wee months of 2026, when everyone suddenly woke up, unlocked their glowing rectangles, and decided to start talking like supplement-addled bridge trolls. Mogging, gooning, bonesmashing, mewing: Without rhyme, reason, or the public’s consent (funny thing about that!), incel language had fully colonized our speech. And after the takeover, no neologism was more prominent than the all-purpose suffix “-maxxing.”

We were not talking about what Cher Horowitz might do with her father’s credit card. We were using a slangier term for optimization, attaching it willy-nilly to any nouns or verbs it could plausibly describe. Working out became gymmaxxing; eating too many beans became fibermaxxing; neglecting to look at your phone for eight whole hours became sleepmaxxing. Like all good colloquialisms, the word was adaptable and edgy and undeniably fun to say. It’s got those playful and lightly pornographic double x’s, the push-pull of a hard k sound—famously the most inherently funny consonant—followed by soft sibilance and a literal “zing.”

But after a full 12 months of maxxing, I am more than ready to minn. And not least because no amount of overuse can obscure the ugly origin of 2026’s favorite syntaxx.

Back in the Gamergate era, maxxing was coined by pathetic losers on websites with names like SlutHate, where they used it to describe how they were hoping to attract women: enhancing their appearances through looksmaxxing, enhancing their social status by moneymaxxing. (Perhaps they could have started by showermaxxing.)

True, innumerable everyday expressions have uncomfortable connotations. Just ask anyone who’s absentmindedly used one of them before pausing to frantically google “Is ‘gung ho’ racist?” (answer: yes, but not in the way you might think) or “butthurt word homophobic??” (fortunately, it’s actually not). But most of those questionable expressions weren’t invented five minutes ago. It’s reasonable to expect the hoi polloi not to know the roots of words that have been grandfathered into the lexicon. Maxxing’s upsetting genesis, though, is easily traceable—and anyone who’s online enough to use it is also probably well-informed enough to know better. (Incidentally, “grandfathered in”? Also racist!)

And then, of course, there’s the death knell of all slang: repetition and co-option by both corporations and figurative bad actors. The fun was over the moment the Cheesecake Factory’s official Instagram account advised its 1.1 million followers to try “brown bread-maxxing.” It was ready for last rites by the time Trump’s Department of Homeland Security tweeted about its intention to engage in “deportationmaxing.” (Yes, with one x; given who authors these posts, you’d think they could have spelled it right.) And it was really in the ground two days after that, when The Wall Street Journal’s X account published this sentence: “The era of AI ‘tokenmaxxing’ in corporate America is over. Companies are now embracing ‘thrift-maxxing.’” At this point, we are dead horse–maxxing every time we insert the term into a new sentence, even—especially—if we’re trying to use it ironically.

Language is contagious, particularly for those who live online. In No One Is Talking About This, Lockwood bemoans internet-speak’s unparalleled ability to infect its users: “It was so tiring to have to catch each new virus, produce the perfect sneeze of it, and then mutate it into something new.” But here’s the thing: Protective measures can be taken. If your phone still autocorrects this particular word to “max inc,” there is hope. The bad news, of course, is that by the time you read this, it’s entirely possible an even worse word will have clawed out of the dark web to take its place.
