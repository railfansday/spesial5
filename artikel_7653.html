Further Discussion of Whether Current Second Amendment Jurisprudence Is Based on a "Misquote"

Tanggal: 2026-11-26

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A podcast discussion of my blogpost, John Bash's critique of a controversial NYT story, and the NYT's response to criticism.

I recently appeared on an episode of the Reload podcast, hosted by Reload founder and editor Stephen Gutowski, to discuss my blog post taking aim at the New York Times story alleging current Second Amendment jurisprudence is based upon a "misquote" of Blackstone.

As noted in my original post, the NYT story was harshly criticized by attorney John Bash, a former clerk to Justice Scalia during the term Heller was decided who was interviewed (but not quoted) in the story. As also noted in that post, the Times issued a response to my post. As discussed in the podcast, I did not find the response particularly persuasive, nor did John Bash.

As I note on the podcast, there are perfectly reasonable arguments that current Second Amendment jurisprudence has gone astray, and that some courts have placed undue weight on the contested passage in the Heller opinion (which did not purport to set out a test). But these are separate questions from whether there was a "misquote" in the Heller opinion. It seems to me the obsession with the latter derives from a desire to discredit contemporary gun rights jurisprudence by suggesting it all stems from a mistake (or worse), rather than by contesting it on the merits.

As for the Blackstone source at issue, just as the Times did not place the contested Heller passage in its full context so that readers could see the point Scalia was making, it did not highlight the full Blackstone passage (other than providing a hyperlink in the web version of the story) or unpack the point Blackstone was making--a point that is arguably even less favorable to gun ownership restrictions than is the relevant passage in Heller.

in his seminal 1769 treatise, "Commentaries on the Laws of England," Blackstone referred to weapons that were "dangerous or unusual," suggesting that a gun did not need to be both in order to be restricted.

The offence of riding or going armed, with dangerous or unusual weapons, is a crime against the public peace, by terrifying the good people of the land; and is particularly prohibited by the statute of Northampton, upon pair of forfeiture of the arms, and imprisonment during the king's pleasure: in like manner as, by the laws of Solon, every Athenian was finable who walked about the city in armour.

Those who want gun rights jurisprudence to hew more closely to Blackstone than does Heller want to be careful what they wish for, as it might not be much authority at all for laws prohibiting the mere possession of unusually dangerous weapons (such as those the Supreme Court will consider this term), as opposed to laws prohibiting the carrying of such arms in ways that could "terrify[]." The Times does not "misquote" Blackstone, but some might conclude the Times was not fairly representing what Blackstone wrote either.
