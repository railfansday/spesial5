These 10 Editor-Approved E-MTBs Deliver Real Trail Capability

Tanggal: 2026-11-14

Trail-ready electric mountain bikes for every budget, from hardtails to high-power builds.

Autumn is our favorite season for mountain biking. The air is crisp, the dirt is grippy, and we can’t wait to head to the singletrack after work and on weekends for great rides. And more than ever, like many other mountain bikers, we’re choosing E-MTBs for our rides. Electric mountain bikes help you maximize those rides and spend more time on your favorite trails.

Today’s electric mountain bikes use lightweight motors and batteries, making them super capable and amazingly fun on the trail. And while heavier than traditional mountain bikes, these pedal-assist models allow you to cover more miles, ride faster, and tackle challenging climbs.

E-MTBs are engineered for performance on singletrack trails and deep in the woods. And while their low-power motors provide a boost when pedalling, E-MTBs are not electric motorcycles. You still have to pedal an electric mountain bike; you can’t simply twist a throttle and blast down the trail.

The best E-MTBs blend mountain bike components, suspension, and geometry with electronic technology in a brilliant fashion. Like non-electric mountain bikes, some E-MTBs blur the lines of XC and lightweight trail bikes, while others are burlier and take suspension and geometry cues from the top-performing enduro and downhill racing mountain bikes.

Aventon recently launched the Current, the brand’s first full-suspension E-MTB, and it is a gem! The Current ADV offers incredible value, and it’s trail-ready right out of the box. Trevor Raab, Bicycling’s Senior Photographer and Test Team member, said, “The bike’s intuitive ride feel will please experienced mountain bikers and also benefit first-time mountain bikers. The Current’s motor and interface provide riders with a wide range of tuning options and lots of data.”

The Current ADV features an aluminum frame with 140mm rear wheel travel and a great-feeling motor boasting 750 peak Watts and 110Nm torque. (The Current also has a Boost mode that bumps power to 850W and torque to 120Nm.) Rounding out the ADV are a RockShox Psylo Gold fork, SRAM DB8 brakes, SRAM Eagle 70 Mechanical transmission, and a tubeless wheelset with Maxxis Minion EXO tires.

In his full review of the bike, “Aventon Goes Full Performance: The Current Electric Mountain Bike Is Surprisingly Dialed,” Raab especially likes the Current’s intuitive and easy-to-use interface. It’s loaded with ride data and the ability to tune the motor settings.

Want a carbon frame and electronic drivetrain? The Current EXP is a great package for $5,999.

Specialized’s Turbo motor is a benchmark for electric mountain bike performance. It has plenty of power and torque, and the Morgan Hill, California-based brand has updated, polished, and refined the motor over several years. However, the biggest downside to Specialized E-MTBs has been their very steep starting price. But that has changed.

Specialized’s newest Levo model brings the platform to a more palatable $5,000 price. The Turbo Levo 4 Alloy uses the same motor and battery as the pricier carbon-chassis Expert model but with an aluminum frame, heavier components, and mechanical shifting to save pennies. The brand bolts its 105Nm torque and 810-watt Turbo 3.1 motor to a 150mm-travel, full-suspension aluminum frame and equips the bike with value-packed parts. SRAM brakes, a mechanical drivetrain, and Marzocchi suspension highlight the alloy Levo 4’s component package.

Hardtail mountain bikes are a gateway into the sport for many riders because they’re simple, sturdy, and affordable. Plus, big, burly full-suspension mountain bikes aren’t for everyone. For some riders, they’re overkill—adding cost and complexity without delivering benefits that match their needs. Hardtail E-MTBs provide pedal assistance for about the same price as full-suspension non-electric mountain bikes.

When launched two years ago, the Ramblas impressed me with its great performance and capability for the price—and Aventon’s recent upgrades make it even better on the trail. The revised Ramblas ADV’s trail-forward components include a RockShox Psylo fork with 35mm stanchions, a 1x12 SRAM Eagle drivetrain that can handle most climbs, powerful four-piston SRAM DB6 brakes, and a useful dropper seatpost. The ADV’s tubeless rims and 2.4-inch Maxxis tires are also upgrades. These components are unmatched by any other hardtail E-MTB, especially at this price.

Superlight e-MTBs offer an intriguing compromise: In exchange for a less powerful drive unit, you get a lighter bike that feels and handles more like an analog mountain bike.

With the Turbo Levo R, Specialized asks: What if you didn’t need to compromise?

The Levo R has the same drive unit and battery as the Levo 4, but in a lighter, shorter-travel package with matched 29-inch wheels. It’s around 5 pounds lighter than a standard Levo 4 at the same price. In the top-of-the-line S-Works trim, the DU offers up to 111 Nm of torque and 850 watts of peak power with an 850 or 900Wh battery. The 41.4-pound (18.8 kg) S-Works Ultralight uses a 600Wh battery to save weight. Lower-priced models offer 105 Nm, 810 watts, and an 840Wh battery.

After spending the better part of a year riding an S-Works Levo R (46.7 lb., size S3), senior test editor Matt Phillips calls it a winning combination for many riders. “It has all the capability and range of a big battery and full-power DU, but in a bike that’s much more flickable, lively, and, frankly, easier to ride than a heavier and bigger-travel e-MTB.” He says Specialized’s e-MTB hardware, frames, and suspension are the icing on top, and calls the Levo R one of the most entertaining mountain bikes, powered or unpowered, he’s ridden this year.

The 29-inch-wheeled Cannondale Moterra 4 has a subdued appearance, which makes it a sleeper. Many full-suspension E-MTBs routinely cost more than $10,000. However, Cannondale keeps the Moterra 4 to a more modest $5,500 while still packing a lot of punch for its price.

At the heart of the Moterra is a trusty Bosch Performance Line CX motor mounted on a 150mm-travel aluminum full-suspension frame. And inside the bike’s stout downtube is a 600Wh Bosch battery. Cannondale’s product team made smart component choices—such as the excellent Shimano Cues drivetrain and 160mm-travel SR Suntour Xeron air-sprung fork—that save cost without sacrificing much performance. The Moterra 4 also has a long-travel dropper seatpost, tubeless wheels, and Tektro disc brakes.

If you buy the Moterra 4 through rei.com, you can have it shipped to your nearest REI location for professional assembly with in-store or curbside pickup. You also get one year of free adjustments—or two years if you’re an REI Co-op member.

“I’ve never considered an e-bike a replacement for a traditional bike—until now.” That’s what Matt Phillips said in his review of the excellent S-Works Turbo Levo 4. The latest version of Specialized’s flagship e-MTB is refined, quiet, fun to ride, and refreshingly free of the common annoyances that plague many e-bikes.

Several standout features set the latest S-Works Turbo Levo 4 apart from the crowded E-MTB segment: a powerful motor delivering 111Nm of torque, a larger 900 Wh battery, and the ability to toggle the pedal-assist cutoff between Class 2 (20mph) and Class 3 (28mph). That extra speed might sound excessive, but it’s a welcome boost when you're riding to or between trails.

Though the S-Works Levo 4 is less expensive than its predecessor, it’s still eye-wateringly pricey at $12,500. For better value—relatively speaking—we recommend the Turbo Levo 4 Expert. It offers the same carbon frame, battery, and motor as the pricier model, though with slightly less torque. A 160mm-travel Fox 38 Performance Elite GRIPX2 fork and a Fox Float X Performance Elite shock handle suspension duties, and SRAM’s GX AXS drivetrain handles gear changes.

Salsa’s Moraine 145 costs less than many hardtail E-MTBs, yet it’s packed with value. This dual 29-inch-wheeled trail bike comes equipped with Fazua’s Ride 60 motor (60 Nm of torque) and a 430Wh battery. While it has less torque and a smaller-capacity battery than higher-priced competitors, the Moraine has enough power for some serious fun in the woods and on singletrack.

The frame uses a Split Pivot suspension system designed by Dave Weagle (the mastermind behind the dw-link suspension system used by Pivot), for a balance of performance and pedalability. A 10-speed Shimano Cues drivetrain with four-piston Shimano brakes, a dropper post, and Terravail tubeless tires round out the component selection on this feature-packed full-suspension E-MTB.

One advantage of an e-MTB is climbing faster and with less effort, leaving you fresher for the descent. A long-travel bike helps you make the most of that on steep, rough trails. It’s like having a personal chairlift for lapping downhill trails.

Cannondale’s Moterra Carbon LT 2 pairs a Bosch Performance Line CX motor and 750Wh battery with 165mm of plush, coil-sprung rear travel and a 170mm fork. Its frame combines a carbon front triangle with an alloy swingarm. Size-specific suspension kinematics tailor the suspension performance to each frame size. The mullet setup pairs a 29-inch front wheel with a 27.5-inch rear, giving you more room to move behind the saddle on steep terrain while keeping the larger front wheel’s rollover advantage. The battery is removable for off-bike charging.

For riders who prioritize steep descents and the refinement and reliability of a Bosch drive unit, the Moterra LT 2 is a strong choice.

It’s no exaggeration to say Avinox reshaped the performance e-MTB market. Developed and launched by drone maker DJI before becoming an independent company, Avinox set new benchmarks with the M1: 105 Nm of torque and up to 1,000 watts of peak power. It also matched or exceeded established brands in features, function, and assist performance. Suddenly, longtime players were off the back, some way off the back.

Before they could catch up, Avinox followed the M1 with the more powerful, refined M2S: up to 150 Nm of torque and 1,500 watts of peak power. But power isn’t the whole story. Senior features director Louis Mazzante says the M2S “isn’t the thunderously loud, chassis-shaking hot-rod motor that its power suggests. It is quiet. It’s smooth. It’s refined.” He also praises its feature-packed, easy-to-use app and touch-screen controls, and says the motor’s power is smooth and tractable.

That blend of power, control, and features has made the M2S highly desirable to bike brands. Ari’s Wire Peak 3.0 stands out as an affordable way to get it.

Starting at $4,999, the Wire Peak pairs the M2S with an 800Wh battery in an aluminum frame with 150mm of rear travel and a 160mm fork. This setup delivers up to 150 Nm of torque in Boost mode and 1,300 watts of peak power. The bike also has a four-bar suspension design, 29-inch or mullet wheel compatibility, and independent high/low and long/short geometry adjustments.

Ari delivers each bike directly to the buyer with a rider-specific setup. Buyers can customize the build through the configurator, and Ari will accommodate most off-menu parts and upgrade requests.

After his first rides on his long-term test bike, senior test editor Matt Phillips called the chassis excellent, Ari’s buying process superb, and the drive unit best in class. He says the Wire Peak would be his choice if he were buying an e-MTB today.

The DRT 1.1 (from REI’s in-house Co-Op Cycles brand) is a good pick if you’re looking for an electric mountain bike to explore mellower trails and ride around town. REI equips the bikes with a dependable Bosch Performance Line mid-drive motor and internal battery.

This hardtail rolls on tubeless-compatible 27.5-inch wheels with 2.6-inch-wide Maxxis Rekon tires for off-road capability. The DRT’s 100mm-travel SR Suntour fork with thru axle isn’t glamorous, but it works. The only notable component missing is a dropper seatpost; it would enhance the bike’s trail capability and help riders feel more confident on challenging terrain.

Traditional analog mountain bikes are usually grouped by frame travel. E-MTBs, on the other hand, are categorized by their power. However, more power ≠ more better. Pick the style that suits your riding needs, budget, and local trail access.

⚡️ Full-Power E-MTBs are the most common style and are generally equipped with roughly 750-watt peak power (1 horsepower) and 85 torque, or more. Bosch, Shimano, and Specialized are three commonly used motor brands for these bikes.

⚡️ Lightweight E-MTBs feature motors with less power and torque (50 to 60 Nm) and lower-capacity batteries. As a result, these bikes weigh less than other E-MTBs and ride more like an unassisted mountain bike, but with a little help on the climbs. Lightweight electric mountain bikes are less popular now than a couple of years ago, which means you can score good deals on great riding bikes.

⚡️ High-power e-MTBs are the newest category of electric mountain bikes, and they’re quickly becoming one of the year’s most talked-about cycling trends. These motors produce more than 1,000 watts of peak power and 110 Nm of torque. Category leader Avinox’s latest unit delivers an incredible 1,500 watts and 150 Nm of torque. Its power and performance have prompted Bosch and Specialized to respond with firmware updates that boost the torque and power of their premium drive units. Even so, the Avinox remains the most powerful drive unit available. But high-power motors aren’t without detractors, or potential consequences. Their use has sparked debate and hand-wringing in the mountain biking community over how much power is too much. Remember: “With great power comes great responsibility.”

When you’re shopping for an electric mountain bike, you must factor in not only the motor’s power and features, but also the bike’s basic capabilities.

Since electronic mountain bikes are heavier than non-motorized bikes, you’ll want to consider a bike with more suspension travel than you have on your non-motorized bike. The longer your travel, the more cushion you’ll get when you encounter obstacles.

Most full suspension E-MTBs are in the 150- to 160mm travel range, which offers a nice balance of increased comfort and improved control for first-time buyers.

There are three electric-assist classes available in the United States, and the use of each class of bike can vary by state, city, and area you are riding. Be sure to check local regulations before buying and before riding on trail systems.

Class 1 motors kick in only when you pedal and stop helping you at 20 mph. Class 2 also offers pedal assist up to 20 mph, but these bikes can also have a throttle-powered mode that allows users to engage the motor without pedaling. Class 3 is pedal-assist like Class 1, but helps you until you reach 28 mph.

Class 1: 20 mph max assist speed; throttle is not standard equipment.

Class 2: 20 mph max assist speed; throttle comes standard equipment.

Class 3: 28 mph max assist speed; throttle is not standard equipment

Other countries classify e-bikes differently, and many countries do not allow e-bikes to assist riders beyond 20 miles per hour.

Most performance-oriented electric mountain bikes are Class 1. Electric mountain bikes generally feature one of two types of motors: mid-drive motors, which are contained in the frame at the bottom of the bike between the pedals; and rear hub motors, which are built into the rear wheel.

Bikes with rear hub motors are generally less expensive but are also less powerful. A mid-drive motor will almost always offer more power. Because the extra weight on a mid-mount bike is centered in the bike and lower to the ground, these bikes are generally easier to handle than bikes with hub motors. Mid-mount bikes do tend to be more expensive, but all of our favorite bikes feature this type of motor.

Battery Life, Range, Maintenance, and Safety

On top of the motor, you’ll want to consider the battery size and range. More is always better for performance, but a battery with a longer range will also weigh more and cost more.

As a bike's weight increases, it requires more effort from the rider to handle. This is especially an important consideration for smaller riders; extra weight can also become a bigger challenge at faster speeds, when you need to ride the bike more aggressively.

A lighter bike will offer better handling, but if you sacrifice too much battery capacity to cut weight, you may find yourself pedaling home with no assist if you take a wrong turn.

Like all e-bikes, E-MTBs can be pedaled as a normal bike if the battery dies, so you’ll still be able to get home, but it will require extra effort and possibly more time.

While we can confidently say a larger battery provides more range, it is difficult to accurately estimate any e-bike's exact range. Many factors impact an e-bike's range: How much boost you’re using (most power systems offer three or four settings), how much you weigh, the terrain you’re riding, how much climbing you’re doing, the air temperature, rolling resistance from the tires, and even how clean and lubricated the drivetrain is.

Don’t take any brand's word on its range claims. Start conservatively as you test your bike’s range. The more you ride, the better you’ll understand how far your batteries can carry you in different conditions. If your bike’s main battery isn’t cutting it, some models let you add an external range booster or swap the internal battery for a fresh one. Be warned: These accessories can be expensive!

Like all batteries, e-bike batteries do lose capacity every time they’re charged and discharged. Unfortunately, that means that the more you ride, the sooner you’ll need to replace your battery. Even so, an e-bike battery should give you years of reliable riding.

Follow your bike's manufacturer’s recommendations for storage, but most batteries will last longer if stored with about a 40-percent charge (but don’t forget to plug your bike in about six hours before riding, to make sure you start your ride with a full charge).

Batteries should also be stored at room temperature whenever possible and not left outside or in an unheated garage in freezing temperatures. Some bike brands, including Specialized, have a setting that will prevent the battery from charging fully. This feature is designed to extend the battery’s life, but you may want to disable the feature if the rides you regularly take use your battery’s full capacity.

If, after a few years, your battery loses enough capacity that you want to replace it, be sure to contact your bike’s manufacturer or local dealer and purchase a certified replacement battery through the brand or its dealer.

Don’t be tempted by cheaper options available online from third-party brands. These third-party batteries are generally not certified by bike brands and may void your bike’s warranty. More importantly, third-party replacement batteries often do not have important safety features, and, as a result, these batteries have been identified as the cause of several serious fires.

There is currently legislation pending at the state and federal level that will bar the sale of non-certified batteries in the U.S., but they are still available as of this writing.

While a handful of bicycle drivetrain manufacturers exist, almost every e-mountain bike will have either a Shimano or SRAM drivetrain. Both are reputable manufacturers that offer reliable shifting and a wide-ranging gear set. Some drivetrains feature electronic shifting. Such drivetrains offer quick and precise shifting, but they will add cost.

The added power of an e-bike puts more strain on a bike’s drivetrain. You can expect to replace your chain more frequently, but you can get more life out of your components if you back off the pedals when shifting, and ease the chain between gears instead of mashing the shifter and the pedal at the same time.

Given the increased weight of an electronic mountain bike, you’ll want to make sure yours is equipped with hydraulic disc brakes, as most are. These brakes offer lots of stopping power and modulation, which ensures you’ll remain in control regardless of your bike's weight, or how fast you go.

Several brands offer hydraulic disc brakes, though again, Shimano and SRAM usually lead the way. For additional power, look for bikes featuring four-piston brakes.
