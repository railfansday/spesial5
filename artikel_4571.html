When You Feel Like an Outsider at Your Own Holiday Gathering

I remember feeling like an outsider as a kid. I didn't speak English and could not communicate with my classmates. I never wanted to go outside to play during recess or lunchtime. While others were excited to get out of the classroom, I just wanted to stay in because those walls were my refuge. The feeling of not belonging still surfaces in various contexts, especially when others share stories, backgrounds, and experiences, ones I cannot relate to. Being the single, childless Latina at family gatherings was tough. In a culture where marriage and kids have been the norm, it is easy for some of us to question our sense of belonging even when we are among family and friends.

With the holiday season and social gatherings upon us, we may feel a mixture of joy and dread. Joy in gathering with those we love, but dread as we anticipate the questions and feelings of being an outsider that may surface. Some of us may feel like outsiders for distinct reasons, including not sharing the same marital status, parenting status, political beliefs, sexual orientation, religious beliefs, and perspectives. We may also feel like outsiders because people may relate to a version of ourselves that no longer exists. You feel like an impostor, trying to contort yourself into someone you are not. Consider the following steps.

“When are you going to get married and have kids?” This question was often on auto-play in the gatherings I attended. Regardless of harmless intent, it made me feel behind in life. Reaching a certain age and not having ticked the right boxes were a cause for concern; not for me, but for those around me. Once I realized that the expectations that other people had were a function of their own beliefs and experiences, which were not mine to adopt, I became less anxious about attending social functions. No journey is the same, and yours is no less valuable because it does not look like the path others have taken.

Kids and marriage are two topics often discussed. People bring up certain topics because they may lack other news, not to make you feel left out. I realized this and offered other topics. If conversations make you uncomfortable, consider redirecting them. Ask about people’s upcoming travel plans, sports, hobbies, projects, celebrations, milestones, or share your own. Research finds that people like to talk about themselves. You can ask about their interests or a story or experience you have in common; this will ease anxiety about other topics. If they discuss something you are not familiar with, ask them to tell you about it.

The narrative running in our heads may say we are outsiders, but it does not mean we are. Whether with friends or family, you have something in common. Reminisce about common themes and share updates about your life if you are comfortable. Every gathering is an opportunity to create new memories and share experiences. Perhaps next year, they will ask about something you shared this year or refer to the experience you are now having. But even if they do not, as President Obama once said, “Do not let people think you do not belong.” You have tremendous value by simply being you. Some contexts will see it, and others may not, but it is not your job to convince them.

If you feel like an outsider even when you are among family and friends, you are not alone. A study of over 200,000 adults across 22 countries found that 14 percent felt like outsiders in their families growing up and about 25 percent, one in four, in the U.S. Ironically, feelings of not belonging and disconnection may be even more pronounced during the holidays, which are marketed as a season of closeness, warmth, and togetherness. Just like filling the gas tank before a road trip, you can emotionally fill your tank before attending gatherings that have left you feeling uneasy in the past.

Draw on the support of the people who accept and know you as you show up, not an older or idealized version of yourself. If you arrive at a gathering and find yourself in conversations that make you question your sense of belonging, try to redirect the conversation toward a less sensitive topic. Assume good intentions. People don't normally mean to make you feel uncomfortable. They may be unaware that questions about your status—marital, biological, or any other type of clock—are not appropriate for discussion. If all else fails, excuse yourself and look for the dessert bar. After all, stressed spelled backward is desserts.
