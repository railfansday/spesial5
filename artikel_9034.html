willow defebaugh on the architecture of wonder and what nature teaches us about design

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

from descartes’ vision of nature as a machine to the principles of biomimicry, the atmos cofounder explores how ecological wisdom can reshape the way we live and design.

The atoms that shiver throughout your being are older than organic life. The elements that bind you together were born in the earliest moments of the universe and forged in faraway stars. The sugars that permit your muscles to move were all made by plants devouring light from a fiery orb. The oxygen that fills your lungs is borrowed from forests and oceans, and the water that makes up most of your body once fell from the sky as drops of rain.

You are constructed of wonder, which is to say nature.

And yet, much of human existence on this planet has been separated from it. The very word we use to describe the sum totality of life is defined by the exclusion of our species. Nature is a learned way of seeing the world as everything that isn’t us. To other anything is the first step to allowing harm, paving the road for extraction and catastrophe. We have bowed out of belonging to the revelry of biological exuberance that is the Earth, and then wonder why we feel lonely.

We can trace the roots of this divide to the Scientific Revolution of 16th- and 17th-century Europe, in which anatomy mapped the body, microscopic life revealed itself, the heavens no longer revolved around us, and gravity was demystified. Alongside these revelations, a new view took shape: nature as a series of mechanical processes, governed by laws that could be observed, understood, and harnessed. And humans, with our uniquely rational minds, were set apart.

Few did more to articulate this perspective than René Descartes. In Discourse on the Method (1637), he described the bodies of animals as machines. He believed that scientific knowledge could allow humans to become “the lords and possessors of nature.” Cartesian dualism was a fissure that cleaved the world in two: mind and matter, thinking subject and physical object. In order to understand the rest of life, we had to observe it from afar.

While Descartes’s cogito ergo sum (“I think, therefore I am”) is widely known, the context is less so. He tied rationalism to doubt: “from the very circumstance that I thought to doubt of the truth of other things, it most clearly and certainly followed that I was.” Doubt, a verb that is so close to wonder, though one houses worry and the other awe. Perhaps rather than doubt leading to thinking to existing, wonder can lead us to feeling to belonging.

Science has taught us extraordinary things about the workings of this world. But to learn how to live within it, we need wisdom.

In the long memory of our planet and the thousands of years in which our species has been living on it, this way of seeing nature as separate from us is a recent phenomenon. The idea that we are separate from nature has never been universal. Many Indigenous cultures have maintained traditions that recognize us as part of the living world, rooted in reciprocity and stewardship. Seeing ourselves as one strand in the web of life is not a new worldview. It’s a very old one.

In Braiding Sweetgrass (2013), botanist and enrolled member of the Citizen Potawatomi Nation Robin Wall Kimmerer advocates for a weaving of Indigenous knowledge and Western science. Through stories about plants that plait the personal, cultural, and biological, she illuminates a world in which every growing thing is a guide beckoning us into deeper relationship with the Earth and all of the beings it houses. This is the fertile ground of ecological wisdom.

If knowledge is a collection of facts, wisdom is what we do with it. While science helps us gather knowledge in order to better understand the world we live in, sapience—the possession of wisdom—helps us discern how to move through it. For example, scientific knowledge can reveal that forests exchange nutrients through fungal networks. Ecological wisdom asks what those relationships might teach us about reciprocity, cooperation, and collective well-being.

It’s telling that, while many of us were raised on the importance of science and studied it in school, few of us are taught sapience. Shouldn’t we let everything we have learned about life on Earth guide us in our individual and collective lives? Climate change, biodiversity loss, and ecocide are the evidence of this disconnect, and the wider chasm between us and nature.

This is core to our work at Atmos, a multimedia ecosystem weaving nature into public consciousness. Our mission is to inspire cultural transformation by re-enchanting people with nature. We reveal the ways that human life is interwoven with the Earth, and believe that this connection leads to the solutions that preserve and protect our planet. Our vision is a society in sync with nature’s efficiencies, reciprocities, and beauty. What we offer is proof of wonder.

I have spent the last six years gathering this in the form of a newsletter called The Overview. Rather than liquify in a chrysalis, dragonflies metamorphose incompletely across many moltings. Can we adopt their patience for transformation? Long before humans invented agriculture, ants were farming fungi for food in a way that benefited both. What if our modern agricultural systems could be similarly symbiotic? Hummingbirds can hover, drawing nectar from flowers without ever needing to land on them. Can we learn to live as lightly, leaving so little trace?

In this time, it has become a continuous practice for me to turn to nature for wisdom about how to live. In moving through grief, I turned to ravens and crows, who have been seen holding funerals for the fallen. When struggling with vulnerability, I looked to frogs, who partly breathe through their sensitive skin. Seeking advice on how to navigate the unknown, I found comfort in bats, who make maps of the dark by listening.

In every life form, crawling or winged, scaled or soaring, there are lessons to be found.

At the scale of systems, one of the most direct ways ecological wisdom can guide the way we design our lives is through biomimicry. As Janine Benyus, who popularized the term with her 1997 book, once told me: Every species that is alive today is an example of life having gotten it right—and elegantly so. In our conversation for The Nature Of podcast, she pointed out that our species has a massive design challenge on our hands, but life’s intelligence is all around us.

Bullet trains that take cues from kingfishers, structural color that mirrors the brilliant blue of morpho butterflies rather than synthetic dyes, shark skin-inspired antimicrobial surfaces, temperature regulation modeled after termite mounds—biomimicry provides the blueprints for a designed society that is not separate from, but functionally intertwined with the wonders of the more than human world. It sees every species as a schematic written with possibilities.

Biomimicry is more than taking inspiration from nature or borrowing its aesthetics: it’s about emulating nature’s artistry in both form and function, in a way that gives back to life. As Benyus told me: “Life creates conditions conducive to life.” And we are part of that creative genius. We can design a society that works with nature as we simultaneously remember our place within it.

In World as Lover, World as Self (1991), the late Buddhist and deep ecologist Joanna Macy describes three movements in the story of our species. The first she labeled participation mystique, the state in which one does not distinguish oneself from surrounding objects and the environment. The second was when we left that state and began to see the world as separate, which gave us science but also alienation. The third movement is the one we must now make: back to an awareness of ourselves as part of nature, bringing with us all we have learned.

As Macy put it, “Now it can dawn on us: we are our world knowing itself.”

The root of “eco” is the Greek oikos meaning “home.” We can return to a way of living with this understanding as the foundation already laid, one that reconnects us to the resplendent, abundant aliveness in the garden of life. Our species is standing at the threshold, and ecological wisdom is the key. Outside the house of isolation that we have constructed, home awaits: a circle of belonging as wide as the world. And the architecture of the world is wonder.

Willow Defebaugh is the cofounder and editor-in-chief of Atmos, a climate and culture publication exploring humanity’s relationship with the natural world. Defebaugh is also the author of The Overview, a deep ecology newsletter and book, and the host of The Nature Of podcast.
