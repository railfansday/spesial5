America’s rental market just hit a surprising turning point. Here’s what it means for your budget

Tanggal: 2026-11-19

Depending on where you live, this might seem unbelievable, but rents have actually come down in many places over the past year.

Across the 50 largest U.S. metro areas, the median asking rent increased only $1 from last year—to $1,692 in October—for studio, one-bedroom, and two-bedroom properties, according to a new report from Realtor.com. This marks the first annual increase in median rents in more than three years, even as 26 major metro areas have seen rents fall over the past year, according to data from the Austin-based real estate site.

Of course, location matters. The story of today’s rent prices depends largely on how robust the construction boom was earlier this decade—and how many projects are in the pipeline. That’s why many renters may not feel like they’re seeing much relief.

“Renters still face a very different affordability picture than they did before the pandemic,” Jiayi Xu, senior economist at Realtor.com, said in a statement. “Elevated borrowing costs may keep more households renting, just as new deliveries slow. That makes the local construction pipeline increasingly important to the outlook for rent relief.”

WHERE RENTS ARE FALLING

Amid a broader affordability crisis, rents have been somewhat contained in recent years. Median asking rents nationwide fell for three straight years, thanks largely to a boom in multifamily construction projects between 2021 and 2022 that added supply to the rental market.

Nationwide, it’s been a good run for people who don’t mind small living, as the median rent for a studio fell 1.2% in the past year, to $1,411 as of October, according to Realtor.com. That marked the 39th consecutive month of annual declines in studio rents.

Renters in the South have experienced the most rent relief. The metro areas that saw the biggest declines in median rent in the past year were:

  • San Antonio, Texas: Down 3.7% to $1,117
  • Tampa, Florida: Down 2.3% to $1,604
  • Nashville, Tennessee: Down 2.1% to $1,504
  • Houston, Texas: Down 1.9% to $1,377
  • Detroit, Michigan: Down 1.8% to $1,146
  • Sacramento, California: Down 1.8% to $1,794

Though rents edged up in Oklahoma City, this metro area holds the distinction for being the only one in the latest report where the median rent is below $1,000, at $956.

WHERE RENTS ARE RISING

As with the broader housing market, the current rental outlook is a tale of four regions. Yet renters nationwide may need to brace for potential rent increases ahead.

The Northeast has recently entered a rebuilding phase of the construction cycle, though rents could continue to rise given the lag before those projects are delivered. Meanwhile, the Midwest still saw rent increases over the past year despite having the largest pipeline of projects relative to pre-pandemic levels. Even in the South and West, where rent prices have been coming down in recent years, relief may be fading. Price declines are narrowing in several Southern metro areas, and weaker construction activity in the West could soon limit further drops.

Renters who want more space—whether to stretch their legs or accommodate familial needs—are seeing the biggest increases in monthly rent. For the first time in more than three years, the median rent for a two-bedroom property increased on an annual basis, up 0.3% to $1,889 nationwide. These renters have seen prices surge by nearly 19% over the past seven years, compared with a 15% median increase across all unit sizes during that same period.

Renters in the Midwest and California’s greater Bay area are getting squeezed most. The metro areas that saw the biggest increases in median rent in the past year were:

  • San Francisco, California: Up 6.3% to $3,094
  • San Jose, California: Up 6.2% to $3,540
  • Milwaukee, Wisconsin: Up 4.1% to $1,767
  • St. Louis, Missouri: Up 3.2% to $1,298
  • Chicago, Illinois: Up 3.2% to $1,907
