What does 007 mean? The 00 license to kill, explained

Tanggal: 2026-11-17

Here's what 007 means, how James Bond's double-0 code number and fictional license to kill work in Ian Fleming's novels and films, and how real U.K. law compares

The number 007 is a job code, not a name. It is James Bond's service number. It tells the reader which section Bond belongs to and what that section authorizes him to do. That is why the question "what does 007 mean" has a precise answer, and that answer starts in the original novels.

Bond is the fictional MI6 officer created by the British novelist Ian Fleming. MI6 is the common name for the Secret Intelligence Service, the U.K.'s foreign intelligence agency. Fleming introduced his agent in the novel Casino Royale, published in 1953. Eon Productions released its first Bond film, Dr. No, in 1962, with Sean Connery in the lead role. The code number moved with the character from page to screen.

The number has two parts. The double zero marks a special section, and the final digit identifies one officer within it. In Fleming's fiction, the prefix carries an authority that ordinary officers do not hold. It is the authority to kill in the line of duty, known in shorthand as the license to kill. Both the section and the license are inventions of the stories, not features of the real MI6.

This guide works through the code one piece at a time. It covers how the prefix is read, how Bond earned it in the original novel and how the 2006 film of the same name retold that moment. It looks at the other double-0 agents who appear on screen and at the film that handed the 007 number to someone new. It also explains the real U.K. law that comes closest to a license to kill, and why that law is something else entirely. The final slide turns to Fleming's wartime work in British naval intelligence and the 1989 film that borrowed the phrase for its title.

Each slide stands on its own, so readers can start with whichever part of the 007 question they need.

007 is James Bond's service number, the code that identifies him inside MI6 in Ian Fleming's spy novels. The double zero places him in a special section, and the closing seven singles him out as one agent within it.

The code works like an address. The first two characters point to the unit. The last character points to one officer inside that unit.

That makes 007 a designation rather than a name. It describes a position within an organization, and Bond is the man who fills it. Both the officer and his section exist only in fiction, not in the real MI6.

The number arrived on the page with the character in Casino Royale, Fleming's first Bond novel. Dr. No, the first Eon Productions film, later carried both to cinemas with Sean Connery in the role.

Fleming never gave a documented reason for choosing seven. No statement from the author explains why Bond received that digit rather than another. Theories about its origin circulate, but none rests on anything Fleming verifiably said or wrote.

The digit's job inside the stories is clearer than its origin. It tells one double-0 officer apart from the others in the same section. Nothing in the code itself says more than that.

In Ian Fleming's James Bond novels, the double-0 prefix marks an officer of the double-0 section. Officers in that section are authorized to kill in the line of duty. "License to kill" is the shorthand for that authority.

The prefix and the license work as a pair. The double-0 number is the marker. The authority to kill is what that marker signals.

The phrase describes a power rather than a formal rank or job title. It compresses what the prefix permits into three words.

The authority is tied to the line of duty. In the fiction, it covers what a double-0 officer does on assignment for the service.

Fleming introduced the idea in Casino Royale, the novel that launched the series. In that book, Bond reflects on his double-0 status and on what the authority to kill means for him. The license enters the series in the same book as the character himself.

The double-0 section carries over into the Eon Productions films. On screen, as in the novels, the license works as a plot device.

Both the section and its powers are story devices. Fleming built them into his invented version of MI6. They exist only on the page and on screen.

James Bond earns his double-0 prefix in Ian Fleming's 1953 novel Casino Royale by killing two people on assignment. The 2006 film of the same name tells that origin story differently, with new targets and new circumstances.

Bond does not start out as a double-0 officer. Casino Royale treats the prefix as a status that must be earned, and the novel names two specific kills as the qualification.

The first kill takes place in New York. Bond's target there is a Japanese cipher expert, a specialist in codes.

The second takes place in Stockholm, the Swedish capital. That target is a Norwegian double agent, someone who appears to serve one side while secretly working for another.

The two kills span two countries, the U.S. and Sweden. Both targets come from the intelligence trade itself. One deals in codes, and the other in divided loyalty. Neither is a soldier or a common criminal. Bond's qualifying work is aimed at the people who make up the spy business.

The 2006 film, starring Daniel Craig, reworks this backstory. It shows Bond's promotion in a black-and-white opening sequence. The monochrome sets those scenes apart from the color footage that follows. It marks them as the step that comes before the rest of the story.

The sequence shows Bond killing two men. One is Dryden, a section chief, whom Bond confronts in Prague. The other is Fisher, Dryden's contact. Both men belong to the spy world, as the novel's targets do. These two deaths replace the novel's kills and earn the screen Bond his double-0 number.

The film keeps the novel's count of two kills but changes the people involved. In the book, the targets are a cipher expert and a double agent. In the film, they are a section chief and his contact. The shape of the origin survives the adaptation, while its details belong to each version alone.

James Bond is not the only officer with a double-0 number in the Eon Productions films. Three others appear on screen: 006 in GoldenEye (1995), 009 in Octopussy (1983) and 004 in The Living Daylights (1987).

All three carry the same double zero as Bond. Only the last digit changes from one officer to the next. That detail shows that 007 is one numbered post among several. It is not a label reserved for one man.

The 006 designation belongs to Alec Trevelyan. Sean Bean plays him in GoldenEye. Here the number comes with a full character name and a named actor. That ties the 006 post to a specific person in the story rather than leaving it as a digit.

Agent 009 appears in the opening of Octopussy. He is already dead when the audience first sees him. He is also disguised as a clown.

Agent 004 appears in the opening of The Living Daylights. He is killed during a training exercise.

The two films use these officers in a similar way. In each, the other double-0 agent appears in the opening sequence rather than later in the story. Neither survives that sequence. The audience meets him only briefly, and his number matters more than his character.

Taken together, the three films sketch the double-0 section as a working unit with several members. On screen, Bond is one numbered officer within that group, and his place in it is 007.

In No Time to Die, the 2021 Bond film, the 007 designation belongs to someone other than James Bond. Bond has left the service, and an officer named Nomi, played by Lashana Lynch, carries the number.

The story turns on that change in Bond's status. He is no longer working for MI6, the Secret Intelligence Service, when the events of the film take place. His exit leaves the code number open, and another officer holds it.

The detail shows how the number works inside the fiction. The 007 code is tied to a post in the double-0 section, not to one particular man. When an officer leaves, the post remains, and so does its number.

The film links two characters to the same three digits. Bond is the character audiences have connected with the number since Ian Fleming's novels. Nomi is the officer who holds it while the film's events unfold.

That arrangement treats 007 as a working designation that MI6 assigns. It is not a personal name that retires when one officer walks away.

The license to kill is not real. MI6 has no double-0 section, and none of its officers holds a standing license to kill. Both exist only in Ian Fleming's novels and the films adapted from them.

MI6 is the Secret Intelligence Service. In the stories, it employs James Bond under the number 007. In reality, it has no numbered section of officers authorized to kill.

The closest real-world comparison is a piece of legislation, not a code number. Section 7 of the U.K. Intelligence Services Act 1994 lets the Secretary of State authorize certain acts outside the British Isles. These are acts that would otherwise break U.K. law.

The purpose of that authorization is protection at home. Staff who carry out an authorized act abroad are shielded from liability in the U.K. for that act.

Section 7 is sometimes nicknamed the "James Bond clause." The nickname borrows from the fiction. It does not describe what the law permits.

Section 7 is not a license to kill. It gives no officer a personal, standing authority to take a life. The power to authorize sits with the Secretary of State, not with any individual officer.

The difference from the fiction lies in where the authority comes from. In Fleming's stories, the double-0 prefix itself marks an officer authorized to kill in the line of duty. The authority travels with the officer's number.

Under the 1994 act, nothing travels with a number. The provision addresses whether staff face liability under U.K. law for an authorized act abroad. It does not create a class of officers set apart for lethal work.

For readers asking whether the license to kill is real, the answer has two parts. The double-0 license is an invention of the stories. The nearest real law works as a legal shield, not a grant of lethal power.

Ian Fleming, the novelist who created James Bond, worked in British naval intelligence during World War II. The phrase most closely tied to Bond's double-0 status later became the title of a 1989 Bond film, Licence to Kill.

Fleming's wartime post was real intelligence work. His spy fiction came afterward. Bond did not appear in print until several years after the war ended.

The order of events separates fact from invention. Fleming's naval service is part of the historical record. The double-0 section he later wrote about is not. Its authority to kill exists only in the novels and the films adapted from them.

The name of his hero came from a different field altogether. Fleming borrowed the name James Bond from an American ornithologist. That James Bond wrote a field guide to the birds of the Caribbean. The fictional spy and the real bird expert share a name and nothing else.

Fleming wrote the Bond novels at Goldeneye, his home in Jamaica. The house shares its name with the 1995 Bond film GoldenEye. The film's title capitalizes the second E. The Caribbean runs through both details. The ornithologist's field guide covered the region's birds, and the novels were written on one of its islands.

Licence to Kill starred Timothy Dalton as Bond. It was the 16th Bond film made by Eon Productions, the production company behind the main Bond film series.

The title keeps the British spelling, licence. In U.S. English, the same word is spelled license. Both spellings refer to the same authority attached to the double-0 prefix.

The story turns the phrase around. In Licence to Kill, Bond acts without official sanction. The title names an authority the service grants, and the plot follows Bond working outside it. A film named for the license shows its hero without the official backing that gives the double-0 number its meaning in the stories.
