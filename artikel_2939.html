​The AI Investment Paradox: Is Sustainable Value Being Built Or Are Technology Costs Just Expanding?

Tanggal: 2026-11-24

Jayasri Ranganathan is VP, Head of Technology Strategy at Trinity Solar | Enterprise AI, Governance & Technology Transformation.

An AI solution can reduce processing time, improve productivity and perform exactly as designed yet still fail to deliver a meaningful financial return. This is one of the biggest misunderstandings in enterprise AI investment. Organizations often measure what AI improves within a task without establishing whether that improvement changes the economics of the business.

According to Gartner’s October 2026 CIO Survey, only 13% of CIOs reported significant or transformational AI value, while 51% expected AI to increase technology’s total cost of ownership. The disconnect is not necessarily a failure of the technology. It is often a failure to connect technical improvements to business outcomes while accounting for the full cost of achieving them.

When evaluating AI investments, I start with a few questions: What business problem are we solving? How will it affect revenue, operating costs, EBITDA, compliance or risk? What will we measure to determine whether the investment delivered?​

Consider an AI solution that classifies documents and reduces manual processing time. The efficiency improvement may be measurable, but the financial return depends on what happens next. API consumption increases with processing volume. Exceptions still require human intervention. Integrations need maintenance and performance must be monitored.

My experience evaluating AI-enabled processes has reinforced the importance of understanding these production economics. A solution that performs well during initial testing may have a different cost profile at scale.

There is also the question of what happens to the time saved. Does the organization reduce operating expenses, handle additional volume without increasing headcount or improve service quality?​ If the organization reports hours saved without measuring the operating costs or how that capacity is used, it may be overstating the return.

AI can improve one part of a workflow while leaving the larger business outcome unchanged. Take a company using AI to identify qualified leads and prioritize sales opportunities. The technology may increase sales activity, but if downstream fulfillment remains constrained or payment cycles remain unchanged, the improvement may not translate into stronger margins or cash flow.​​

AI can generate responses quickly, but the organization remains responsible for their accuracy. Where information requires validation or errors carry significant consequences, human oversight may still be necessary. That oversight introduces costs. Removing it can introduce risks.

The appropriate level of automation depends on data reliability, business impact and the organization’s ability to govern the process. Those considerations should be part of the financial evaluation before deployment, not addressed after the solution is operational. For me, the question is not simply whether AI can complete a task faster. It is whether the entire process performs better after accounting for cost, quality, risk and the business outcome.

These process-level outcomes must ultimately be evaluated against the full cost of deploying, operating and governing the AI solution.

Technology leaders must evaluate AI alongside cybersecurity, infrastructure resilience, ERP modernization and other enterprise priorities. An AI assistant may improve customer service productivity. But if customer information is fragmented across systems, investing in data integration may produce greater immediate value and improve the economics of future AI initiatives.

This is a trade-off I consider when evaluating competing technology investments: Is AI the highest-value use of available resources right now? The calculation must include more than licensing and implementation. Data readiness, integration, API consumption, monitoring, cybersecurity, human validation and ongoing maintenance all affect total cost of ownership.

Information risk also matters. AI systems processing personal or confidential information can create regulatory, contractual and reputational exposure if safeguards are inadequate.

Broader infrastructure pressures add further uncertainty. Gartner has predictied that worldwide data-center electricity consumption would increase 26% in 2026, while a subsequent projection in October warned that more than $3.5 trillion in AI infrastructure spending could be exposed to impairment by 2030. Cloud pricing, computing availability and supplier economics all influence the long-term affordability of AI investment.

These costs and dependencies are connected to the central investment question. An efficiency improvement creates business value only when its benefits justify the resources and risks required to sustain it.

Organizations should establish expected AI value before approving an investment. In my experience, these four decisions are particularly important:

1. Establish The Business Baseline: Understand current processing costs, cycle times, error rates, revenue performance or other relevant measures. Without a baseline, improvements are difficult to quantify.

2. Define The Business Outcome And Owner: Decide whether the investment is intended to reduce expenses, increase capacity, improve revenue, strengthen compliance or reduce risk. Business leaders must be accountable for realizing the expected outcome, not just IT for delivering the technology.

3. Evaluate The Economics At Scale: Model recurring consumption costs, exception handling, human oversight and maintenance. Measure cost per completed transaction, not simply the number of tasks automated.

4. Agree On When To Scale, Redesign Or Stop: Establish performance and financial thresholds before implementation. Review outcomes at defined intervals and be prepared to redirect investment if the expected value does not materialize.

Business, finance, technology and risk leaders must work together on these decisions throughout the investment lifecycle, not just at launch. AI innovation requires experimentation, but experimentation should not become an indefinite commitment to spending.

AI will continue to create opportunities for organizations to work differently. But deploying more technology is not the same as creating more enterprise value. A technically successful implementation can still be an unsuccessful investment if operating costs rise faster than the benefits or productivity improvements never translate into business results. The CIO’s responsibility is to make those distinctions visible before they become expensive.

As AI investment grows, the most important leadership question will be whether we can demonstrate how the technology improves the economics of the business, not simply how much work it automates. And when the numbers do not support scaling, are we prepared to make the harder decision to wait?​
